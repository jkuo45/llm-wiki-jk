# Strongyloidiasis

**Strongyloidiasis** is a parasitic disease caused by the threadworm **Strongyloides stercoralis**. A defining feature is **autoinfection** — the parasite's ability to complete part of its life cycle within the same host — which allows chronic, often asymptomatic infection to persist for decades and, under immunosuppression, to explode into disseminated, frequently fatal hyperinfection.

## Etiology and Pathophysiology

After skin penetration by infective L3 larvae, *S. stercoralis* migrates through the lungs and is swallowed, maturing into parthenogenetic females in the duodenum and jejunum. Eggs hatch in the mucosa, releasing L1 larvae that are excreted in stool. A fraction mature into autoinfective L3 within the gut or perianal skin and re-penetrate, sustaining infection without exogenous re-exposure. In immunocompetent hosts, Th2 immunity ([[Eosinophils]], IgE, IL-5) keeps larval burden low. Corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive cancer therapies suppress this control, permitting uncontrolled larval proliferation, pulmonary/alimentary invasion, and bacterial translocation via the disrupted mucosa.

## Clinical Presentation

Chronic infection may be silent or cause intermittent abdominal pain, diarrhea, urticaria, and a characteristic larva currens (rapidly migrating cutaneous eruption). **Hyperinfection syndrome** presents with severe enterocolitis, paralytic ileus, diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, and gram-negative sepsis; mortality is high. Risk groups include patients on long-term steroids, those receiving [[Chemotherapy]], hematopoietic stem-cell transplant, or checkpoint-inhibitor immunotherapy (e.g., anti-[[CTLA-4]], anti-PD-1) for [[Cancer]].

## Diagnosis and Biomarkers

Stool microscopy has poor sensitivity due to intermittent larval output; serology (detecting anti-strongyloides IgG) and molecular stool/duodenal assays improve detection. Eosinophilia supports the diagnosis but may be absent in hyperinfection. Screening endemic-area patients before immunosuppression is standard of care.

## Therapeutic Landscape

[[Ivermectin]] is the treatment of choice (superior to [[Albendazole]]) for both acute and prophylactic use; repeated courses may be needed. In disseminated disease, subcutaneous/IV ivermectin plus broad-spectrum antibiotics for bacterial sepsis are employed. Eradication must be documented serologically and parasitologically.
