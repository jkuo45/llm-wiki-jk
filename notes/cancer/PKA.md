# PKA

Protein kinase A (PKA), also known as cAMP-dependent protein kinase, is a ubiquitous serine/threonine kinase that translates signals from G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) via the second messenger cyclic AMP (cAMP) into phosphorylation of downstream targets. PKA regulates numerous cellular processes, including metabolism, ion channel activity, gene transcription (through CREB), and cell survival/death decisions, making it a central node in [[Signal Transduction]].

## Structure & Mechanism

The inactive holoenzyme is a tetramer of two regulatory (R) and two catalytic (C) subunits. Binding of four cAMP molecules to the R subunits releases the active C subunits, which then phosphorylate substrates at canonical RRxS/T motifs. The catalytic subunit adopts the canonical bilobed kinase fold with an activation loop that is constitutively primed. Downstream, PKA phosphorylates substrates such as [[CREB]], [[Bad]], [[Caspase-9]], and [[Bim]], linking hormonal and neurotransmitter signals to survival outcomes.

## Physiological & Pathological Function

PKA promotes cell survival in many contexts by phosphorylating pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members and apoptotic executioners, thereby dampening [[Apoptosis]]. Specifically, PKA phosphorylates [[Caspase-9]] and [[Bim]], modulating their stability and activity to suppress the intrinsic apoptotic program. This anti-apoptotic tone can protect normal cells during stress but also contributes to the survival of [[Cancer]] cells, where elevated cAMP/PKA signaling downstream of GPCRs can support the [[Hallmarks of Cancer]], including resistance to cell death. Dysregulation of the pathway is implicated in endocrine tumors, leukemias, and other malignancies.

## Clinical & Research Relevance

PKA signaling is targeted indirectly by drugs that modulate cAMP (e.g., phosphodiesterase inhibitors) and by GPCR-directed therapies. Understanding PKA's anti-apoptotic phosphorylation of [[Caspase-9]] and [[Bim]] informs strategies to sensitize tumors to [[Chemotherapy]] and targeted agents. The kinase remains a key research tool, with its regulatory subunit dimer widely used as an affinity reagent for cAMP pull-down assays.
