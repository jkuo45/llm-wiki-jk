# Bladder Cancer

Bladder cancer is a malignant neoplasm arising from the urothelium (transitional epithelium) lining the urinary bladder. It is the 10th most common cancer globally, with approximately 550,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths per year. Incidence varies geographically, with the highest rates in Southern and Western Europe, North America, and Egypt (the latter driven by endemic [[Schistosoma haematobium]] infection). In men, it is the 4th most common malignancy after [[Prostate Cancer|prostate]], [[Lung Cancer|lung]], and [[Colon Cancer|colorectal cancer]]. The median age at diagnosis is 73 years, and the lifetime risk is approximately 1 in 27 for men and 1 in 89 for women.

## Risk Factors

**Cigarette smoking** is the single most important risk factor, accounting for approximately 50% of all bladder cancer cases. Smokers have a 2–4 fold increased risk compared to never-smokers, and the risk persists for decades after cessation. Tobacco-derived [[carcinogens]] such as 4-aminobiphenyl and 2-naphthylamine are concentrated in urine and directly expose the urothelium.

Occupational exposures contribute to an estimated 10–20% of cases. High-risk industries include rubber manufacturing, dye and textile processing, leather tanning, and aluminum smelting. The primary causative agents are **aromatic amines** (benzidine, 2-naphthylamine, 4-aminobiphenyl) and **aniline dyes**. The latency period between exposure and diagnosis often exceeds 20–30 years.

**Chronic inflammation** of the bladder mucosa is an established risk factor. In Egypt and parts of Africa, chronic infection with [[Schistosoma haematobium]] (schistosomiasis) is the dominant cause, strongly associated with [[Squamous Cell Carcinoma|squamous cell carcinoma]] of the bladder. Other inflammatory triggers include long-term indwelling urinary catheters, recurrent urinary tract infections, and bladder stones. **Cyclophosphamide** and **ifosfamide** (alkylating chemotherapeutic agents) increase risk through acrolein-mediated urothelial damage, with a cumulative dose–response relationship. Prior pelvic [[Radiation Therapy|radiation]] also raises risk.

Additional risk factors include [[Arsenic]] contamination of drinking water (endemic in areas of South America and Asia), family history (2-fold increased risk in first-degree relatives), and [[Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colorectal Cancer]] (Lynch syndrome), which confers an early-age onset phenotype.

## Histologic Types

Approximately 90% of bladder cancers are **urothelial carcinoma** (also called transitional cell carcinoma), arising from the urothelium. These tumors can be papillary (exophytic fronds, low-grade) or non-papillary (solid, flat, high-grade). Urothelial carcinoma may also exhibit divergent differentiation with squamous, glandular, or sarcomatoid features.

**Squamous cell carcinoma** accounts for 5–10% of cases in Western countries but up to 75% in regions endemic for [[Schistosoma haematobium]]. It arises from metaplastic squamous epithelium in the setting of chronic inflammation. Pure **adenocarcinoma** (~2%) may be of urachal origin (arising from the urachal remnant) or non-urachal. **Small cell carcinoma** of the bladder is rare (<1%) but highly aggressive, with neuroendocrine features and a strong tendency for early metastasis. Other rare subtypes include sarcomatoid carcinoma, micropapillary variant, plasmacytoid, and nested variant — all associated with more aggressive behavior.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

**Painless gross hematuria** is the presenting symptom in approximately 80% of patients and is the hallmark clinical feature. Hematuria may be intermittent, leading to diagnostic delay. Irritative voiding symptoms — [[frequency]], [[urgency]], and [[dysuria]] — occur in about 20% of patients, particularly those with carcinoma in situ (CIS). Advanced disease may present with flank pain (ureteral obstruction), pelvic pain, lower extremity edema, or palpable suprapubic mass.

The diagnostic evaluation begins with **cystoscopy** and **transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT)**, which provides tissue for histologic diagnosis and initial staging. **Urine cytology** has high sensitivity for high-grade lesions and CIS but limited sensitivity for low-grade tumors. Adjunctive urinary biomarkers (UroVysion FISH, NMP22, ImmunoCyt) are used in select settings but have not replaced cystoscopy. **CT urography** (multiphasic CT with excretory phase) is essential to evaluate the upper urinary tract (renal pelvis and ureters) for synchronous tumors, which occur in 2–5% of cases. [[MRI]] is an emerging alternative for staging, particularly for detecting extravesical extension.

## Staging and Classification

Bladder cancer is dichotomized into two clinically distinct groups based on depth of invasion:

**Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC)** accounts for approximately 75% of new diagnoses and includes stages Ta (confined to urothelium), T1 (invades lamina propria, not muscularis propria), and CIS (flat, high-grade, non-invasive). NMIBC is managed conservatively but has a high recurrence rate (50–70%) and a 10–20% risk of progression to muscle invasion within 5 years.

**Muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC)** comprises stage T2 (invades detrusor muscle), T3 (perivesical fat invasion), and T4 (invades adjacent organs — prostate, uterus, vagina, pelvic or abdominal wall). Approximately 25% of patients present with or develop MIBC, which carries a significantly worse prognosis. Nodal involvement (N+) and distant metastasis (M1) are staged via [[CT]], [[PET|PET/CT]], or chest imaging.

## Treatment

### NMIBC

The cornerstone of NMIBC management is complete **TURBT**, followed by risk-stratified intravesical therapy. A single postoperative instillation of **mitomycin C** reduces recurrence in low-risk tumors. For intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC, the standard of care is induction and maintenance intravesical [[BCG]] (Bacillus Calmette–Guérin) immunotherapy, which reduces recurrence and progression. Patients with BCG-unresponsive disease may receive intravesical **valrubicin**, **gemcitabine**, **docetaxel**, or **nadofaragene firadenovec** (gene therapy). Lifelong cystoscopic surveillance is mandatory given the high recurrence rate.

### MIBC

The standard of care for localized MIBC (cT2–T4a, N0) is **neoadjuvant cisplatin-based combination chemotherapy** (typically gemcitabine + cisplatin or MVAC [methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, cisplatin]) followed by **radical cystectomy** with pelvic lymph node dissection and **urinary diversion** (ileal conduit, neobladder, or continent cutaneous reservoir). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy provides a 5–8% absolute survival benefit at 5 years. Patients who are unfit for or decline surgery may be treated with **definitive chemoradiation** (trimodality therapy: TURBT + chemoradiation).

### Metastatic Disease

First-line treatment for metastatic bladder cancer is **platinum-based chemotherapy** (gemcitabine + cisplatin or carboplatin in cisplatin-ineligible patients). The landscape has been transformed by **immune checkpoint inhibitors** targeting [[PD-L1|PD-1/PD-L1]]: [[pembrolizumab]] and [[atezolizumab]] are approved as second-line therapy after platinum failure, and as first-line in cisplatin-ineligible patients with PD-L1-positive tumors. [[FGFR3]] inhibitors — **erdafitinib** — are approved for metastatic disease harboring activating FGFR3 mutations or fusions (present in 15–20% of MIBC). The [[Antibody-Drug Conjugate|antibody-drug conjugate]] **enfortumab vedotin** (targeting Nectin-4) has shown significant activity in previously treated metastatic disease. **Sacituzumab govitecan** (Trop-2-directed ADC) and **disitamab vedotin** (HER2-directed ADC) represent additional emerging options.

### Prognosis

Five-year survival for NMIBC exceeds 85% with appropriate surveillance and treatment. For MIBC treated with cystectomy, 5-year survival is approximately 50% for pT2, 30% for pT3, and 10% for pT4. Metastatic disease has a median overall survival of 12–18 months with modern systemic therapy.
