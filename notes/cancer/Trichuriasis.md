# Trichuriasis

Trichuriasis (whipworm infection) is a soil-transmitted helminth ([[STH]]) infection caused by the nematode [[Trichuris]] trichiura. An estimated 600–800 million people are infected worldwide, with the highest prevalence in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation, particularly sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and Central/South America.

## Biology

Adult *T. trichiura* worms measure approximately 30–50 mm in length and exhibit a distinctive whip-like morphology — a thin, thread-like anterior end that embeds into the colonic mucosa and a thicker, robust posterior end that remains free in the lumen. The anterior portion contains the esophagus and penetrates the epithelial layer, while the posterior segment houses the reproductive organs. Females produce characteristic barrel-shaped eggs with prominent bipolar mucoid plugs (mucoid prominences at each pole), which are shed into the environment through feces.

## Life Cycle

The life cycle is direct and monoxenous (no intermediate host). Infective embryonated eggs containing first-stage (L1) larvae are ingested from contaminated soil, food, or water. Larvae hatch in the [[Small Intestine|small intestine]], penetrate the villi, and undergo molting. Over approximately one month, developing larvae migrate to the [[Cecum|cecum]] and proximal [[Colon|colon]], where adult worms establish with their anterior ends embedded in the mucosal epithelium. Adult females begin egg production 60–70 days post-infection, releasing 3,000–20,000 eggs per day into the intestinal lumen to pass with stool. Eggs embryonate in soil over 2–4 weeks under warm, moist conditions to become infective, completing the cycle.

## Clinical Presentation

Clinical manifestations depend on worm burden. **Light infections** (fewer than 100 worms) are typically asymptomatic. **Moderate infections** cause abdominal pain, diarrhea, tenesmus (painful straining), and dysentery. **Heavy infections**, most common in children, produce the classic *Trichuris dysentery syndrome*: chronic bloody mucus diarrhea with tenesmus, growth stunting, anemia, clubbing, and — in severe cases — [[Rectal Prolapse|rectal prolapse]] due to chronic straining and inflammation. Chronic infection in children is also associated with impaired cognitive development and nutritional deficits.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on microscopic identification of the characteristic barrel-shaped eggs with bipolar plugs in stool specimens. The [[Kato-Katz]] thick-smear technique enables quantitative egg counts to assess infection intensity (light: 1–999 eggs/g; moderate: 1,000–9,999 eggs/g; heavy: ≥10,000 eggs/g). [[Colonoscopy]] in symptomatic patients may reveal adult worms with their anterior ends embedded in the colonic mucosa, often described as a "cobblestone" or "nodular" appearance. Molecular diagnostics (PCR) are available but primarily used in research settings.

## Treatment

First-line treatment includes [[Albendazole]] (400 mg daily for 3 days) or [[Mebendazole]] (100 mg twice daily for 3 days), both of which have moderate efficacy. For heavy infections or treatment-refractory cases, [[Ivermectin]] is added. Combination therapy with albendazole plus ivermectin achieves significantly higher cure and egg reduction rates than either drug alone (OR 2.65 for cure, per meta-analyses). [[Oxantel pamoate]] (in combination with albendazole) is a promising alternative showing high efficacy against *Trichuris*, as single-dose albendazole alone has limited efficacy against this parasite compared to other STHs.

## Prevention

Prevention centers on improved sanitation, hygiene education, hand washing, food safety (washing raw vegetables), and access to clean water. School-based mass drug administration (MDA) programs deliver periodic deworming (e.g., albendazole or mebendazole once or twice yearly) in endemic regions, reducing both prevalence and intensity of infection. Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) interventions targeting open defecation and soil contamination provide sustainable long-term control.
