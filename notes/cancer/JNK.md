# JNK

**c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)**, also known as stress-activated protein kinase (SAPK), is a member of the [[MAPK]] family that translates cellular stress—including UV, oxidative, genotoxic, and cytokine stress—into transcriptional and apoptotic responses. Three genes encode JNK isoforms (JNK1/MAPK8, JNK2/MAPK9, JNK3/MAPK10); JNK1 and JNK2 are broadly expressed. JNK regulates [[Apoptosis]], proliferation, and [[Inflammation]], and in cancer its function is famously dual—capable of suppressing tumors by triggering death or promoting them by driving survival and invasive signaling depending on context.

## Structure & Activation

JNKs are serine/threonine kinases activated by dual phosphorylation of a TPY motif in the activation loop. Upstream [[MAPK kinase|MAP2Ks]] MKK4 and MKK7 are themselves activated by MAP3Ks (ASK1, MEKK1, MLK3) responsive to ROS, calcium, and receptor signals. Activated JNK translocates to the nucleus and phosphorylates substrates including the transcription factor c-Jun (enhancing AP-1 activity), the BH3-only protein [[Bim]], the anti-apoptotic protein [[Mcl-1]], and p53.

## Mechanism of Action

JNK promotes apoptosis principally by phosphorylating [[Bim]], which displaces it from [[Microtubule]]-associated dynein complexes to activate [[Bax]]/Bak, and by phosphorylating and destabilizing [[Mcl-1]], removing a survival brake. Through c-Jun/AP-1, JNK also induces pro-inflammatory and proliferative gene programs. Context determines outcome: sustained JNK in normal cells favors death (suppressing tumor initiation), whereas in established tumors chronic JNK activation can support survival, [[EMT]], and [[Metastasis]] via cytokine and AP-1 networks.

## Physiological & Pathological Function

JNK is a sensor of [[Oxidative Stress]] and genotoxic damage, coupling these insults to either repair or death. In hepatocellular and other carcinomas, JNK signaling can be co-opted to enhance invasion and resistance to [[Chemotherapy]]. Conversely, JNK activation by certain cytotoxic therapies contributes to their efficacy, making JNK both a potential sensitizer and a resistance mediator.

## Clinical & Research Relevance

JNK isoforms are pursued as targets, though isoform-specific inhibitors (e.g., SP600125, AS601245) have seen limited clinical success because of the pathway's pleiotropy. The therapeutic strategy often focuses on modulating JNK contextually—e.g., combining JNK activation with agents that lower the [[Apoptosis]] threshold, or inhibiting JNK in tumors where it drives invasive signaling.
