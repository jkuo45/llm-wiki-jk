# Onchocerca volvulus

## Overview

*Onchocerca volvulus* is a filarial [[Nematode]] and the causative agent of [[Onchocerciasis]] (river blindness). Approximately 21 million people are infected worldwide, with 99% of cases concentrated in 31 African countries; smaller foci exist in Yemen and the Americas (Brazil, Venezuela). The parasite is transmitted by blackflies of the genus *[[Simulium]]*, which breed in fast-flowing rivers—hence the colloquial name "river blindness." Adult worms live coiled in subcutaneous nodules (onchocercomata), while first-stage larvae ([[Microfilariae]]) migrate through the skin and eyes, driving the pathology.

## Biology

Adult females measure 30–80 cm in length (males are smaller, ~3–5 cm) and are enveloped in fibrous host-derived nodules in subcutaneous tissue. Microfilariae are unsheathed, measure approximately 250–300 µm long, and are found in the dermis, lymphatics, and ocular tissues. A defining feature of *O. volvulus* is its obligate symbiotic relationship with *[[Wolbachia]]* endobacteria, which are essential for worm survival, embryogenesis, and fecundity. Targeting *Wolbachia* with antibiotics (e.g., [[Doxycycline]]) provides an indirect chemotherapeutic strategy that sterilizes adult females and leads to long-term parasite suppression.

## Life Cycle

The cycle begins when a female *Simulium* blackfly ingests microfilariae during a blood meal from an infected human. Within the fly, microfilariae penetrate the gut wall and migrate to thoracic flight muscles, developing through L1 and L2 larval stages to the infective L3 stage over 10–14 days. L3 larvae are transmitted to a new human host during a subsequent blood meal, entering through the bite wound. Larvae migrate through subcutaneous tissue, molting to L4 and then to immature adults over 6–12 months. Adult worms coil in subcutaneous nodules, where males migrate between nodules and females release unsheathed microfilariae. Microfilariae can survive for up to 12–18 months, and the adult reproductive lifespan may exceed 10–15 years, sustaining chronic infection.

## Disease

The clinical spectrum of onchocerciasis is driven primarily by host inflammatory responses to microfilariae.

**Dermatitis** manifests as intense pruritus, papular rash, and chronic scratching that leads to lichenification ("lizard skin"). Chronic depigmentation, particularly over the shins, produces a spotted appearance known as "leopard skin." These skin changes are disfiguring and socially stigmatizing.

**Ocular disease** is the most devastating consequence. Microfilariae migrate into the cornea, anterior chamber, and posterior segment, triggering immune-mediated inflammation that causes keratitis, iridocyclitis, chorioretinitis, and optic atrophy. Progressive corneal opacification and retinal damage lead to irreversible blindness. The Mazzotti reaction—an acute inflammatory flare of pruritus, urticaria, fever, and ocular inflammation—can occur after rapid microfilarial killing (e.g., with [[Ivermectin]] or diethylcarbamazine) and requires clinical attention.

**Diagnosis** is confirmed by skin snip biopsy (identification of microfilariae emerging from a small skin sample in saline), slit-lamp examination for microfilariae in the anterior chamber of the eye, and antigen-based diagnostic tests. Serology is less specific due to cross-reactivity with other filariae.

## Treatment

**[[Ivermectin]]** (150 µg/kg orally every 6–12 months) is the cornerstone of mass drug administration. It rapidly kills microfilariae, reducing skin and eye burden, but has limited activity against adult worms, requiring repeated dosing.

**[[Doxycycline]]** (100–200 mg daily for 4–6 weeks) targets *Wolbachia* endosymbionts, leading to female worm sterilization and a gradual decline in microfilarial production. This approach offers sustained therapeutic benefit and is particularly useful in co-endemic settings where elimination is feasible.

**Nodulectomy** (surgical removal of palpable onchocercomata) reduces adult worm burden and is practiced in localized settings.

**Prevention** relies on vector control (larviciding of *Simulium* breeding sites in fast-flowing rivers) and community-directed treatment with ivermectin (CDTI). No vaccine is currently available.
