# Strongyloides

**Strongyloides** is a genus of parasitic roundworms (nematodes) in the family Strongyloididae. The species of greatest medical importance is **Strongyloides stercoralis**, the cause of human [[Strongyloidiasis]]. These threadworms are exceptional among parasitic nematodes because they can complete both a parasitic (host-dependent) cycle and a free-living (environmental) cycle, and they are capable of **autoinfection** — the production of filariform larvae within the host that re-invade the same individual, allowing chronic infection to persist for decades.

## Life Cycle and Biology

Infection begins when skin contact with contaminated soil permits penetration by infective L3 (filariform) larvae. Larvae migrate via the bloodstream and lungs, are swallowed, and mature into parthenogenetic females in the small-intestinal mucosa, where they lay eggs that hatch into L1 (rhabditiform) larvae passed in stool. In the external environment, L1 can develop either into free-living adult worms (free-living cycle) or directly into infective L3. Critically, within the host some L1 mature into L3 that penetrate the intestinal wall or perianal skin — the **autoinfective cycle** — enabling lifelong persistence and, under immunosuppression, massive larval amplification (hyperinfection).

## Relevance to Cancer and Immunosuppression

The oncology-relevant dimension of *Strongyloides* concerns **iatrogenic immunosuppression**. Patients receiving corticosteroids, chemotherapy, or biologics (including anti-[[CTLA-4]] / anti-PD-1 checkpoint inhibitors) for [[Cancer]] may carry latent *S. stercoralis* infection. Glucocorticoids disrupt the Th2/[[Eosinophils]] milieu that normally constrains the parasite, precipitating **hyperinfection syndrome** and disseminated strongyloidiasis with high mortality. Screening (serology and stool PCR) before initiating immunosuppressive regimens is therefore recommended in endemic areas.

## Diagnosis and Treatment

Diagnosis combines stool microscopy (often low-yield due to intermittent shedding), serology, and molecular assays. Treatment relies on [[Ivermectin]] (preferred) or [[Albendazole]], with ivermectin also used for prophylaxis in at-risk immunocompromised patients. Eradication must be confirmed, since autoinfection precludes natural clearance.
