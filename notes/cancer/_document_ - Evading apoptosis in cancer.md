## Abstract

Carcinogenesis is a mechanistically complex and variable process with a plethora of underlying genetic causes. Cancer development consists of a multitude of steps that occur progressively starting with initial driver mutation(s), to tumorigenesis, and ultimately metastasis. During these transitions, cancer cells accumulate a series of genetic alterations that confer upon the cells an unwarranted survival and proliferative advantage. During the course of development, however, cancer cells also encounter a physiologically ubiquitous cellular program that aims to eliminate damaged or abnormal cells: Apoptosis. Thus, it is essential that cancer cells acquire instruments to circumvent programmed cell death. Here we discuss emerging evidence indicating how cancer cells adopt various strategies to override apoptosis including amplifying the anti-apoptotic machinery, downregulating the pro-apoptotic program, or both.

**Keywords:** BH3, Caspase, MOMP, Phosphorylation, Ubiquitination

## Regulation of apoptosis in normal and cancer cells

[[Apoptosis]] is a cellular suicide program that organisms have evolved to eliminate unnecessary or unhealthy cells from the body in the course of development or following cellular stress. It involves a series of cellular events that ultimately leads to activation of a family of cysteine proteases called [[Caspases]]. In response to various apoptotic stimuli, “initiator” [[Caspases]] ([[Caspase-2]], [[Caspase-8| -8]], [[Caspase-9| -9]], or [[Caspase-10| -10]]) are activated. Initiator [[Caspases]], in turn, cleave and activate the zymogenic forms of “executioner” [[Caspases]] (e.g. [[Caspase-3]] or [[Caspase-7| -7]]), resulting in the proteolytic cleavage of specific cellular substrates and, consequently, cell death ([Figure 1](#F1)). In this regard, the cleavage (activation) of executioner [[Caspases]] is a hallmark of [[Apoptosis]]. There are two routes to [[Apoptosis]]: [[Extrinsic Pathway|extrinsic]] and [[Intrinsic Pathway|intrinsic]]. In the [[Extrinsic Pathway|extrinsic pathway]], initiator [[Caspase-8]] and [[Caspase-10| -10]] are activated through the formation of a death-inducing signal complex ([[DISC]]) in response to engagement of extracellular ligands (e.g. [[Fas]] or [[TNF]] \[Tumor necrosis factor\]) by cell surface receptors ([Figure 1](#F1)). In the [[Intrinsic Pathway|intrinsic pathway]] of [[Apoptosis]], [[MOMP|mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP)]] is involved; [[MOMP]] triggers the release of a group of pro-apoptotic proteins, including [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] and [[Smac-DIABLO|second mitochondria-derived activator of caspases (SMAC)]], from the mitochondrial intermembrane space to the cytoplasm ([Figure 1](#F1)). In the cytoplasm, [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] binds to the adaptor protein [[Apaf-1|APAF-1 (apoptotic protease activating factor 1)]] to form a [[Caspase-9]]-activating complex, called the [[Apoptosome|apoptosome]] ([Figure 1](#F1)). [[Smac-DIABLO|SMAC]] augments [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] -induced caspase activation by binding and neutralizing [[XIAP]] (X-linked [[Inhibitor of Apoptosis Proteins|inhibitor of apoptosis protein]]), an inhibitor of [[Caspase-3]], [[Caspase-7| -7]], and [[Caspase-9| -9]] ([Figure 1](#F1)).

![Figure 1](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/6064/4091735/2e261e09f9a3/nihms508400f1.jpg)

The [[Intrinsic Pathway|intrinsic pathway]] to [[Apoptosis]] is initiated by activation of [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only protein]]. Among the [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only proteins]], whose expression is often transcriptionally induced following an apoptotic stimulus, [[Bid|BID]] is unique in that it is activated by caspase cleavage (by [[Caspase-2|caspases-2]] and [[Caspase-8|-8]]/[[Caspase-10|10]]); cleaved [[Bid|BID]], termed [[Bid|tBID]]. BH3 proteins trigger the activation of multi-BH domain proteins by direct binding or by inhibition of anti-apoptotic [[Bcl-2]] (B-cell lymphoma 2) family proteins. Once activated, multi-BH domain proteins form oligomers that induce [[MOMP]]. [[MOMP]] prompts pro-apoptotic proteins [[Smac-DIABLO|SMAC]] and [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome c]] to be released to the cytoplasm, where cytochrome induces the formation of the [[Apoptosome]]. The initiator [[Caspase-9]] activated within the [[Apoptosome]] initiates the activation of executioner [[Caspase-3]] and [[Caspase-7|-7]], resulting in [[Apoptosis]]. Of note, [[Caspase-2]] is activated via formation of the [[PIDDosome]], a complex which consists of the adaptor proteins [[PIDD]] (p53-induced death domain protein) and [[RAIDD]] (RIP-associated protein with a death domain). However, despite identification of this complex, the precise mechanism of caspase-2 activation remains elusive. In addition, only a few cellular substrates of caspase-2, including BID, have been identified. Colored triangles denote that the indicated gene or gene product may be transcriptionally (blue), translationally (red), and post-translationally (green) up- or down-regulated in cancer cells (see text and Table 1 ).

The balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins is a fundamental determinant for the initiation of [[MOMP]]. The [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins are classified based on the presence of shared blocks of sequence homology, termed [[Bcl-2 family|BCL-2 homology (BH)]]. The pro-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] members, which promote [[MOMP]], include [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only proteins]] (e.g. [[Bim|BIM]] \[Bcl2-interacting mediator of cell death\], [[Bid|BID]] \[BH3-interacting domain death agonist\], and [[Bad|BAD]] \[Bcl2-associated agonist of cell death\] that share only a single block, BH3 domain, of [[Bcl-2 family|BCL-2 homology]]) and multi-BH domain proteins (e.g. [[Bax]] \[Bcl2-associated protein X\] and [[Bak|BAK]] \[Bcl2 antagonist/killer\], which share BH1-BH3 domains). Following cytotoxic or genotoxic stress, [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only proteins]] are activated and promote oligomerization of [[Bax]] (or [[Bak|BAK]]), resulting in [[MOMP]], whereas pro-survival members (e.g. [[Bcl-2]], [[Bcl-xL]] \[B-cell lymphoma extra large\], and [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] \[Induced myeloid leukemia cell differentiation protein\], which contain all four BH domains) counteract this process by sequestering pro-apoptotic family members ([Figure 1](#F1)). Importantly, the interactions between pro- and anti-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins are determined with specific binding specificities \[[^1], [^2]\]; for instance, [[Bim|BIM]] interacts with all six anti-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins as well as [[Bax]] and [[Bak|BAK]] \[[^3]\], whereas [[Noxa|NOXA]] (Phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate-induced protein 1) only binds to [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] and [[A1-Bfl-1|BFL-1/A1]].

Unlike normal cells, cancer cells are under constant stress, such as oncogenic stress, genomic instability, and cellular hypoxia. In response to such internal apoptotic stimuli, the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis would normally be activated. Yet, cancer cells can often avoid this cellular response by disabling the apoptotic pathways. Notably, mouse genetic models have shown that genetic inactivation of a BH3-only protein or a caspase can not only lead to resistance to certain pro-apoptotic stimuli but also accelerate tumor formation in mice \[[^4] – [^6]\]. Moreover, forced expression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins can significantly augment tumor development induced by an oncogene, such as _MYC_, although overexpression of an anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family protein alone may not result in similar tumor formation as seen with an oncogene alone \[[^7] – [^9]\]. Thus, it is strongly suggested that inhibition of apoptosis plays a critical role in cancer cell survival and tumor development.

Cancer cells can modulate apoptotic pathways transcriptionally, translationally, and post-translationally. In some cases, cancer cells may escape apoptosis by increasing or decreasing expression of anti- or pro-apoptotic genes, respectively. Alternatively, they may inhibit apoptosis by stabilizing or de-stabilizing anti- or pro-apoptotic proteins, respectively. Moreover, cancer cells may also prevent apoptosis by changing the functions of anti- or pro-apoptotic proteins through post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation. Importantly, these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and cancer cells may employ one or multiple mechanisms to evade apoptosis. In this review, we highlight the core anti-apoptotic machinery that cancer cells may employ for survival, focusing our discussion on the transcriptional, translational, and post-translational modifications to evade apoptosis (note that recent studies suggest that cancer metabolism can modulate this machinery to protect cells from apoptosis \[[^7], [^8]\]; however, this is outside the current scope of this discussion).

## Transcriptional/translational regulation

Genomic and epigenomic abnormalities are a characteristic of cancer cells. This includes gene copy number amplification, gene deletion, gene silencing by DNA methylation, and activation (or inactivation) of transcription factors, which impact expression of apoptotic regulators \[[^10]\]. In addition, microRNAs negatively control gene expression by targeting 3′UTR of mRNAs, although they could function as tumor suppressors or oncogenes, depending on the target messengers \[[^11]\]. Thus, transcriptional and translational alternations can be a means for cancer cells to directly raise a threshold for apoptotic induction at gene expression levels. In this section, we will discuss how cancer cells may increase or block expression of anti- or pro-apoptotic gene products, respectively (see [Table 1](#T1) for loss or amplification of apoptotic genes identified in cancer tissues).

### Inducing Anti-apoptotic Protein Expression

To overcome stress signals, cancer cells commonly overexpress anti-apoptotic proteins, especially anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins. Cancer cells prevent MOMP by amplification of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins and inhibition of one or multiple anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins cause apoptosis in cancer cells, but not in healthy normal cells – it is often said that cancer cells are primed to death or addicted to anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins. Overexpression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins is often associated with poor prognosis, recurrence, and resistance to cancer therapeutics \[[^12] – [^13]\]. The _BCL-2_ gene was originally found in B-cell follicular lymphoma where genetic translocation resulted in constitutive expression of BCL-2 \[[^14] – [^15]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Amplification of the _BCL-XL_ gene has been reported in various cancers, including lung and giant-cell tumor of bone \[[^16] – [^18]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Likewise, frequent amplification of the _MCL-1_ gene has also been reported in breast and lung cancer samples \[[^18],[^19]\] ([Table 1](#T1)).

In normal cells, [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] transcription is induced upon cytokine and growth factor stimulation \[[^20], [^21]\], while its synthesis is inhibited upon various cellular stresses, including DNA damage \[[^22] – [^24]\]. In particular, it was shown that the transcription factor [[E2F1]], known to control proliferation and [[Apoptosis]], could repress _[[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]_ gene expression by directly binding to the _[[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]_ promoter \[[^25]\], resulting in increased [[Apoptosis]]. Alternatively, the transcription factors CREB (cAMP response element-binding protein) and [[STAT3]] (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3), known to activate genes responding to pro-survival stimuli, can transactivate the _[[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]_ gene \[[^20],[^22]\]. Importantly, whereas these signaling pathways operate to maintain cellular homeostasis in normal cells, they are often deregulated in cancer cells. Especially, since the [[PI3K|phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)]]/[[Akt]] pathway is frequently hyperactivated in cancer cells by mutations and other mechanisms, the transcriptional regulation of this pathway (e.g., activation of CREB or [[STAT3]]) could promote _[[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]_ expression in certain cancer cells ([Figure 2](#F2)). In this regard, it was also shown that _[[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]_ mRNA translation could be facilitated by [[mTORC1]] (mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1), a downstream target of [[PI3K]]/[[Akt]] signaling, in a mouse lymphoma model ([Figure 2](#F2)) \[[^26]\].

![Figure 2](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/6064/4091735/a02b1c9172bc/nihms508400f2.jpg)

Oncogenic kinases regulate BIM, PUMA, and MCL-1 by changing gene expression, protein stability, and protein functions by activating two major pro-survival pathways: the AKT and ERK (Extracellular signal-regulated kinases) pathways. Pro-survival and pro-apoptotic pathways are denoted in blue and red, respectively.

microRNAs also play a role in the regulation of anti-apoptotic proteins. miR-15 and miR-16 inhibit _BCL-2_ mRNA translation \[[^27]\]. Likewise, miR-29b, miR-101, and miR-193a-3p suppress _MCL-1_ mRNA translation, making cells susceptible to apoptosis \[[^28] – [^29]\] – note that the role of miR-29b in cell death may be complex as it also targets several BH3-only proteins, inhibiting apoptosis in neurons \[[^30]\]. Expression of miR-15 and miR-16 is reduced in chronic lymphocytic leukemia \[[^27]\], whereas expression of miR-101 is diminished in hepatocellular carcinoma \[[^29]\] suggesting a possible mechanism to evade apoptosis by these cancers. Last, two recent studies have shown that expression of miR-7 and miR-24, which inhibit _XIAP_ mRNA, is suppressed in various cancer cells including cervical cancer and lung cancer cells, resulting in elevated XIAP protein levels and blocked caspase activation \[[^31], [^32]\]. It would be interesting to fully investigate whether expression of these microRNAs is altered in various cancer tissues and whether their expression patterns are associated with prognostic outcomes.

### Suppressing Pro-apoptotic Protein Expression

In normal cells, genotoxic and cytotoxic stress can induce expression of pro-apoptotic genes, particularly some of the BH3-only proteins that trigger apoptosis. In cancer cells, this mechanism is often nullified by mutation and silencing of a key apoptotic gene(s).

In normal cells, [[p53]] is among the most critical transcription factor that modulates the expression of an array of genes known to trigger [[Apoptosis]] following various cellular stresses. Given the fact that _TP53_ encoding [[p53]] is the most frequently mutated, or inactivated, tumor suppressor gene in cancer (~50%) \[[^33], [^34]\], apoptotic pathways induced by this gene, including _[[Bax]]_, _[[Puma|PUMA]]_ ([[p53]] upregulated modulator of apoptosis), _[[Noxa|NOXA]]_, _[[Apaf-1|APAF-1]]_, may be widely affected in cancer cells \[[^35] – [^38]\] – note that frameshift mutations in the _[[Bax]]_ gene have also been reported in colon cancer \[[^39]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Interestingly, [[p53]] could also activate [[Bax]] (and [[Bak|BAK]]) in a transcription-independent manner in response to DNA damage and metabolic changes \[[^40] – [^41]\]. Thus, loss of [[p53]] in cancer cells will elevate a threshold for [[MOMP]] by limiting _[[Bax]]_ expression and reducing a chance of [[Bax]] oligomerization.

The transcription of the [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only protein]] [[Bim|BIM]] is induced upon growth factor withdrawal and other apoptotic stimuli \[[^5], [^42]\]. Various transcription factors have been implicated in _[[Bim|BIM]]_ expression, including [[FOXO Transcription Factors|FOXOs]] \[[^42] – [^43]\], RUNX3 \[[^44]\], AP-1 \[[^45]\], and [[E2F1]] \[[^46]\]. Among the transcription factors, [[FOXO Transcription Factors|FOXOs]] are of particular interest since its activity can be suppressed by two major pro-survival kinases, [[Akt]] \[[^47]\] and [[ERK]] \[[^48] – [^49]\]. Thus, in cancer cells with high levels of [[Akt]] or [[ERK]], [[Bim|BIM]] expression is suppressed; conversely, agents that directly or indirectly inhibit [[Akt]] or [[ERK]] could trigger expression of [[Bim|BIM]] ([Figure 2](#F2)).

[[Bim|BIM]] plays an important role in cancer cells when [[Apoptosis]] is induced by [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)]] \[[^5], [^49]\]. Since [[Akt]] and [[ERK]] are key effectors downstream of oncogenic kinases (e.g., [[EGFR]] \[epidermal growth factor receptor\], [[HER2]] \[Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2\], BCR-ABL), inhibition of the tyrosine kinase could result in suppression of [[Akt]] and [[ERK]] activity, resulting in the expression of [[Bim|BIM]] ([Figure 2](#F2)). Notably, a recent study showed that [[Bim|BIM]] RNA levels can predict clinical responsiveness to targeted cancer therapy, such as [[EGFR]], [[HER2]], and [[PI3K]] inhibitors; higher [[Bim|BIM]] RNA levels detected prior to the inhibitor treatments are associated with better clinical outcomes \[[^49]\]. Additionally, a _[[Bim|BIM]]_ deletion polymorphism has been reported in certain human populations, and is significantly associated with innate resistance to [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|TKI]] therapies in chronic myeloid leukemia and [[non-small-cell lung cancer]] \[[^50]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Therefore, [[Bim|BIM]] induction is critical for the efficacy of [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|TKI]] therapies and [[Bim|BIM]] RNA levels may be used as a biomarker to predict cancer patients’ prognoses and susceptibility to [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|TKI]] therapies.

A large somatic copy number analysis identified another [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only family member]] _[[Puma|PUMA]]_ as one of the pro-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] genes that are most frequently deleted in cancer (the study identified two genes and the other deleted gene was _BOK_) \[[^18]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). _[[Puma|PUMA]]_ was originally discovered as a [[p53]]- and p73 target gene \[[^36], [^51]\]. However, [[FOXO Transcription Factors|FOXOs]] can also transactivate _[[Puma|PUMA]]_, independently of [[p53]], in response to growth factor withdrawal or [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|TKI]] treatments \[[^5], [^52]\] ([Figure 2](#F2)). In this regard, a recent study has shown that [[Puma|PUMA]] may be implicated in [[Apoptosis]] triggered by [[FOXO Transcription Factors|FOXOs]] following inhibition of the [[PI3K]]-[[Akt]] pathway, while [[Bim|BIM]] may be primarily under control of the [[ERK]] pathway in [[HER2]]-positive [[Breast Cancer]] and [[EGFR]] mutant [[Lung Cancer]] (as described below, [[Bim|BIM]] is also regulated at the protein levels by [[ERK]]); for full execution of [[Apoptosis]], inhibition of both pathways by [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|TKIs]], i.e., expression of both [[Puma|PUMA]] and [[Bim|BIM]], is required \[[^5]\]. This finding sheds light on an underappreciated role of [[Puma|PUMA]] in [[Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors|TKI]]-induced [[Apoptosis]]. Since _[[Bim|BIM]]_ is also known to be regulated by [[FOXO Transcription Factors|FOXOs]], how [[Puma|PUMA]] and [[Bim|BIM]] are independently regulated remains to be fully elucidated ([Figure 2](#F2)).

In addition to the signaling pathways that impede activation of BAX and BAK, a defect in the pathway following cytochrome _c_ release could further provide cancer cells an additional survival advantage, independent of BAX and BAK inhibition ([Figure 1](#F1)). Expression of some caspase genes (caspases-2, 7, -8, and -9) is transcriptionally regulated in response to E2F1 or p53 \[[^53] – [^54]\]; in cancer cell where these pathways are inactivated, expression of these caspases could be compromised resulting in apoptotic protection. _APAF-1_ gene is transactivated by p53 \[[^37], [^38]\] and E2F1 \[[^38], [^55]\] and its mRNA translation is initiated through an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) \[[^56]\]. Loss of APAF-1 expression has been reported in melanoma \[[^57] – [^58]\], colorectal cancer \[[^59], [^60]\], gastric cancer \[[^61]\] and bladder cancer \[[^62], [^63]\], and its loss is often associated with poor clinical outcomes. Studies show that loss of APAF-1 expression in cancer cells can be secondary to promoter methylation \[[^57], [^60], [^61], [^63]\] and loss of heterogeneity at the _APAF-1_ locus \[[^57] – [^58], [^61]\] ([Table 1](#T1)).

Collectively, it is evident that the transcriptional network is deregulated in cancer cells, which significantly impacts the expression of apoptotic regulators and, consequently, contributes to tumor development and resistance to therapeutic agents.

## Post-translational regulation

Various apoptotic regulators are modulated not only by transcriptional/translational machinery but also by post-translational modifications, including [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] and [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]]. Protein [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] is implicated in protein stability and is dynamically regulated through the ubiquitin/proteasome pathway \[[^64]\], whereas [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] can change protein functions \[[^65], [^66]\]. It should be noted, however, that [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] can also influence [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] of the target protein by providing a docking site for an E3 ubiquitin ligase (phospho-degron).

The [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitin/proteasome pathway]] involves a series of biochemical events, which are mediated by E1 ubiquitin-activating enzymes, E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes, and E3 ubiquitin ligases \[[^67]\]. In normal cells, expression levels of an anti-apoptotic protein with a short half-life (e.g., [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]) may readily decline by inhibition of transcription or translation following an apoptotic stimulus. In contrast, an apoptotic signal may prevent constant degradation of a pro-apoptotic protein (e.g., [[Bim|BIM]]), resulting in the accumulation of that protein. Importantly, cancer cells may deregulate this process by enhancing the degradability of specific pro-apoptotic proteins or by promoting the stability of anti-apoptotic proteins. This may also be the case when cancer cells acquire resistance to a therapeutic agent. It was recently shown that [[HER2]]-amplified breast cancer cells modulate stabilities of pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins, both upstream and downstream of [[MOMP]], when acquiring resistance to the [[HER2]] inhibitor [[lapatinib]]; consequently, the resistant cancer cells exhibit strong protection against cell death induced by [[HER2]] inhibition \[[^68]\].

Here we discuss how the stability of some apoptotic regulators ([[Mcl-1|MCL-1]], [[Bim|BIM]], and [[Bax]]) is controlled in normal cells and how this stability may be altered in cancer cells, contributing to apoptotic protection. Then, we will highlight some examples where [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] plays a role to alter functions of apoptotic regulators.

### Stabilizing MCL-1

Whereas [[Bcl-2]] and [[Bcl-xL]] proteins are relatively stable, [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein is characterized by its rapid turnover \[[^23], [^69]\]. As described above, [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] transcription is stimulated, at least in part, by pro-survival factors, such as cytokines. Hence, once its transcription is suppressed following apoptotic stimuli, [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein levels quickly drop, potentially facilitating [[MOMP]]. Importantly, emerging evidence strongly suggest that the rapid turnover of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein may be abrogated in some cancer cells. Thus far, five E3 ligases have been discovered to ubiquitinate [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] for degradation, including the most recently identified TRIM17 (tripartite motif containing17), which facilitates [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] degradation during neuronal [[Apoptosis]] \[[^70]\]. It should be noted, however, that [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein could also be degraded in a [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] independent manner \[[^71]\] although the precise mechanism remains elusive.

[[Phosphorylation]] also plays a key role in the stability of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein. [[Phosphorylation]] of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] at T163 followed by [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] at S155/S159 targets the protein for proteasomal degradation, which is mediated by the E3 ligase [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]]</sup> (Skp, Cullin, F-box containing complex with [[Beta-TrCP|β-transducin repeat-containing protein]]) \[[^69], [^72]\]. The priming [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] site T163 is a target of multiple kinases ([Figure 3](#F3)). An initial study identified [[GSK3|glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3)]] as the major kinase that phosphorylates this residue; however, a more recent study demonstrated that [[JNK]] (c-Jun N terminal kinase) primarily phosphorylates T163; in both cases, T163 [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] will trigger subsequent [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of S159 (and then S155) by [[GSK3]] \[[^69], [^73]\]. Collectively, [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] at S155/S159/T163 serves as a degron sequence for [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]]</sup> ([Table 1](#T1) and [Figure 3](#F3)).

![Figure 3](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/6064/4091735/18331d498410/nihms508400f3.jpg)

(A) [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] can be phosphorylated at T163 by several kinases including [[GSK3]]. [[Akt]] inhibits [[GSK3]]. Thus, in low levels of [[Akt]], [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] by [[GSK3]] sequentially occurs at S155/S159, targeting [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] for degradation by E3 ligases, [[SCF Complex|SCF]] [[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]], [[SCF Complex|SCF]] [[FBW7]] (F-box/WD repeat-containing protein 7), and TRIM17. (B) In cancer cells with high levels of [[Akt]] and [[ERK]], [[ERK]] is likely to be the main kinase that phosphorylates [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]. [[Phosphorylation]] does not continue to S155/S159 and [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] is stabilized.

Paradoxically, it has also been reported that [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] at T163 by [[ERK]] can lead to marked [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] stabilization \[[^74], [^75]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). How can we reconcile these conclusions? Is the mechanism of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] degradation simply cell type specific? We speculate that it may be [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of S159 that determines the fate of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein following T163 [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]]. It is known that [[GSK3]] activity is negatively regulated by [[Akt]], which is commonly upregulated in cancer cells; therefore, in cancer cells with high [[Akt]] and [[ERK]] activity, [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] may be only phosphorylated at T163 but not at S155 and S159, which promotes stabilization of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] ([Figure 3](#F3)). Supporting this, it is reported that the abundance of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein is associated with low [[GSK3]] activity in breast cancer tissues \[[^76]\]. In contrast, in normal cells, cellular stress signals, such as growth factor deprivation, would lead to inactivation of [[Akt]] (and activation of [[JNK]]), which elicits serial [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] events at S155/S159/T163; this would result in degradation of the [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein ([Figure 3](#F3)). It should be noted, however, that there is at least one additional E3 ligase [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[FBW7]]</sup>, that also regulates [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] stability depending on the [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] status of T163 (see below). Thus, the regulation of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein stability mediated by this [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] motif is apparently complex and the mechanism remains to be fully elucidated.

A failure in the cell cycle can trigger [[Apoptosis]] in normal cells. Furthermore, prolonged mitotic arrest can lead to the activation of the [[Intrinsic Pathway|intrinsic apoptotic pathway]]. For this reason, some of microtubule inhibitors that induce mitotic arrest by inhibiting mitotic spindle assembly (e.g., [[paclitaxel]]) are clinically used to treat various cancers. However, the emergence of cancer cells with intrinsic or acquired resistance to this type of chemotherapeutic agents is an unresolved clinical problem. Three independent studies described the mechanism of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] degradation during prolonged mitotic arrest, suggesting a possible role for [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] stabilization in the resistance to mitotic [[Apoptosis]]. These studies have shown that in addition to the mechanisms described above, two E3 ligases, [[APC-C|APC/C]] <sup>[[CDC20]]</sup> ([[APC-C|Anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome]] with [[CDC20|cell-division cycle protein 20]]) and [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[FBW7]]</sup>, target [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] for destruction during mitosis \[[^77] – [^79]\]. [[APC-C|APC/C]] <sup>[[CDC20]]</sup> [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] is mediated by [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] at T92 by CDK1 (cyclin-dependent kinase 1)/CYCLIN B and the D-box motif during mitotic arrest \[[^77]\]. [[Ubiquitination]] of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] by [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[FBW7]]</sup> is mediated by two phospho-degrons on [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] (S121/E125 and S159/T163), of which S159/T163 appears to play a major role \[[^78], [^79]\]. Interestingly, [[JNK]], p38, and CKII (Casein kinase II) phosphorylate both degrons during mitotic arrest \[[^79]\] although [[GSK3]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] at S159/T163 may be sufficient to target [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] for [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[FBW7]]</sup> -mediated degradation \[[^78]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Since, unlike CDK1/CYCLIN B, these kinases are not directly controlled by the cell cycle, the mechanism of mitotic phosphorylation of MCL-1 remains undetermined. In addition, it is not known whether APC/C <sup>CDC20</sup> and SCF <sup>FBW7</sup> compete for the mitotic degradation of MCL-1 or work independently. From the clinical viewpoint, it is interesting to note that mutations or loss of FBW7 (a substrate-binding component of the multi-subunit E3 complex SCF <sup>FBW7</sup>) has been reported in various cancer types \[[^80] – [^81]\]. Moreover, cancer cells with low FBW7 levels have elevated MCL-1 protein and exhibit intrinsic resistance to ABT-737, a small molecule inhibitor of BCL-2, BCL-XL, and BCL-W \[[^78]\] ([BOX 1](#BX1)).

#### BOX 1. BH3 mimetics and cancer therapy.

The binding between pro- and anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins is mediated through a hydrophobic groove on the anti-apoptotic proteins and a BH3 domain. Several small molecule inhibitors, so-called BH3 mimetics (e.g., ABT-737, ATB-263, ATB-199), have been engineered to specifically bind to the hydrophobic pocket, thereby antagonizing the function of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins. The initial inhibitor of this class, ABT-737, and its orally bioavailable analog ABT-263, both of which inhibit both BCL-2 and BCL-XL, were a pre-clinical success \[[^163], [^164]\]. Nonetheless, the clinical success was hampered by thrombocytopenia caused by BCL-XL inhibition. To circumvent this problem, a BCL-2 specific inhibitor ABT-199 was recently developed and is now being tested in clinical trials \[[^165]\]. Another challenge in BCL-2/BCL-XL inhibitors is that cancer cells can acquire resistance to this class of inhibitors by overexpressing MCL-1 \[[^166]\]. Recently, two groups independently identified MCL-1 inhibitors through genomic or biochemical screenings: Golub and colleagues identified compounds that suppress _MCL-1_ transcription \[[^167]\], whereas Walensky and colleagues discovered a small molecule, MIM1, which binds to the BH3-domain binding pocket of MCL-1 \[[^168]\] – these inhibitors may overcome resistance to BCL-2/BCL-XL inhibitors. Of note, the short protein half-life of MCL-1 makes MCL-1-addicted cancer cells more susceptible to chemotherapy than cancer cells depending on BCL-2 or BCL-XL for survival \[[^8]\]. However, given the multiple mechanisms of MCL-1 stabilization in cancer cells (see text), it is assumed that this approach may not work in all of MCL-1-addicted cancer cells. For cancer cells with stabilized MCL-1, agents that directly inhibit MCL-1 protein (e.g., MIM1) or promote MCL-1 protein degradation may be an option.

A HECT-domain containing E3 ligase, [[HUWE1]] ([[HUWE1|MULE]], [[Mcl-1|Mcl-1]] ubiquitin ligase E3), also targets [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] for proteasomal degradation through a pathway that does not require [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] by [[GSK3]]; the binding between [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] and [[HUWE1]] is mediated through the BH3 domains on each protein \[[^82]\]. Interestingly, it has been reported that [[ERK]] activity, directly or indirectly, reduces the interaction between [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] and [[HUWE1]] although the detailed molecular mechanism remains to be elucidated \[[^83]\]. It would be interesting to test whether the aforementioned T163 [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] by [[ERK]] is implicated in the [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]-[[HUWE1]] interaction.

Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are negative regulators of the [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]]-mediated protein degradation as DUBs can stabilize their substrate proteins by removing polyubiquitin chains. [[USP9X|Ubiquitin specific peptidase 9 X-linked, USP9X]], reverses [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] \[[^84]\]. As opposed to [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]]</sup> and [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[FBW7]]</sup>, the binding of [[USP9X]] to [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] is negatively regulated by [[GSK3]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] at S155/S159/T163, suggesting that [[USP9X]] may directly counteract [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] degradation controlled by [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]]</sup> and [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[FBW7]]</sup>. [[USP9X]] and [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] protein expression levels were positively correlated in several cancer types, including follicular lymphomas \[[^84]\]. Elevated [[USP9X]] transcription was also associated with poor prognosis in multiple myelomas \[[^84]\].

### Destroying Pro-apoptotic Proteins

As cancer cells can attenuate [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] degradation, they may also suppress accumulation of pro-apoptotic proteins, such as [[Bim|BIM]]. As with [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]], [[Bim|BIM]] is also characterized by its short protein half-life. [[Bim|BIM]] stability is regulated by [[ERK]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] at S69; [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of this site promotes [[Bim|BIM]] degradation through the [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitin-proteasome pathway]] \[[^85], [^86]\]. A transgenic mouse study demonstrated that replacement of the [[ERK]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] sites with Ala prolongs [[Bim|BIM]] protein half-life and increases apoptotic cell death in response to serum starvation \[[^4]\]. [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]]</sup>, c-CBL, and TRIM2 are the E3 ligases responsible for [[ERK]]-dependent [[Bim|BIM]] degradation \[[^87] – [^89]\]. Notably, [[SCF Complex|SCF]] <sup>[[Beta-TrCP|β-TrCP]]</sup> -mediated [[Bim|BIM]] [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] requires [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of the degron sequence containing S93/S94/S98 by [[RSK]] (ribosomal S6 kinase), which is promoted by [[ERK]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] at S69 \[[^88]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Whether [[Bim|BIM]] [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] by c-CBL and TRIM2 may also require [[RSK]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] remains elusive. Last, the ubiquitin E3 ligase complex ElonginB/C-Cullin2-CIS is also implicated in [[Bim|BIM]] [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] and degradation \[[^90]\]. [[Bim|BIM]] [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] mediated by ElonginB/C-Cullin2-CIS is enhanced following [[paclitaxel]] treatment and a defect in this [[Ubiquitination|ubiquitination]] pathway may confer resistance to this agent in cancer cells \[[^90]\].

[[Bax]] is a central component of [[Apoptosis]] as its activation is directly linked to [[MOMP]] ([Figure 1](#F1)). Thus, causing degradation of [[Bax]] protein may be a “last minute” safeguard for cancer cells to prevent [[Apoptosis]]. [[Bax]] protein mainly localizes in the cytoplasm. Upon genotoxic or cytotoxic stress, [[Bax]] translocates to the mitochondria outer membrane, forms oligomeric structures, and triggers [[MOMP]] \[[^91]\]. A recent study has shown, however, that the subcellular localization of [[Bax]] protein may be in a more dynamic equilibrium between the cytoplasm and the mitochondrial outer membrane \[[^92]\]. Although the detailed mechanism of how the equilibrium may change following apoptotic stimuli remains elusive, the [[Bax]] equilibrium hypothesis suggests there is a mechanism that prevents activation of [[Bax]] on the mitochondrial outer membrane. Interestingly, it has been known that [[Bcl-xL]] can block the mitochondrial translocation of [[Bax]], by re-directing [[Bax]] back to the cytoplasm \[[^93]\]. Moreover, [[Bax]] degradation takes place on mitochondrial outer membrane following apoptotic stimuli \[[^94], [^95]\]. [[Bax]] is a protein with a short half-life \[[^96], [^97]\] and the rapid degradation of [[Bax]] protein has been associated with poor clinical outcomes and high tumor grades in chronic lymphocytic leukemia and [[Prostate Cancer]] \[[^96]\]. The E3 ligase IBRDC2 (IBR domain containing 2; also known as p53RFP or RNF144B) was recently identified and shown to ubiquitinate [[Bax]] specifically on the mitochondrial outer membrane \[[^95]\]. Overexpression of IBRDC2, however, did not block [[Apoptosis]] triggered by actinomycin D or staurosporine treatment \[[^95]\], suggesting that there may be additional factors (e.g., co-factors or other ligases) involved in this process.

### Altering protein functions

In addition to modulating a docking site for an E3 ligase and subsequent ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] can also alter the function of apoptotic regulators. In particular, [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] by pro-survival kinases (e.g., [[Akt]] and [[ERK]]) often results in suppression of the pro-apoptotic function of the target protein, whereas [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] by pro-apoptotic kinases (e.g., [[GSK3]] and [[JNK]]) can lead to activation of pro-apoptotic proteins or inhibition of anti-apoptotic proteins (see [Table 1](#T1) for a comprehensive list of known [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] sites of apoptotic regulators). For instance, the pro-apoptotic activity of the [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only protein]] [[Bad|BAD]] is inhibited by a number of pro-survival kinases, including PAK1 and [[Akt]] \[[^98] – [^100]\]. Likewise, [[ERK]] [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of [[Bim|BIM]] prevents the interaction between [[Bim|BIM]] and [[Bax]], thereby inhibiting [[Apoptosis]], whereas [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of [[Bim|BIM]] by [[JNK]] or p38 MAP kinase promotes the pro-apoptotic activity of [[Bim|BIM]] by enhancing binding to anti-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins \[[^4],[^101] – [^104]\]. As described above, [[ERK]] also promotes suppression of _[[Bim|BIM]]_ expression and degradation of [[Bim|BIM]] protein \[[^4], [^85], [^86]\] ([Figure 2](#F2)), further highlighting a critical role for [[ERK]] in [[Bim|BIM]] inhibition.

Pro-survival kinases also negatively regulate apoptotic pathways downstream of mitochondrial [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] release ([Figure 1](#F1)). Activation of [[Apaf-1|APAF-1]] in response to [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] is inhibited via [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] of [[Apaf-1|APAF-1]] by [[RSK]] \[[^105]\], a kinase often amplified in [[Prostate Cancer]] \[[^106]\] ([Table 1](#T1)). Leukemic tyrosine kinases, such as BCR-ABL, also inhibit [[Apaf-1|APAF-1]] oligomerization by modulating the [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] status of the [[Apaf-1|Apaf-1 inhibitor]] [[HSP90β]] \[[^107]\]. In addition to [[Apaf-1|APAF-1]], [[Caspases]] are direct targets of various kinases; activation of [[Caspases]] can be suppressed by direct [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] ([Table 1](#T1)). Furthermore, it is known that [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] can protect some caspase substrates from proteolytic cleavage \[[^66]\]. For example, [[Phosphorylation|phosphorylation]] by CK1 or CK2 protects [[Bid|BID]] from [[Caspase-8|caspase-8]], thereby blocking the activation of this [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only protein]] and subsequent [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] release from mitochondria \[[^108]\] ([Table 1](#T1)).

In summary, post-translational modifications play a critical role in the regulation of [[Apoptosis]] by modulating protein stabilities and functions.

## Concluding remarks

In order to survive and proliferate, cancer cells need to override a number of barriers that otherwise would cause [[Apoptosis]]. It is conceivable that cancer cells employ multiple mechanisms, instead of a single pathway. Given the multiplicity of anti-apoptotic mechanisms utilized by cancer cells, it is speculated that agents that can globally alter cancer’s apoptotic resistance by targeting multiple pathways, may provide us with the most valuable therapeutic benefits in the future of cancer treatment. To target cancer cells most efficiently and selectively, we must further elucidate the mechanisms of how they evade the apoptotic program (see Outstanding Questions box).

## Outstanding Questions.

- Do cancer cells simply depend on the net abundance of pro-survival [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins relative to the total [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only proteins]]? Alternatively, may the regulation of a particular [[Bcl-2 family]] member selectively play a role for survival in certain cancer types? How do the functional redundancy and specificity of pro-survival [[Bcl-2 family]] impact cancer cell survival?
- Cancer cells employ multiple mechanisms to silence the apoptotic machinery. Letai and colleagues have developed a functional assay, called [[BH3 profiling]], to gauge the dependency of cancer cells on anti-apoptotic [[Bcl-2 family]] proteins. Although the [[BH3 profiling]] made a successful breakthrough \[[^169]\], is there any other way to predict which pathways are altered in particular cancer cells?
- Abundance of [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] is one of the mechanisms for the resistance to a [[Bcl-2]]/[[Bcl-xL]] inhibitor in cancer ([BOX 1](#BX1)). If a [[Bcl-2]]/[[Bcl-xL]] inhibitor is used concomitantly with an [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] inhibitor, can we cure all of cancer? Alternatively, will cancer cells eventually acquire the resistance to the combination of a [[Bcl-2]]/[[Bcl-xL]] inhibitor and an [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] inhibitor? If so, how?
- Why is the pathway downstream of mitochondrial [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome *c*]] often inhibited in cancer cells in addition to the inhibition of [[MOMP]] \[[^170]\]?
- The failure to activate [[Caspases]] in response to an apoptotic stimulus can result in caspase-independent cell death \[[^171]\]. Does caspase-independent cell death play a role in cancer cell survival?
