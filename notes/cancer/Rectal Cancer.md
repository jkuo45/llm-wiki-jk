# Rectal Cancer

Rectal cancer is a malignancy arising from the rectal epithelium, anatomically defined as the last 12–15 cm of the large intestine proximal to the anal verge. While often grouped with [[Colon Cancer]] as colorectal cancer, rectal cancer is managed distinctly due to pelvic anatomical constraints, higher local recurrence risk, and differing treatment paradigms centered on sphincter preservation.

## Overview

The rectum is encased within the narrow bony pelvis, making surgical resection technically challenging and limiting the ability to achieve wide radial margins. Unlike colon cancer, where surgery is the primary modality, rectal cancer routinely incorporates [[neoadjuvant]] therapy. The principal oncologic priority — beyond cure — is preservation of anal sphincter function and quality of life, which shapes virtually every treatment decision.

## Treatment

**Neoadjuvant chemoradiation (nCRT):** Standard of care for locally advanced rectal cancer (stage II–III). Radiation (45–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions) is delivered concurrently with a radiosensitizer, either [[5-FU]] (continuous infusion) or [[Capecitabine]] (oral prodrug). This regimen achieves tumor downstaging, increases the likelihood of sphincter-sparing surgery, and reduces local recurrence from ~25% to <10%.

**Total mesorectal excision (TME):** Surgical removal of the rectum and its surrounding mesorectal envelope as an intact unit. TME is the gold-standard surgical technique and, combined with nCRT, has dramatically improved local control and survival.

**Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT):** An emerging strategy in which systemic chemotherapy (e.g., [[FOLFOX]] or [[CAPOX]]) is delivered before or after chemoradiation, prior to surgery. TNT improves pathologic complete response (pCR) rates and is increasingly adopted for high-risk disease.

**Watch-and-wait (non-operative management):** Patients achieving a clinical complete response (cCR) after nCRT may be offered selective non-operative management with intensive surveillance (digital exam, endoscopy, [[MRI]]). This approach avoids surgery-related morbidity and permanent colostomy. Long-term outcomes appear comparable to immediate TME in carefully selected patients.

**Recurrence patterns:** Rectal cancer exhibits a higher propensity for locoregional recurrence (pelvic) compared to colon cancer, which more often recurs distantly. This difference underpins the emphasis on aggressive local therapy in the rectum.

## Clinical Presentation

Symptoms include tenesmus (sensation of incomplete evacuation), rectal bleeding, altered stool caliber, and obstructive symptoms. Staging relies on [[endorectal ultrasound]] (EUS) and [[MRI]] for T-stage assessment. MRI evaluation of the mesorectal fascia (MRF) is critical: MRF involvement (threatened or involved circumferential resection margin) dictates the need for nCRT.

## Molecular Pathology

Rectal cancers harbor a higher frequency of [[APC]] mutations and lower rates of microsatellite instability ([[MSI]]) compared to right-sided colon cancers. [[TP53]] mutations are common, and the [[Wnt signaling pathway]] is nearly universally dysregulated.
