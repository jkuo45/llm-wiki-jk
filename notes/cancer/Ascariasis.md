# Ascariasis

Ascariasis is a disease caused by the parasitic roundworm [[Ascaris]] lumbricoides, the most common helminth infection in humans, affecting an estimated 800 million to 1 billion people worldwide. Prevalence is highest in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation, where contamination of soil with human feces facilitates transmission. Children are disproportionately affected, and chronic heavy burdens can impair growth and cognitive development.

## Life Cycle

The life cycle of *Ascaris lumbricoides* begins when embryonated eggs from contaminated soil are ingested via unwashed hands, produce, or water. Larvae hatch in the small intestine, penetrate the intestinal wall, and enter the portal circulation, traveling through the liver and heart to the lungs. In the pulmonary capillaries, larvae break into alveolar spaces, ascend the bronchial tree, and are coughed up and swallowed. Upon return to the small intestine, larvae molt into adult worms (15–30 cm in length), which mate and produce up to 200,000 eggs per day, excreted in stool. The entire cycle from ingestion to egg production takes approximately 2–3 months.

## Clinical Presentation

Most infections are asymptomatic, with worm burden determining disease severity. Light infections may cause vague abdominal discomfort, while heavy burdens produce malnutrition, abdominal distension, and colicky pain. The most serious complication in children is intestinal obstruction, which can be fatal without surgical intervention. Adult worms migrating into the pancreatic or biliary ducts can cause acute pancreatitis, cholecystitis, or cholangitis. The pulmonary phase (Löffler syndrome) manifests as cough, eosinophilia, and transient pulmonary infiltrates resulting from larval migration through alveolar tissue. Diagnosis is confirmed by stool microscopy identifying characteristic eggs; the Kato-Katz thick smear technique is the standard quantitative method. Eosinophilia may be present during the pulmonary phase.

## Treatment

The World Health Organization recommends single-dose [[Albendazole]] (400 mg) or [[Mebendazole]] (500 mg) as first-line therapy, achieving cure rates above 90%. [[Ivermectin]] (150–200 μg/kg) is also effective, though less active against immature stages. These benzimidazoles bind nematode β-tubulin, inhibiting microtubule polymerization and glucose uptake, leading to parasite immobilization and death. For intestinal obstruction, supportive care and nasogastric decompression are indicated; surgical intervention may be required for perforation or complete obstruction.

## Prevention

Control relies on improved sanitation, hand washing with soap, and thorough washing and cooking of food. Mass drug administration (MDA) programs — annual or biannual single-dose [[Albendazole]] or [[Mebendazole]] — are widely implemented in endemic regions, often integrated with lymphatic filariasis or soil-transmitted helminth (STH) control campaigns. Health education targeting defecation practices and food hygiene reduces environmental contamination.
