# Scabies

Scabies is a highly contagious [[ectoparasite|ectoparasitic]] infestation caused by the mite [[Sarcoptes scabiei]] var. hominis. It is a major global health problem with an estimated 200–300 million cases annually, concentrated in tropical and resource-limited settings, though outbreaks occur worldwide in crowded living conditions.

## Biology

The adult female [[Sarcoptes scabiei]] mite measures approximately 0.3–0.5 mm and is barely visible to the naked eye. After mating on the skin surface, the female burrows into the [[stratum corneum]], creating a serpentine tunnel where she deposits 2–3 eggs per day over her 4–6 week lifespan. Eggs hatch within 3–5 days, releasing larvae that migrate to the skin surface, molt through protonymph and tritonymph stages, and mature into adult mites in approximately 2 weeks. Transmission occurs through prolonged direct skin-to-skin contact; mites can survive off the host for 24–36 hours at room temperature, allowing fomite-mediated spread via bedding and clothing.

## Clinical Presentation

**Classic scabies:** The hallmark symptom is intense [[pruritus]], characteristically worse at night (nocturnal pruritus), resulting from a delayed type IV hypersensitivity reaction to mite products (feces, eggs, saliva). Burrows appear as thin, grayish-white, wavy lines 5–15 mm long, most commonly found in the interdigital webs of the hands, flexor wrists, elbows, axillae, waist, buttocks, genitalia (especially the glans penis in men), and areolae in women. Inflammatory [[papule|papules]], [[nodule|nodules]], and excoriations are common secondary lesions.

**Crusted (Norwegian) scabies:** A severe, hyperinfestation form seen in [[immunocompromised]] patients, the [[elderly]], those with [[HIV]]/[[AIDS]], or individuals with neurological or sensory deficits. Patients harbor thousands to millions of mites, with thick, hyperkeratotic crusts on the hands, feet, scalp, and trunk. This form is highly contagious and can cause institutional outbreaks.

## Diagnosis

Clinical diagnosis is based on characteristic distribution and nocturnal pruritus. Confirmation is obtained via [[dermoscopy]] (showing the "delta wing sign" — the mite's anterior body at the end of a burrow), skin scraping with microscopic identification of mites, eggs, or fecal pellets (scybala), or adhesive tape testing. In resource-limited settings, the burrow ink test may be used.

## Treatment

**First-line:** [[Permethrin]] 5% cream applied from neck to toes, washed off after 8–14 hours; a second application is recommended 1 week later. [[Ivermectin]] 200 µg/kg orally, repeated after 7–14 days, is an effective alternative, particularly for institutional outbreaks or when compliance with topical therapy is poor.

**Special populations:** Precipitated sulfur 6% in petrolatum (applied for 3 consecutive days) is safe for pregnant women, lactating mothers, and infants under 2 months. [[Crotamiton]] 10% cream and [[Benzyl benzoate]] 25% lotion are alternative topical agents.

**Crusted scabies:** Requires combination therapy with topical [[Permethrin]] (extended to scalp and face) plus oral [[Ivermectin]] on days 1, 2, 8, 9, and 15, along with keratolytic agents (e.g., [[salicylic acid]] or [[lactic acid]]) to remove hyperkeratotic crusts and reduce mite burden.

**Post-scabies itch:** Pruritus may persist for 2–4 weeks after successful treatment due to residual hypersensitivity. Antihistamines and emollients provide symptomatic relief; retreatment is indicated only if active mites are identified.
