# Gnathostomiasis

Gnathostomiasis is a zoonotic [[Helminth]] infection caused by larval nematodes of the genus [[Gnathostoma]], most commonly *Gnathostoma spinigerum*. The disease is acquired through ingestion of third-stage larvae encysted in raw or undercooked freshwater fish, eels, frogs, snakes, or poultry. It is endemic in Southeast Asia, particularly Thailand, Japan, and other countries where raw freshwater preparations (e.g., koi pla in Thailand) are traditional. In recent decades, cases have risen in non-endemic regions due to global travel and the international fish trade.

## Life Cycle

Adult *Gnathostoma* worms reside in the wall of the stomach of definitive hosts — domestic cats, dogs, and wild felids — forming tumor-like nodules. Eggs are passed in feces and, upon reaching freshwater, hatch into first-stage larvae, which are ingested by copepods (first intermediate host). Within the copepod, larvae develop into second-stage larvae. When the copepod is eaten by a second intermediate host — freshwater fish, eels, frogs, snakes, or poultry — larvae migrate to muscle tissue and encyst as third-stage larvae. Humans are accidental dead-end hosts, acquiring infection by ingesting raw or undercooked second intermediate hosts containing third-stage larvae. In humans, larvae rarely reach sexual maturity and cannot complete the life cycle; instead, they migrate through tissues and encyst in subcutaneous or visceral sites.

## Clinical Presentation

Gnathostomiasis presents in two major clinical forms: cutaneous and visceral.

**Cutaneous gnathostomiasis** is the most common presentation, characterized by migratory, intermittent, painful or pruritic subcutaneous swellings (creeping eruption or larva currens–like) with surrounding edema and erythema. Lesions typically appear 3–4 weeks after ingestion and can migrate at rates of several centimeters per day as the larva moves through subcutaneous tissue. Swellings may resolve spontaneously but recur at adjacent sites, persisting for months to years if untreated.

**Visceral gnathostomiasis** occurs when larvae penetrate deeper tissues. Migration to the [[Liver]] can cause hepatitis-like pain and hepatomegaly. Ocular involvement produces painful, sight-threatening anterior or posterior chamber inflammation. Central nervous system invasion results in eosinophilic meningitis or meningoencephalitis, with severe headache, radicular pain, cranial nerve palsies, and eosinophilia in cerebrospinal fluid. Pulmonary migration may cause pleuritic chest pain, cough, and hemoptysis. Gastrointestinal tract penetration can lead to perforation, peritonitis, or obstructive symptoms. [[Eosinophilia]] is a prominent laboratory finding in both forms, often exceeding 20% of total leukocyte count.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical presentation of migratory subcutaneous swellings in a patient with relevant dietary travel history to endemic areas. Peripheral blood [[Eosinophilia]] is highly suggestive. Serologic confirmation using Western blot to detect antibodies against *Gnathostoma* antigens is the most specific non-invasive method. Imaging with ultrasound or CT can demonstrate the characteristic serpiginous tract of the larva in subcutaneous tissue or muscle. In eosinophilic meningitis, CSF analysis shows eosinophilic pleocytosis. Definitive diagnosis is made by surgical extraction and identification of the larva, though this is rarely accomplished.

## Treatment

The recommended pharmacotherapy is a combination of [[Ivermectin]] (200 μg/kg/day for 2 days) and [[Albendazole]] (400 mg twice daily for 21 days), which has demonstrated synergistic efficacy against migrating larvae. Surgical excision is curative for accessible subcutaneous or ocular lesions and provides diagnostic confirmation. Corticosteroids may be used adjunctively in eosinophilic meningitis to mitigate inflammatory damage. Prevention centers on thorough cooking of freshwater fish and other potential intermediate hosts; freezing at −20 °C for 7 days also inactivates larvae. Health education to avoid raw freshwater preparations, particularly in endemic regions, remains the primary public health strategy.
