# Cancer

**Cancer** is a large group of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell growth with the potential to invade or spread to other parts of the body. It shares a "meta-hallmark" of epigenetic instability with aging.

## Shared Epigenetic Hallmarks with Aging

- **Global DNA Hypomethylation**: Loss of methyl groups at repetitive sequences ([[LINE-1]], [[Heterochromatin]]), leading to [[Genomic Instability]]
- **Site-Specific Hypermethylation**: [[CpG Island]] hypermethylation silencing tumor suppressors
- **Loss of Histone Marks**: Collapse of heterochromatin architecture

## Hallmarks

Key hallmarks include sustained proliferative signaling, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to [[Apoptosis]], replicative immortality, [[Angiogenesis]], invasion/metastasis, [[Metabolic Reprogramming]] ([[Warburg Effect]]), and immune evasion.

## Signaling Pathways

- [[PI3K]]/[[Akt]]/[[mTOR]]
- [[MAPK]]/ERK
- [[Wnt]]/β-catenin
- [[JAK2]]/[[STAT3]]
- [[NF-κB]]
- [[Hippo Pathway]] ([[YAP1]])

## Therapeutics

Approaches include chemotherapy ([[Cisplatin]], [[docetaxel]]), targeted therapy ([[trastuzumab]]), [[Drug Repositioning]] ([[Ivermectin]], [[Fenbendazole]]), and natural products ([[melittin]]).

[[Epigenetics]] alterations are a fundamental link between **Cancer** and [[Aging]]. While aging is the primary risk factor for cancer, the two processes share a "meta-hallmark" of epigenetic instability that creates a permissive environment for tumorigenesis.


Cancer is a large group of diseases that can start in almost any organ or tissue of the body when abnormal cells grow uncontrollably, go beyond their usual boundaries to invade adjoining parts of the body and/or spread to other organs.

In the context of [[Yamanaka Factors]], research has explored reprogramming cancer cells into pluripotent stem cells to reduce their malignant potential, such as in sarcomas. However, factors like [[c-Myc]] and [[Klf4]] are known oncogenes and can increase the risk of tumorigenesis.


Cancer is a large group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells, often driven by the acquisition of specific [[Hallmarks of Cancer]].

## 1. Shared Epigenetic Hallmarks
Both aging and cancer exhibit a characteristic "epigenetic drift" or erosion of the landscape:

*   **Global DNA Hypomethylation:** A widespread loss of methyl groups, particularly in repetitive DNA sequences (like [[LINE-1]]) and [[Heterochromatin]]. This leads to [[Genomic Instability]] and the reactivation of [[Transposable Elements]].
*   **Site-Specific Hypermethylation:** While the genome loses methylation overall, specific regions—notably [[CpG Island]]s near gene promoters—gain methylation. These often involve genes critical for development and differentiation.
*   **Loss of Histone Proteins & Marks:** A general decline in histone levels and changes in modifications lead to the collapse of heterochromatin architecture and "transcriptional noise."


## 2. The "Hyperaged" Cancer Phenotype
Cancer is often described as having a "hyperaged" epigenetic profile. Research shows that the specific CpG sites that become hypermethylated during normal aging are frequently the same sites that are hypermethylated in tumors. This suggests that aging creates an "epigenetic field effect" or a pre-malignant state that lowers the barrier for cancer-driving mutations (oncogenes) to take hold and for tumor suppressors to be silenced.


## 3. The Aging-Cancer Relationship
While they share many features, some hallmarks of aging act in opposition to cancer:

*   **Telomere Attrition:** A hallmark of aging that limits cell lifespan. It prevents cancer by inducing senescence; cancer cells must bypass this (e.g., via telomerase reactivation) to become immortal.
*   **[[Cellular Senescence]]:** Initially acts as a tumor-suppressor mechanism by stopping the growth of damaged cells. However, the chronic presence of senescent cells and their secretion of pro-inflammatory factors ([[SASP]]) can later promote tumor growth in the surrounding tissue.
*   **[[Epigenetic Clock]]s:** Tumors typically show significantly higher "epigenetic ages" than the chronological age of the patient, reflecting their rapid and disorganized cellular proliferation.


## 4. Summary of Mechanisms
*   **Aging:** Epigenetic changes are often stochastic and lead to a loss of cellular identity and function.
*   **Cancer:** These changes become "locked in" and selected for, providing the plasticity needed for cells to survive, proliferate, and evade the immune system.

In essence, **aging provides the epigenetic "soil"** (silencing of protective genes, loss of chromatin integrity) that allows the **"seeds" of cancer** (genetic mutations) to grow more effectively.


## Overview
Cancer development is a complex multi-step process involving genetic mutations and epigenetic changes that lead to dysregulated cellular functions. Key biological processes involved in cancer progression include [[Angiogenesis]], which provides blood supply to tumors, and the evasion of programmed cell death mechanisms such as [[Apoptosis]], [[Autophagy]], and [[Pyroptosis]].


## Pathophysiology
Tumor cells often undergo [[Metabolic Reprogramming]] to support rapid proliferation, a phenomenon known as the [[Warburg Effect]], which is characterized by increased [[Glycolysis]] even in the presence of oxygen. This metabolic shift involves the upregulation of transporters like [[GLUT1]] and enzymes such as [[Hexokinase 2]] and [[Pyruvate Kinase M2]]. The transition from a sedentary to a migratory phenotype is often facilitated by [[EMT]] (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition), enabling metastasis.


## Signaling Pathways
Several signaling pathways are frequently dysregulated in cancer, including:
- [[PI3K]] / [[Akt]] / [[mTOR]] pathway
- [[MAPK]] / ERK pathway
- [[Wnt]] signaling
- [[JAK2]] / [[STAT3]] signaling
- [[Hippo Pathway]] (involving [[YAP1]])
- [[NFκB]] signaling
- [[Integrins]] and [[FAK]] (Focal Adhesion Kinase) signaling


## Genetic and Molecular Drivers
Key molecular drivers include oncogenes like [[EGFR]], [[HER2]], and [[MYC]], as well as tumor suppressor genes such as [[p53]] and [[BRCA]]. Elevated levels of markers like [[Ki-67]] often indicate high proliferative activity. Furthermore, the evasion of the immune system can be mediated by the expression of [[PD-L1]].

## Therapeutics and Research
Treatment strategies include traditional [[Cisplatin]] and [[docetaxel]], as well as targeted therapies like [[trastuzumab]]. Recent research has explored [[Drug Repositioning]], investigating the anticancer potential of antiparasitic agents such as [[Ivermectin]] and the [[Benzimidazole]] class (e.g., [[Fenbendazole]], [[Mebendazole]], [[Albendazole]], and [[Oxfendazole]]). Natural products like [[Honeybee venom]] and its active component [[melittin]] are also under investigation for their ability to suppress growth factor receptors.

One of the significant challenges in treatment is [[Multidrug Resistance]], which often leads to treatment failure and recurrence.
