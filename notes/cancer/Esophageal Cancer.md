# Esophageal Cancer

Esophageal cancer is a malignant neoplasm of the esophagus, the muscular tube connecting the pharynx to the stomach. It is the 9th most common cancer worldwide, with approximately 600,000 new cases and 540,000 deaths per year. Incidence shows striking geographic variation, reflecting the distinct epidemiological profiles of its two dominant histological subtypes: **squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)** and **adenocarcinoma**. Esophageal cancer carries a poor prognosis, with an overall 5-year survival rate of approximately 20%, though outcomes improve substantially when disease is detected early.

## Squamous Cell Carcinoma

Esophageal SCC arises from the squamous epithelium lining the esophageal mucosa. It predominates in the upper and middle third of the esophagus. Globally, SCC remains the most common histological type, though its relative incidence has declined in Western nations.

**Epidemiology:** SCC is most prevalent in Asia, Eastern and Southern Africa, and parts of South America. High-incidence regions include the "esophageal cancer belt" stretching from Iran through Central Asia to Northern China. In these areas, age-standardized incidence rates may exceed 100 per 100,000.

**Risk factors:** Tobacco smoking and heavy alcohol consumption are the principal risk factors, acting synergistically. Additional contributors include consumption of very hot beverages (thermal injury to the mucosa), betel quid chewing, infection with [[human papillomavirus]] (HPV), chronic esophageal irritation from lye strictures or achalasia, nutritional deficiencies (zinc, selenium), and dietary exposure to [[nitrosamines]] and [[mycotoxins]]. [[Plummer-Vinson syndrome]] (iron deficiency anemia, dysphagia, and upper esophageal webs) also predisposes to SCC.

## Adenocarcinoma

Esophageal adenocarcinoma arises from [[Barrett's Esophagus|Barrett's Esophagus]], a premalignant condition in which the normal squamous epithelium is replaced by metaplastic columnar epithelium resembling intestinal mucosa. Adenocarcinoma overwhelmingly develops in the lower third of the esophagus and at the esophagogastric junction.

**Epidemiology:** Adenocarcinoma incidence has increased dramatically over the past four decades in Western countries, particularly among white men in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Western Europe. It now surpasses SCC in these populations.

**Risk factors:** [[GERD|Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)]] is the primary driver, leading to chronic acid and bile exposure of the distal esophagus. The progression from GERD to [[Barrett's Esophagus]] to low-grade dysplasia, high-grade dysplasia, and invasive adenocarcinoma represents the canonical carcinogenic sequence. [[Obesity]] is an independent risk factor, acting through increased intra-abdominal pressure, reflux promotion, and systemic inflammatory and metabolic alterations. Tobacco smoking confers a moderate risk, while [[Helicobacter pylori]] infection (particularly CagA+ strains) appears protective, possibly through reduced acid secretion. Alcohol is not a major risk factor for adenocarcinoma.

## Clinical Presentation

Dysphagia — the sensation of food sticking on swallowing — is the most common presenting symptom and usually indicates advanced, obstructing disease. It follows a progressive course, beginning with difficulty swallowing solids and advancing to liquids. **Odynophagia** (painful swallowing), **weight loss** (often marked), and **hoarseness** (from recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement) are frequent accompaniments. Patients may also report regurgitation, chronic cough, or aspiration pneumonia from tracheoesophageal fistula. [[Hematemesis]] and [[melena]] are late signs and suggest tumor erosion into a major vessel or the lumen. Many patients already have locally advanced or metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis.

## Staging and Treatment

Staging relies on **endoscopic ultrasound** (EUS) for T and N staging, combined with CT chest/abdomen and **PET-CT** for detection of occult metastases.

**Early-stage disease (T1a):** Superficial tumors confined to the mucosa (lamina propria) can be managed with **endoscopic resection** (endoscopic mucosal resection, EMR, or endoscopic submucosal dissection, ESD), with or without ablative therapy for concomitant [[Barrett's Esophagus|Barrett's esophagus]].

**Locally advanced disease (T1b–T2 N0):** Standard treatment is **esophagectomy** — either the **Ivor Lewis** (laparotomy + right thoracotomy) or **McKeown** (right thoracotomy + laparotomy + neck incision) approach. Lymphadenectomy (en bloc two- or three-field) is integral for accurate staging and local control.

**Locoregionally advanced disease (T3+ or N+):** The standard of care for resectable disease is **neoadjuvant chemoradiation** followed by surgery, based on the landmark **CROSS trial**. The CROSS regimen consists of carboplatin (AUC 2) and paclitaxel (50 mg/m²) weekly for 5 weeks with concurrent radiotherapy (41.4 Gy in 23 fractions). This approach achieves an R0 resection rate of ~92% and a pathologic complete response rate of ~29% for adenocarcinoma and ~49% for SCC, with a significant overall survival benefit.

**Metastatic disease (M1):** Palliative systemic therapy is the mainstay. Platinum-fluoropyrimidine doublets ([[5-FU]]/[[cisplatin]], [[FOLFOX]], [[FOLFIRI]]) form the backbone. For patients with **PD-L1** expression (combined positive score ≥10), the addition of [[immune checkpoint inhibitors]] — [[pembrolizumab]] or [[nivolumab]] — improves survival. In **HER2-positive adenocarcinoma** (approximately 15–20% of cases), [[trastuzumab]] combined with chemotherapy is the first-line standard. [[Trastuzumab deruxtecan]] is an option in later lines.

## Prognosis

Overall 5-year survival is approximately 20% across all stages. For early-stage tumors amenable to endoscopic resection, survival exceeds 80%. For locally advanced disease treated with multimodality therapy, 5-year survival is 40–60% in patients who achieve a pathologic complete response. Once distant metastases are present, 5-year survival falls below 5%, with median survival of 8–12 months. Poor prognostic factors include nodal involvement, higher T stage, weight loss >10%, and poor performance status.

## Connections

- [[XIAP]] — XIAP overexpression in esophageal cancer is associated with chemoresistance and poor prognosis; targeting XIAP sensitizes cells to cisplatin-induced apoptosis.
- [[Apoptosis]] — Evasion of apoptosis is a hallmark of esophageal cancer; dysregulation of both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor) pathways contributes to tumor progression and treatment resistance.
- [[Caspase-2]] — Caspase-2 has been implicated in DNA damage-induced apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells and may modulate response to genotoxic chemotherapy.
- [[Barrett's Esophagus]] — The premalignant precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma; progression from metaplasia to dysplasia to carcinoma defines the stepwise carcinogenic cascade.
- [[GERD]] — Chronic gastroesophageal reflux is the primary etiological driver of Barrett's esophagus and subsequent adenocarcinoma risk.
