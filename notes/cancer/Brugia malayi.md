# Brugia malayi

## Overview

*Brugia malayi* is a filarial nematode (roundworm) and one of the three causative agents of [[Lymphatic Filariasis]] in humans, alongside *[[Wuchereria bancrofti]]* and *[[Brugia timori]]*. It is endemic primarily in South and Southeast Asia, including parts of India, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam. The parasite is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected mosquitoes, predominantly of the genera *[[Mosquito|Mansonia]]* and *[[Mosquito|Anopheles]]*. Lymphatic filariasis caused by *B. malayi* represents a significant public health burden in affected regions, leading to chronic morbidity, disability, and social stigma.

## Biology

Adult *B. malayi* worms reside in the lymphatic vessels and lymph nodes of the human host. The females are significantly larger than males, measuring approximately 55–80 mm in length and 0.16–0.28 mm in width, whereas males are 22–23 mm long and 0.09–0.11 mm wide. Both sexes possess a long, slender, thread-like morphology characteristic of filarial worms. The adult worms are white to cream-colored and exhibit a smooth cuticle.

The females are viviparous, producing large numbers of microfilariae — the first-stage larvae — that are shed into the lymphatic system and subsequently enter the peripheral bloodstream. *B. malayi* microfilariae exhibit a marked nocturnal periodicity, peaking in the peripheral blood between 10 p.m. and 2 a.m., a behavioral adaptation that synchronizes with the feeding activity of their mosquito vectors. The microfilariae are sheathed and measure approximately 200–275 μm in length.

During development within the mosquito vector, the parasite undergoes three molts: from the ingested microfilaria (L1) to the first-stage larva, then to the second-stage larva (L2), and finally to the third-stage infective larva (L3). This developmental cycle within the mosquito typically requires 10–14 days, depending on environmental temperature and humidity.

## Life Cycle

The life cycle of *B. malayi* alternates between the human definitive host and the mosquito intermediate host. When a female mosquito takes a blood meal from an infected human, it ingests microfilariae circulating in the blood. Within the mosquito, the microfilariae penetrate the midgut wall and migrate to the thoracic flight muscles, where they develop through L1 and L2 stages before reaching the infective L3 stage.

When the infected mosquito takes a subsequent blood meal, the L3 larvae are deposited onto the human skin near the puncture wound. The larvae actively penetrate the skin through the bite wound, enter the subcutaneous tissues, and migrate to the lymphatic vessels. Over a period of 3–12 months, the L3 larvae molt twice — first to L4 and then to immature adults — before reaching sexual maturity. Adult worms mate within the lymphatic system, and gravid females begin producing microfilariae that enter the bloodstream, completing the cycle.

The prepatent period (time from infection to detectable microfilariae in blood) is approximately 3–6 months in *B. malayi* infections, shorter than the 9–12 months typical of *W. bancrofti*. Adult worms have a lifespan of 5–10 years, though microfilariae survive only 6–12 months in the blood.

## Disease

*B. malayi* causes [[Lymphatic Filariasis]], a chronic parasitic disease characterized by a spectrum of clinical manifestations. Acute disease presents as recurrent episodes of [[Fever]], filarial lymphangitis (inflammation of lymphatic vessels), and lymphadenitis, often accompanied by pain and swelling of the affected limb. Chronic complications arise from long-term lymphatic damage and include [[Lymphedema]] (tissue swelling due to impaired lymphatic drainage), [[Elephantiasis]] (severe, disfiguring swelling and thickening of the skin and underlying tissues, typically of the legs), and hydrocele (fluid accumulation in the scrotum). Unlike *W. bancrofti*, *B. malayi* rarely causes hydrocele or chyluria, and its chronic manifestations tend to be restricted to the lower limbs.

Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia, a hypersensitivity reaction to filarial antigens, can occur in infected individuals and presents with nocturnal cough, wheezing, dyspnea, and marked peripheral eosinophilia.

Diagnosis is confirmed by the microscopic detection of sheathed microfilariae in peripheral blood smears, ideally collected at night (10 p.m. to 2 a.m.) to coincide with peak periodicity. [[PCR|Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)]]-based assays and serological tests for filarial antigens provide greater sensitivity.

The cornerstone of treatment is [[Diethylcarbamazine]] (DEC), which rapidly kills both microfilariae and adult worms. Mass drug administration (MDA) programs, coordinated by the World Health Organization, typically use annual single-dose combinations of [[Ivermectin]] plus [[Albendazole]] (in areas co-endemic for onchocerciasis) or DEC plus albendazole to interrupt transmission. Morbidity management focuses on hygiene, limb elevation, compression bandaging, and surgical intervention for advanced elephantiasis or hydrocele.
