# Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is the reversible covalent addition of a phosphate group (PO₄³⁻) to a substrate, most commonly a protein. It is the most abundant [[Post-translational modification]], with an estimated one-third of all human proteins being phosphorylated at some point. The reaction is catalyzed by [[Kinase|kinases]] (~518 encoded in the human kinome) and reversed by [[Phosphatase|phosphatases]]. Phosphorylation occurs predominantly on [[Serine]] and [[Threonine]] residues (~90% of phosphosites), with [[Tyrosine]] accounting for ~10%, and more rarely on [[Histidine]], [[Aspartate]], [[Lysine]], and [[Cysteine]].

## Chemistry

The phosphorylation reaction involves nucleophilic attack of the hydroxyl oxygen (‑OH) of a Ser, Thr, or Tyr side chain on the γ-phosphate of [[ATP]] (or [[GTP]]), forming a phosphoester bond. This transfers the high-energy phosphoanhydride bond energy to the protein, making the modification thermodynamically favorable (ΔG ≈ −30 kJ/mol). The introduction of a phosphate group adds two negative charges at physiological pH, which can induce electrostatic repulsion or attraction with neighboring residues, triggering conformational changes that alter enzyme activity, substrate binding, or protein-protein interaction surfaces.

## Functions

Phosphorylation regulates virtually every aspect of protein function:

- **Enzyme activation/inactivation** — Phosphorylation at active-site loops or allosteric sites can switch enzymes between active and inactive conformations (e.g., [[Glycogen phosphorylase]] activation by [[Phosphorylase kinase]]).
- **Protein localization** — Phosphorylation can expose or mask nuclear localization signals (NLS) or membrane-targeting motifs, controlling subcellular trafficking.
- **Protein-protein interactions** — Phosphotyrosine motifs (pYXXP) serve as docking sites for SH2-domain-containing adaptor proteins, while phosphoserine/threonine motifs bind 14-3-3, WD40, or FHA domains.
- **[[Signal transduction]]** — Phosphorylation cascades amplify and propagate extracellular signals through the [[MAPK]], [[PI3K]]/[[AKT]], and [[JAK]]/[[STAT]] pathways.
- **[[Cell cycle]] regulation** — Cyclin-dependent kinases ([[CDK]]) paired with cyclins drive ordered progression through G1, S, G2, and M phases via timed phosphorylation of substrates.
- **Gene expression** — [[Transcription factor]] phosphorylation (e.g., [[CREB]] at Ser133, [[p53]] at multiple sites) modulates DNA binding, coactivator recruitment, and transcriptional output.
- **Protein stability** — Phosphorylation can target substrates for [[Ubiquitination]] by creating phosphodegrons recognized by E3 ubiquitin ligases (e.g., [[SCF]]-[[FBXW7]] recognition of phosphorylated [[Cyclin E]]).

## Signaling Cascades

Phosphorylation lies at the heart of canonical signaling networks:

- **[[MAPK]] cascade** — [[Receptor tyrosine kinase]] ([[RTK]]) activation at the membrane recruits [[GRB2]] and [[SOS]], which load [[RAS]] with GTP. RAS-GTP activates [[RAF]] (a MAP3K), which phosphorylates [[MEK]] (a MAP2K), which phosphorylates [[ERK]] (a MAPK). ERK then phosphorylates cytoplasmic targets and translocates to the nucleus to regulate transcription factors such as [[ELK1]], [[c-Fos]], and [[c-Jun]].
- **[[PI3K]]/[[AKT]]/[[mTORC1]] pathway** — RTK or GPCR activation recruits [[PI3K]], which generates [[PIP3]] at the membrane. [[PIP3]] recruits [[AKT]] via its [[PH domain]]; [[PDK1]] and [[mTORC2]] phosphorylate AKT at Thr308 and Ser473, respectively. Active AKT phosphorylates [[TSC2]], relieving inhibition of [[RHEB]] and activating [[mTORC1]], which drives [[Protein synthesis]], [[Lipid synthesis]], and [[Autophagy]] suppression.
- **[[PKA]]/[[CREB]]** — [[cAMP]] elevation activates [[PKA]], which phosphorylates [[CREB]] at Ser133, recruiting [[CBP]]/[[p300]] coactivators to drive transcription of genes containing [[cAMP response elements]] (CRE).
- **[[JAK]]/[[STAT]]** — Cytokine receptors associate with [[JAKs]] that trans-phosphorylate each other and the receptor tail, creating docking sites for [[STAT]] transcription factors. JAKs then phosphorylate STATs, triggering their dimerization and nuclear translocation.

## Pathology

Dysregulated phosphorylation is a hallmark of many human diseases:

- **Cancer** — Constitutive activation of kinases via mutation or amplification drives uncontrolled proliferation. Examples include [[EGFR]] (lung cancer, glioblastoma), [[BRAF V600E]] (melanoma), [[PIK3CA]] (breast, colorectal cancer), and [[SRC]] (multiple cancers). Conversely, loss-of-function mutations in phosphatases (e.g., [[PTEN]]) sustain pathway activation. This has driven the development of [[Kinase inhibitor|kinase inhibitors]] as targeted therapeutics, including [[Imatinib]] ([[BCR-ABL]] in CML), [[Dasatinib]] (SRC/ABL), [[Erlotinib]] (EGFR), [[Trametinib]] (MEK), and [[Sunitinib]] (VEGFR/PDGFR).
- **[[Alzheimer's Disease]]** — Hyperphosphorylation of [[Tau]] protein at >40 serine/threonine sites promotes aggregation into paired helical filaments and neurofibrillary tangles, a defining pathological feature.
- **Diabetes** — Defective insulin signaling involves impaired phosphorylation of [[IRS1]] and [[AKT]], contributing to insulin resistance.
- **Inflammatory and autoimmune disorders** — Hyperactive [[JAK]]-[[STAT]] signaling underlies many autoimmune conditions, leading to clinical use of [[JAK inhibitors]] such as [[Tofacitinib]] and [[Baricitinib]].
