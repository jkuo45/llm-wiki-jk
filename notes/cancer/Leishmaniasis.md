# Leishmaniasis

**Leishmaniasis** is a vector-borne parasitic disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus [[Leishmania]], transmitted through the bite of infected female phlebotomine [[Sandfly|sandflies]]. The disease presents in three major clinical forms: visceral leishmaniasis (VL, Kala-azar), cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL). Approximately 50,000–90,000 new cases of VL and up to 1 million new cases of CL occur annually worldwide, with over 350 million people at risk across tropical and subtropical regions, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and parts of Central and South America.

## Forms

### Visceral Leishmaniasis (Kala-azar)

Visceral leishmaniasis is the most severe form, caused primarily by *[[Leishmania donovani]]* in the Indian subcontinent and East Africa, and *[[Leishmania infantum]]* (syn. *Leishmania chagasi*) in the Mediterranean, Middle East, and Latin America. After an incubation period of weeks to months (typically 2–6 months), patients develop persistent fever, marked [[Hepatosplenomegaly|hepatosplenomegaly]], pancytopenia (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), weight loss, and progressive wasting. Hyperpigmentation of the skin, from which the term "Kala-azar" (Hindi for "black fever") derives, may appear in advanced cases. If left untreated, VL is almost universally fatal, often due to secondary infections or hemorrhage. In immunocompromised individuals (e.g., HIV co-infection), reactivation and atypical presentations are common.

### Cutaneous Leishmaniasis

Cutaneous leishmaniasis is the most prevalent form, caused by species including *[[Leishmania major]]*, *[[Leishmania tropica]]*, *[[Leishmania mexicana]]*, and *[[Leishmania aethiopica]]*. Following an incubation period of weeks to months, a painless papule develops at the bite site, progressively enlarging into an ulcerated nodule with raised margins — the classic "oriental sore." Lesions may be single or multiple, typically heal spontaneously over months to years but leave depressed scars. Chronic or recidivans forms (leishmaniasis recidivans) can persist, particularly with *L. tropica* infection.

### Mucocutaneous Leishmaniasis

Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, primarily caused by *[[Leishmania braziliensis]]* in Latin America, occurs when parasites disseminate from the initial cutaneous lesion to the nasopharyngeal mucosa, months to years after the primary ulcer heals. Progressive destruction of the nasal septum, palate, pharynx, and larynx leads to severe disfigurement, nasal perforation, and functional impairment. MCL does not heal spontaneously and requires systemic therapy to halt progression.

## Life Cycle

The [[Leishmania]] life cycle involves two hosts: sandfly vectors and mammalian hosts. When a female sandfly feeds on an infected host, it ingests macrophages containing amastigotes. In the sandfly midgut, amastigotes transform into flagellated promastigotes, which multiply, differentiate into highly motile metacyclic promastigotes, and migrate to the proboscis. Upon a subsequent blood meal, the sandfly injects metacyclic promastigotes into the dermis. These are rapidly phagocytosed by resident [[Macrophage|macrophages]] and other phagocytic cells. Inside the macrophage, promastigotes transform into round, non-flagellated amastigotes within a parasitophorous vacuole (parasitophorous vacuole), where they multiply by binary fission, eventually rupturing the host cell to infect neighboring macrophages.

## Diagnosis

Microscopic identification of amastigotes (2–4 μm, round or oval with a distinct nucleus and kinetoplast) remains the gold standard. In VL, amastigotes are visualized in Giemsa-stained aspirates from bone marrow, spleen (high sensitivity but risk of hemorrhage), or lymph nodes. In CL, tissue smears or biopsy specimens from the ulcer margin or base are examined. [[Culture]] of aspirates using Novy–MacNeal–Nicollé (NNN) medium or Schneider's insect medium can confirm the species. Serological testing with the rK39 rapid immunochromatographic test is widely used for VL diagnosis in endemic areas, demonstrating high sensitivity for *L. donovani* infection. [[Polymerase chain reaction|PCR-based assays]] offer high sensitivity and species identification, particularly valuable in MCL and in differentiating relapse from reinfection.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the clinical form, causative species, and geographic region.

Visceral leishmaniasis: [[Liposomal amphotericin B]] (AmBisome) is the first-line therapy in most settings, with high efficacy and a favorable safety profile. Pentavalent antimonials (sodium stibogluconate, meglumine antimoniate) remain widely used despite cardiac and hepatic toxicity. [[Miltefosine]], the only oral agent, is effective in the Indian subcontinent but teratogenic and increasingly associated with resistance. [[Paromomycin]] (intramuscular) is used in combination regimens for VL in East Africa.

Cutaneous leishmaniasis: Uncomplicated lesions may heal spontaneously. Local therapies include topical paromomycin, intralesional antimonials, cryotherapy, and thermotherapy. Systemic therapy (oral miltefosine, fluconazole, or intravenous antimonials/liposomal amphotericin B) is indicated for multiple, large, or chronic lesions and for species with mucosal tropism (e.g., *L. braziliensis*, *L. panamensis*).

Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis requires prolonged systemic therapy with liposomal amphotericin B or pentavalent antimonials; miltefosine and [[Pentamidine]] are alternative options.

## Prevention

No licensed vaccine exists. Prevention relies on vector control: indoor residual spraying, insecticide-treated bed nets, and environmental management to reduce sandfly breeding sites. In zoonotic VL (Mediterranean, Latin America), reservoir control (e.g., seropositive dogs) is attempted. Early diagnosis and treatment of human cases reduce transmission. Personal protective measures include insect repellent and long-sleeved clothing during peak sandfly activity (dusk to dawn).
