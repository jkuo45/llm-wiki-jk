# Ascaris

**Ascaris** is a genus of large parasitic roundworms belonging to the phylum [[Nematode|Nematoda]]. Adult worms measure 15–30 cm in length, making them the largest and most common intestinal roundworm parasites of humans and pigs. The principal species are *Ascaris lumbricoides* (infecting humans) and *Ascaris suum* (infecting pigs), though cross-species transmission occurs. An estimated 800 million to 1 billion people are infected globally, predominantly in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation.

## Biology

Adult *Ascaris* worms are large, round, and pinkish-white to yellow in color. Males are smaller (15–25 cm) with a characteristic coiled or curved posterior tail, while females are larger (20–35 cm) with a straight, blunt posterior. Females are exceptionally fecund — a single female can lay up to **200,000 fertilized eggs per day**, which are shed in the host's feces. The eggs are extremely durable, with a thick, three-layered shell (the outer mammillated layer is characteristic) that allows them to survive for years in moist soil, resistant to desiccation, freezing, and many disinfectants. The larvae undergo two molts inside the egg, reaching the infective L3 (third-stage) larval stage before hatching.

## Life Cycle

The *Ascaris* life cycle is direct and monoxenous (one host). Infective embryonated eggs containing L3 larvae are ingested via contaminated food, water, or fomites. Once in the small intestine, larvae hatch, penetrate the intestinal wall, and enter the portal circulation. They travel through the liver (arriving ~2–3 days post-infection) and reach the heart and lungs (days 4–7). In the pulmonary capillaries, larvae break into the alveolar spaces, where they molt twice (L3→L4, L4→L5/immature adult). This tissue-migration phase lasts 10–14 days. The immature worms ascend the bronchial tree to the pharynx, are coughed up and swallowed, and return to the small intestine. There they mature into dioecious adults over 6–10 weeks. Adult worms live 1–2 years, copulate, and females release eggs into the stool, completing the cycle. The total prepatent period (infection to egg production) is approximately 60–75 days.

## Disease

*Ascaris* infection ([[Ascariasis]]) presents with clinical manifestations that depend on worm burden and migratory phase:

- **Intestinal ascariasis** — the most common form. Light infections are often asymptomatic. Moderate-to-heavy burdens cause malnutrition, impaired growth in children, abdominal distension, colicky pain, and occasionally **intestinal obstruction** (particularly in children with high worm burdens). Worms can migrate into the appendix, bile duct, or pancreatic duct.
- **Pulmonary ascariasis** — during larval migration through the lungs (days 7–15 post-infection), patients may develop **eosinophilic pneumonia** (Löffler syndrome): cough, dyspnea, wheezing, fever, and transient pulmonary infiltrates with peripheral eosinophilia. Symptoms are usually self-limited and resolve within 1–2 weeks.
- **Hepatobiliary and pancreatic** — adult worms migrating into the common bile duct cause biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, and acute pancreatitis.
- **Nutritional effects** — worms compete for host nutrients and can cause vitamin A and protein deficiency; chronic infection is associated with reduced cognitive development in children.

Treatment is with [[Albendazole]] (400 mg single dose) or [[Ivermectin]]; mebendazole and pyrantel pamoate are alternatives. Mass drug administration programs in endemic regions remain the cornerstone of public health control.
