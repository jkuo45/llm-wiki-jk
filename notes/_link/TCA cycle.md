## Overview

The [[TCA cycle]] (tricarboxylic acid cycle, or Krebs cycle) is a series of eight enzyme-catalyzed reactions in the mitochondrial matrix that oxidize acetyl-CoA derived from carbohydrates, fatty acids, and amino acids. It is the central hub of cellular [[Metabolism]], linking catabolism to the electron transport chain via reducing equivalents.

## Biological Role / Mechanism

Each turn of the cycle:

- Condenses acetyl-CoA with oxaloacetate to form citrate.
- Releases two CO2, regenerates oxaloacetate, and produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 GTP (or ATP).
- Feeds NADH/FADH2 into [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]] for ATP generation.

Key enzymes include citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, succinyl-CoA synthetase, succinate dehydrogenase (Complex II), fumarase, and malate dehydrogenase. The cycle is tightly controlled by [[Allosteric Regulation]] and product inhibition (e.g., NADH, ATP) and activated by ADP and Ca2+ in energy-demanding states.

## Physiological / Pathological Relevance

TCA intermediates ("anaplerosis/cataplerosis") feed biosynthesis (heme, amino acids, neurotransmitters). Cycle dysfunction underlies metabolic and mitochondrial diseases, and multiple TCA enzymes are tumor suppressors or oncogenes (e.g., [[SDH]], FH, IDH mutations). In the wiki, the TCA cycle is central to sirtuin-mediated metabolic regulation and stress resilience, intersecting with [[Mitochondrial Uncoupling]] and redox balance.
