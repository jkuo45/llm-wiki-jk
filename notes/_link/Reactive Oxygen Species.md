# Reactive Oxygen Species

**Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)** are highly reactive molecules containing oxygen, formed as natural byproducts of oxygen metabolism. They play dual roles as signaling molecules and agents of oxidative damage.


[[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species]] (ROS) are highly reactive chemicals formed from oxygen. In biological systems, ROS are produced as byproducts of normal metabolism but can also be induced by external stressors, such as viral infection.


## Types of ROS
- [[Superoxide anion]] (O₂⁻•): Primary ROS produced by mitochondria or enzymes like NADPH oxidase.
- [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide]] (H₂O₂): Stable signaling molecule, can diffuse across membranes; byproduct of [[MAO]] activity.
- [[Hydroxyl radical]] (•OH): Extremely reactive and damaging; formed from H₂O₂ via the Fenton reaction.


## Role in [[Adrenochrome]] Formation
ROS drive the non-enzymatic [[Oxidation]] of [[notes/_link/Epinephrine]] to [[Adrenochrome]]. This process is often part of a [[Redox Cycling]] loop where Adrenochrome itself generates further [[Superoxide anion]].


## Sources
- **Endogenous:** Mitochondria, activated [[notes/_link/Neutrophils]] (respiratory burst), peroxisomes, and enzymes like [[notes/_link/Myeloperoxidase]].
- **Exogenous:** [[Smoking]], pollution, UV radiation.


## Biological Impact
At low levels, ROS are essential for cell signaling and immune defense. However, when levels exceed the body's antioxidant capacity, they lead to [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]], causing damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA. This process is central to the pathogenesis of [[notes/_link/Parkinson's Disease]] and other [[notes/_link/Neurodegenerative Diseases]].

## Definition
Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) are chemically reactive chemical species containing oxygen. Examples include [[Superoxide Radicals]], [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide]], and [[Hydroxyl Radicals]].


## Physiological Role
At low levels, ROS serve as important signaling molecules in processes like [[notes/_link/Apoptosis]] and [[Immunity]].


## Pathological Role
At high levels, they cause [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]] and damage cellular structures.


## Key Species
- [[Superoxide Radicals]] (O2•−)
- [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide]] (H2O2)
- [[Hydroxyl Radicals]] (•OH)
- [[Singlet Oxygen]] (1O2)


## Role in Cellular Reprogramming
During the early stages of [[Cellular Reprogramming]] via viral transduction of [[OSKM]], levels of ROS significantly increase. Elevated ROS can:
- Cause oxidative [[notes/_link/DNA Damage]].
- Trigger the activation of [[notes/_link/p53]].
- Promote [[notes/_link/Apoptosis]] and [[notes/_link/Senescence]].
- Enhance the differentiation of [[Embryonic Stem Cells]], thereby antagonizing the induction of [[Pluripotency]].


## Mitigation Strategies
The use of ROS scavengers (antioxidants like Vitamin C) and hypoxic growth conditions have been shown to improve the efficiency of inducing pluripotency by buffering ROS-induced damage and preventing premature differentiation.


## Connections
- [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]] ↔ Condition resulting from an imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defenses.
- [[Adrenochrome]] ↔ Formed via ROS-mediated oxidation of adrenaline.
- [[notes/_link/Neutrophils]] ↔ Major source of ROS during [[notes/_link/Inflammation]] and the respiratory burst.
- [[Redox Cycling]] ↔ Process that catalytically generates ROS using molecules like [[Aminochromes]].


- [[Cellular Reprogramming]]: High ROS levels are a major roadblock in the reprogramming process.
- [[Innate Immunity]]: Viral-induced innate immune responses are a primary source of ROS during reprogramming.
- [[notes/_link/DNA Damage]]: A direct consequence of elevated ROS levels.
- [[notes/_link/p53]]: Activated as a response to ROS-induced stress and DNA damage.
