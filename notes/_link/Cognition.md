# Cognition

Cognition encompasses the full suite of mental processes by which organisms acquire, process, store, and use information about themselves and the world. These processes include perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making. The neural architecture supporting cognition is distributed across cortical and subcortical regions: the [[Prefrontal Cortex]] mediates executive function and working memory; the [[Hippocampus]] is essential for memory formation and spatial navigation; the temporal cortex supports language comprehension and semantic knowledge; the parietal cortex integrates attention and visuospatial processing; and the occipital cortex handles visual input. The [[Cerebellum]], once considered purely motor, is now recognized as contributing to procedural memory, timing, and cognitive coordination. The emergent property of these distributed networks is a flexible, adaptive system capable of abstract thought, planning, and self-regulation.

## Neural Substrates

Large-scale brain networks coordinate cognitive operations. The default mode network (DMN)—spanning medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate, and angular gyrus—is active during self-referential thought, autobiographical memory retrieval, and mind-wandering. The central executive network (CEN), centered on dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and posterior parietal regions, supports working memory, goal-directed attention, and reasoning. The salience network (SN), anchored by the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex, detects behaviorally relevant stimuli and switches between DMN and CEN states. Dynamic coupling between these networks underlies the fluidity of normal cognition; disrupted network interaction is observed in [[Alzheimer's Disease]], [[Depression]], and schizophrenia.

Neurotransmitter systems modulate cognitive performance across multiple domains. [[Dopamine]] is critical for reward-based learning, motivation, and working memory—the prefrontal cortex operates within an inverted-U dose-response curve for dopaminergic signaling, with both excess and deficiency impairing performance. Acetylcholine, released from basal forebrain nuclei (nucleus basalis of Meynert), enhances cortical attention and memory encoding; degeneration of these cholinergic neurons is a hallmark of [[Alzheimer's Disease]]. [[Serotonin]] modulates mood, impulse control, and aspects of cognitive flexibility. Glutamate, the principal excitatory neurotransmitter, drives long-term potentiation (LTP) at hippocampal and cortical synapses, the cellular substrate of learning. GABA, the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter, shapes neural oscillations (gamma, theta rhythms) essential for working memory maintenance and attentional selection.

## Cognitive Domains

Memory encompasses several distinct systems. Episodic memory—the conscious recollection of specific events—depends on hippocampal-cortical circuits and declines early in [[Alzheimer's Disease]]. Semantic memory, the storage of general knowledge and facts, is distributed across temporal and frontal cortices. Procedural memory (skills and habits) relies on [[Cerebellum]] and basal ganglia circuits and is relatively preserved in early neurodegeneration. Working memory, the capacity to hold and manipulate information over short delays, is subserved by persistent firing in prefrontal networks and is sensitive to [[Dopamine]] and [[BDNF]] signaling.

Attention comprises sustained vigilance (maintaining focus over time), selective filtering (prioritizing relevant stimuli), and divided attention (multitasking). These functions depend on frontoparietal networks and neuromodulatory input from locus coeruleus (norepinephrine) and basal forebrain (acetylcholine). Executive function—inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, planning, and abstract reasoning—is the hallmark of prefrontal cortical maturation and is among the first domains affected in normal aging and many neurodegenerative conditions. Language involves comprehension (Wernicke's area in the superior temporal gyrus) and production (Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus), connected by the arcuate fasciculus. Visuospatial processing depends on the dorsal visual stream (parieto-occipital) and is notably impaired in [[Parkinson's Disease]] dementia and posterior cortical atrophy.

## Physiological Function

Learning and memory consolidation depend on synaptic plasticity. At excitatory synapses, repeated coincident pre- and postsynaptic activity triggers LTP at [[NMDA receptor]]s, leading to calcium influx, activation of CaMKII, and insertion of [[AMPA receptor]]s. This early phase is followed by late-phase LTP requiring [[CREB]]-dependent transcription, [[BDNF]] secretion, and structural synaptic remodeling. [[BDNF]] promotes dendritic branching, spine formation, and survival of existing neurons, particularly in the hippocampus.

Synaptic plasticity is not limited to LTP; long-term depression (LTD) and homeostatic plasticity (synaptic scaling) maintain network stability and prevent runaway excitation. During development, activity-dependent pruning refines neural circuits—a process governed by complement-mediated phagocytosis (C1q, C3) and microglial surveillance. This sculpting continues into early adulthood and is essential for efficient cognitive processing.

Adaptation to the environment requires continuous updating of internal models. Prediction error signals, generated in dopaminergic midbrain and prefrontal circuits, drive reinforcement learning and model-based reasoning. The capacity for cognitive flexibility—the ability to shift strategies in response to changing contingencies—is a core adaptive function linking prefrontal executive systems to survival.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Age-related cognitive decline is a normative process characterized by reduced processing speed, diminished working memory capacity, and occasional word-finding difficulties. These changes correlate with volumetric reductions in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, white matter hyperintensities, and declining dopaminergic tone. While not pathological per se, age-related decline increases vulnerability to neurodegenerative disease.

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) represents a prodromal stage between normal aging and dementia. The amnestic subtype, with prominent episodic memory deficits, converts to [[Alzheimer's Disease]] at approximately 10–15% per year. Non-amnestic MCI may herald [[Parkinson's Disease]] dementia, [[Alzheimer's Disease]], or frontotemporal dementia depending on the pattern of domain impairment.

[[Alzheimer's Disease]] is the most common cause of dementia worldwide, progressing from early episodic memory loss to global cognitive impairment. Pathologically, it features extracellular amyloid-β plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated [[Tau]], synaptic loss, and neuronal death, beginning in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus before spreading to association cortices. Vascular cognitive impairment arises from cerebrovascular disease—large-vessel strokes, small-vessel ischemia, or microbleeds—and often co-occurs with Alzheimer pathology (mixed dementia). Lewy body dementia, characterized by [[Alpha-synuclein]] aggregation, produces fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, and prominent visuospatial and attentional deficits. Frontotemporal dementia involves selective degeneration of frontal and temporal lobes, manifesting as behavioral variant (disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy) or language variants (semantic dementia, progressive nonfluent aphasia). [[Parkinson's Disease]] dementia develops in 60–80% of patients over time, with executive dysfunction and visuospatial impairment predominating.

Current pharmacotherapy is palliative. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) enhance cholinergic transmission and provide modest, temporary symptomatic improvement in attention and memory in [[Alzheimer's Disease]] and Lewy body dementia. [[Memantine]], an uncompetitive [[NMDA receptor]] antagonist, reduces excitotoxic glutamatergic signaling and is used in moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's Disease, often in combination with cholinesterase inhibitors. Disease-modifying therapies targeting amyloid-β (lecanemab, donanemab) have recently shown slowing of cognitive decline in early Alzheimer's Disease. Non-pharmacological interventions—physical exercise (which elevates [[BDNF]]), cognitive training, Mediterranean-style diets, social engagement, and cardiovascular risk management—contribute meaningfully to cognitive resilience and may delay onset or progression of cognitive impairment.
