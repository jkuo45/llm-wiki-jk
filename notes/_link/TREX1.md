# TREX1

[[TREX1]] (Three Prime Repair Exonuclease 1, also known as DNase III) is a 3'→5' DNA exonuclease that degrades cytosolic DNA, preventing inappropriate activation of the [[cGAS-STING|cGAS]]-[[STING]] pathway. TREX1 is the major cytosolic DNase in mammalian cells, critical for maintaining immune tolerance to self-DNA. Mutations in TREX1 cause autoimmune and autoinflammatory diseases characterized by excessive type I [[Interferon]] production.

## Structure & Domains

TREX1 is a 314 amino acid protein with an N-terminal DEED (Asp-Glu-Glu-Asp) catalytic domain containing four conserved acidic residues essential for exonuclease activity. The C-terminal transmembrane domain anchors TREX1 to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, where it co-localizes with [[cGAS-STING|cGAS]] and [[STING]]. TREX1 forms homodimers via a hydrophobic interface. The catalytic groove accommodates single-stranded and double-stranded DNA with a preference for pyrimidine-rich 3' overhangs. The crystal structure reveals a dimeric ring with back-to-back active sites, enabling processive degradation of cytosolic DNA substrates. The ER-targeting C-terminal domain is essential for TREX1 function in vivo, as forced cytoplasmic mislocalization impairs its ability to prevent innate immune activation.

## Mechanism of Action

TREX1 degrades cytosolic DNA — including genomic DNA leaked from the nucleus, mitochondrial DNA, retroelement-derived DNA, and endogenous retroviral DNA — to prevent [[cGAS-STING|cGAS]] activation. In the absence of TREX1, cytosolic DNA accumulates and engages the [[cGAS-STING|cGAS]] sensor, leading to 2'3'-cGAMP production and [[STING]] activation. This triggers a signaling cascade through [[TBK1]] and IRF3, resulting in type I [[Interferon]] (IFN-α/β) production and chronic interferon signaling that drives autoimmunity. TREX1 also degrades HIV-1 cDNA reverse transcription products in the cytosol, contributing to innate immune evasion by the virus. TREX1's exonuclease activity is enhanced by its interaction with the ER membrane and with other DNA repair proteins, including [[ATM]] and the [[DNA Repair]] machinery.

## Physiological Function

TREX1 serves as the primary safeguard against self-DNA sensing. It degrades nuclear DNA that leaks into the cytosol during mitosis, nuclear envelope rupture, and [[DNA Damage]]. It degrades mitochondrial DNA released from damaged mitochondria, preventing sterile inflammation. TREX1 also degrades retroelement cDNA (LINE-1, SVA elements) to suppress retrotransposition and innate immune activation. Additionally, TREX1 plays a role in HIV-1 restriction by degrading viral cDNA in the cytosol. TREX1 is constitutively expressed in most tissues, with highest expression in lymphocytes and neurons — cell types with particularly high metabolic activity and vulnerability to interferon-mediated damage.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

### Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome (AGS)

TREX1 mutations cause AGS type 1, the most common genetic cause of this disorder. AGS is a type I interferonopathy characterized by severe encephalopathy, basal ganglia calcifications, white matter abnormalities, chilblain skin lesions, elevated CSF lymphocytes, and high IFN-α in cerebrospinal fluid. Onset is typically neonatal or infantile, with progressive neurodegeneration that is often fatal in childhood. TREX1 knockouts in mice develop a lethal autoinflammatory disease with myocarditis and elevated type I [[Interferon]], recapitulating the human phenotype.

### Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

TREX1 polymorphisms are associated with SLE risk, notably the rs117974998 exon 3 missense variant. TREX1 mutations cause monogenic SLE in some families, representing a monogenic form of the polygenic disease. The mechanism involves failure to degrade cytosolic self-DNA, leading to chronic [[Interferon]] stimulation and loss of immune tolerance.

### Chilblain Lupus

TREX1 mutations cause cutaneous lupus (acral vasculopathy triggered by cold exposure), a milder manifestation of the interferonopathy spectrum. Skin lesions typically affect acral sites (fingers, toes, ears) and are exacerbated by cold temperatures.

### Therapeutic Landscape

Current therapeutic approaches include [[JAK Inhibitors]] (e.g., ruxolitinib) that block interferon signaling downstream of [[STING]], corticosteroids with limited efficacy, and investigational gene therapy approaches. Understanding the TREX1–cGAS–[[STING]] axis has opened new avenues for targeting type I interferonopathies, including small molecule inhibitors of [[cGAS-STING|cGAS]] and [[STING]] itself.
