# Pompe Disease

Pompe Disease (Glycogen Storage Disease Type II, GSD II) is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disease caused by deficiency of acid alpha-glucosidase ([[GAA]], EC 3.2.1.20/33), the lysosomal enzyme responsible for degrading glycogen to glucose. GAA deficiency leads to progressive accumulation of lysosomal glycogen, particularly in skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and smooth muscle tissues. The disorder has an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 40,000 to 300,000 births, with significant geographic variation. The [[GAA gene]] is located on chromosome 17q25.3, and over 400 disease-causing mutations have been identified. Pompe disease represents a continuum of severity, ranging from a rapidly progressive infantile form with severe cardiomyopathy to a slowly progressive late-onset form dominated by skeletal myopathy and respiratory insufficiency. The condition was first described by Joannes Cassianus Pompe in 1932 and remains one of the most clinically significant lysosomal storage disorders due to its predilection for striated muscle tissue.

## Structure & Domains

[[GAA]] is synthesized as a 110 kDa precursor polypeptide in the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]], then transported through the [[Golgi Apparatus]] where it undergoes N-linked glycosylation and mannose-6-phosphorylation, a critical post-translational modification that directs the enzyme to lysosomes via the mannose-6-phosphate receptor pathway. Within the lysosome, the 110 kDa precursor undergoes sequential proteolytic processing: 110 kDa → 95 kDa → 76 kDa → 70 kDa (the most catalytically active mature form) and a 72 kDa isoform. The mature enzyme functions as an (α/α)₄ homotetramer, with each subunit containing a catalytic domain belonging to the [[Glycoside Hydrolase]] family 31. The catalytic domain adopts a (β/α)₈ barrel fold with a conserved catalytic nucleophile (Asp521) and acid/base catalyst (Glu233). GAA contains multiple N-glycosylation sites (Asn99, Asn243, Asn390, Asn448, Asn723), which are essential for proper folding, stability, and lysosomal trafficking. The enzyme has an acidic pH optimum of 3.5–4.0, optimized for the low-pH lysosomal environment. Crystal structures of the catalytic domain have revealed the molecular basis for substrate specificity toward α-1,4- and α-1,6-glucosidic linkages in glycogen.

## Pathophysiology

The fundamental pathogenic mechanism in Pompe disease involves lysosomal glycogen accumulation due to GAA deficiency. In the absence of functional enzyme, undegraded glycogen accumulates within membrane-bound lysosomes, causing progressive lysosomal swelling. These engorged lysosomes eventually rupture, releasing glycogen and hydrolytic enzymes into the cytoplasm, which triggers further cellular damage. A critical aspect of Pompe disease pathology, particularly in skeletal muscle, is severe impairment of the [[Autophagy|autophagy]] pathway. Autophagosomes and autophagic debris accumulate massively in myofibers, often occupying vast regions of the cell cytoplasm and displacing normal contractile apparatus. This autophagic buildup — not merely lysosomal glycogen storage — is now recognized as a major contributor to skeletal muscle pathology and a key factor limiting therapeutic efficacy. In the heart, glycogen accumulation within cardiomyocytes leads to progressive cellular hypertrophy and dysfunction, manifesting as [[Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy|hypertrophic cardiomyopathy]]. In skeletal muscle, the combined effects of lysosomal dysfunction, autophagic impairment, and myofiber destruction produce a vacuolar myopathy characterized by progressive weakness and atrophy. Diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle involvement leads to restrictive respiratory failure, often the primary cause of morbidity and mortality in late-onset patients.

## Clinical Phenotypes

Pompe disease presents as a clinical spectrum defined by age of onset and severity. The **infantile-onset (classic) form** manifests between 2 and 6 months of age with severe generalized [[Hypotonia]] ("floppy baby" presentation), massive [[Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy]] (often with extreme left ventricular hypertrophy), [[Macroglossia]] (enlarged tongue), hepatomegaly, feeding difficulties, and progressive respiratory insufficiency. Without treatment, death typically occurs from cardiac or respiratory failure by age 1–2 years. Notably, cognitive development is preserved, as the central nervous system is relatively spared.

The **late-onset form** encompasses a broad range, from childhood to adult presentation. It is characterized by slowly progressive [[Proximal Myopathy]] affecting limb-girdle muscles (hip flexors, quadriceps, paraspinal muscles), progressive [[Respiratory Failure|respiratory insufficiency]] due to diaphragmatic weakness, and absence of significant cardiac involvement. Patients may present with difficulty climbing stairs, rising from a seated position, or with unexplained respiratory symptoms. The respiratory insufficiency often precedes skeletal muscle weakness and can be the dominant clinical feature. Adult-onset patients may remain undiagnosed for years, with symptoms attributed to other neuromuscular conditions. The rate of decline varies considerably among individuals, influenced by residual GAA enzyme activity, specific mutations, and modifier factors.

## Diagnostic Approach

Diagnosis of Pompe disease relies on several complementary approaches. **Enzyme activity assay** is the primary diagnostic test: GAA enzymatic activity is measured in dried blood spots, leukocytes, or cultured fibroblasts using artificial substrates. Reduced or absent GAA activity confirms the diagnosis. **Genetic testing** of the [[GAA gene]] identifies biallelic pathogenic mutations, enables carrier testing, and informs prognosis — certain genotype–phenotype correlations exist (e.g., the common IVS1 mutation c.32-13T>G is associated with late-onset disease). **Biomarker analysis** supports diagnosis and monitoring: urinary Glc4 (tetrasaccharide, a lysosomal glycogen-derived biomarker) is elevated and serves as a sensitive marker of disease burden; plasma Glc4 levels correlate with disease severity; and serum [[Creatine Kinase]] (CK), AST, and ALT are often mildly to moderately elevated. **Muscle biopsy** demonstrates vacuolar myopathy with PAS-positive, glycogen-filled lysosomes and autophagic vacuoles, though biopsy is increasingly supplanted by non-invasive diagnostics. **Newborn screening** (NBS) has been incorporated into many state and regional screening programs, enabling presymptomatic detection and early treatment initiation, which significantly improves outcomes particularly in the infantile form.

## Therapeutic Landscape

**Enzyme Replacement Therapy (ERT)** is the established standard of care. Alglucosidase alfa ([[Myozyme]]/Lumizyme, recombinant human GAA) is administered intravenously every two weeks. Approved in 2006, ERT has transformed the natural history of infantile Pompe disease, with dramatic improvements in survival (from <1 year to >10 years in many cases), cardiac normalization, and achievement of motor milestones. However, efficacy in skeletal muscle remains suboptimal, particularly in late-onset patients, due to limited uptake into mature myofibers and the confounding effect of autophagic buildup. **Anti-drug antibodies** (ADA) develop in a significant proportion of patients (up to 80–90% in infantile-onset), potentially reducing ERT efficacy; immunomodulatory strategies using [[Rituximab]] and [[Methotrexate]] are employed in selected cases.

**Avalglucosidase alfa** (Nexviazyme), a second-generation recombinant GAA with modified glycosylation resulting in higher mannose-6-phosphate content, was approved in 2021. It demonstrates improved cellular uptake and non-inferiority to alglucosidase alfa in clinical trials, with potential advantages in patients with high ADA titers.

**Substrate Reduction Therapy** aims to reduce glycogen synthesis and is under active investigation. **Gene therapy** using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors to deliver functional [[GAA gene]] represents a promising future approach, with several clinical trials underway. **Pharmacological chaperones** such as [[Miglustat]] are being explored as potential ERT adjuncts, stabilizing misfolded GAA variants to improve lysosomal trafficking. Combination strategies, including ERT with chaperone co-administration, are under investigation to overcome the limitations of monotherapy.
