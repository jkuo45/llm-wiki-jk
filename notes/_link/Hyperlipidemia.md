# Hyperlipidemia

[[Hyperlipidemia]] is a metabolic disorder defined by abnormally high concentrations of circulating lipids—most often low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, total cholesterol, and/or triglycerides—with reciprocal reductions in high-density lipoprotein (HDL). It is a major modifiable risk factor for [[Atherosclerosis]] and [[Cardiovascular Disease]].

## Rapamycin-Associated Hyperlipidemia

Chronic inhibition of [[mTORC1]] by [[Rapamycin]] and its analogs ([[Rapalog|rapalogs]]) frequently elevates cholesterol and triglyceride levels. Mechanistically, the [[mTOR]]–[[S6K1]] axis normally promotes lipogenesis while suppressing adipose lipid storage; chronic rapamycin disrupts this balance, upregulating hepatic gluconeogenesis and impairing lipid deposition in adipose tissue, producing glucose intolerance and hyperlipidemia ([Houde et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20299475/)). In clinical cohorts, elevated cholesterol and triglycerides are among the most common metabolic adverse events of everolimus in both transplantation and tuberous sclerosis complex populations ([French et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27613521/); [Lee et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38310895/)).

> [!warning] Context matters
> Rapamycin-induced hyperlipidemia is mechanistically linked to mTORC1 inhibition and is usually dose-dependent and reversible on discontinuation. It is distinct from, and often co-occurs with, [[Glucose Intolerance]] and [[Insulin Resistance]]—trade-offs emphasized in the longevity literature.

## Clinical Relevance

Hyperlipidemia is listed among the "manageable" class-effects of rapalogs in trials such as EXIST-3, yet it is less tolerable in healthy individuals taking rapamycin for anti-aging or aesthetic purposes where no overt disease justifies the risk ([Roark & Iffland, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12226543/)). Standard lipid-lowering agents such as [[Statins]] are commonly co-prescribed.
