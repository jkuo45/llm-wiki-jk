# Relocalization of Chromatin Modifiers (RCM) Hypothesis

The **Relocalization of Chromatin Modifiers (RCM) Hypothesis** is a prominent framework in aging biology, primarily developed by [[David Sinclair]] and colleagues. It posits that aging in eukaryotes (including mammals) results largely from the progressive loss of epigenetic information due to the repeated relocalization of [[Chromatin Modifiers|chromatin-modifying proteins]] during [[DNA Damage|DNA repair]].

> [!info] Primary Source
> Yang, Sinclair et al., "Loss of epigenetic information as a cause of mammalian aging," *Cell* 186, 1–19 (2023). [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0092867422015707)

## Core Idea

[[Chromatin Modifiers|Chromatin modifiers]] — including [[Sirtuins|sirtuins]] such as [[SIRT1]] and [[SIRT6]], [[HDAC1]], and [[PARP1]] — normally reside at specific genomic loci to maintain the gene-expression programs that define cellular identity and function. When [[DNA Damage|DNA damage]], particularly [[Double-Strand Break|double-strand breaks (DSBs)]], occurs, these proteins are recruited to damage sites to assist repair. This transiently disrupts their original positions, altering local gene expression.

In young cells the system largely resets after repair, with modifiers returning to their native loci. Over a lifetime of accumulated damage, however, not all modifiers return perfectly. The cumulative effect is:

- Gradual erosion of the [[Epigenetic Landscape|epigenetic landscape]].
- Loss of cellular identity (cells "forget" their differentiated state).
- Dysregulated gene expression, including inappropriate activation of silenced genes.
- Emergence of broad aging phenotypes.

> [!quote] Framing within the Information Theory of Aging
> RCM is cast as part of the broader [[Information Theory of Aging]], in which aging is treated as a "software" (epigenetic) problem rather than purely "hardware" (e.g., [[Mutations|mutations]]) damage. Unlike permanent mutations, epigenetic changes are potentially reversible.

## Evidence and Key Experiments

- **Yeast origins:** The hypothesis extends observations in budding yeast, where relocalization of the [[Sir2 (yeast)|Sir2 complex]] (a [[Sirtuins|sirtuin]]) from silent mating-type loci to rDNA or damage sites drives sterility and other aging hallmarks.
- **Mammalian support — the ICE mouse:** The [[David Sinclair|Sinclair lab]] generated the [[ICE Mouse|ICE mouse (Inducible Changes in the Epigenome)]], which uses a controllable endonuclease ([[I-PpoI]]) to induce targeted [[Double-Strand Break|DSBs]] *without* introducing mutations. Inducing breaks, permitting repair, and assessing outcomes produces accelerated epigenetic aging, physiological decline (metabolic dysfunction, reduced tissue function, cognitive changes), and shifts in the [[Epigenetic Clock|epigenetic clock]] — consistent with RCM.
- **Modifiers known to relocalize:** [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], [[HDAC1]], and [[PARP1]]. Repeated activation disrupts youthful patterns and can de-repress [[Retrotransposon|retrotransposons]].
- **Predictable drift:** RCM explains why aging is somewhat deterministic despite stochastic damage: the conserved damage-response program yields consistent epigenetic drift.

## Evolutionary Perspective (Antagonistic Pleiotropy)

[[Antagonistic Pleiotropy|RCM likely originated as a beneficial short-term response]]. Recruiting modifiers to damage sites helps cells survive acute threats (e.g., rapid replication in microbes/embryos or injury) by prioritizing repair and survival over normal gene regulation. This is advantageous in youth; across a lifetime, the cumulative "failure to reset" becomes detrimental and contributes to aging.

## Connections to Other Aging Concepts

- **[[Epigenetic Clock]]:** [[Double-Strand Break|DSB repair]] and RCM may advance clocks by perturbing [[DNA Methylation|DNA methylation]] machinery (e.g., [[TET Enzymes|TETs]], [[DNMT1|DNMTs]]).
- **[[Cellular Reprogramming]]:** [[Partial Reprogramming|Partial reprogramming]] (e.g., via [[Yamanaka Factors|Yamanaka factors]]) can reset epigenetic information, supporting the reversibility of aging at the information level.
- It complements other theories (e.g., [[DNA Damage|DNA damage accumulation]], loss of proteostasis) but elevates epigenetic information loss as a primary, upstream driver.

## Therapeutic Implications

Research continues into chromatin "fatigue" — reduced capacity to re-establish prior gene-expression programs after repair. Interventions targeting [[Sirtuins|sirtuins]] (e.g., [[NAD+]] boosters), improving repair fidelity, or directly resetting the [[Epigenome|epigenome]] are active areas. The hypothesis suggests cells could be "rebooted" by restoring epigenetic fidelity.
