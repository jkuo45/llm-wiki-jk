# Cysteine

**Cysteine** (Cys, C; 2-amino-3-mercaptopropionic acid) is a semi-essential proteinogenic amino acid distinguished by its reactive thiol (-SH) group. With the molecular formula HO₂CCH(NH₂)CH₂SH, cysteine occupies a unique position in amino acid biochemistry: the thiol group is highly nucleophilic, enabling disulfide bond formation, metal ion coordination, and catalytic activity in enzyme active sites. Cysteine is the rate-limiting precursor for [[Glutathione]] (GSH) synthesis, making it indispensable for cellular redox homeostasis and detoxification. Unlike the other sulfur-containing amino acid [[Homocysteine]], cysteine carries a free sulfhydryl group that directly participates in oxidative chemistry.

## Chemical Properties

The thiol group of cysteine has a pKa of approximately 8.3, meaning that at physiological pH (~7.4), a small but significant fraction exists in the thiolate anion form (Cys-S⁻), which is the reactive species for most biochemical reactions. The thiolate is a potent nucleophile and can undergo several critical chemical transformations:

- **Disulfide bond formation**: Two cysteine residues can oxidize to form a cystine disulfide bond (Cys-S-S-Cys), stabilizing tertiary and quaternary protein structures. This is particularly important for secreted proteins and extracellular domains, where the oxidizing environment of the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]] promotes disulfide isomerization. Disulfide bonds are critical in immunoglobulin folding, insulin processing, and albumin structure.
- **Metal chelation**: The thiol group coordinates zinc, iron, and other transition metals. Cysteine residues are integral to [[Zinc Finger]] protein domains, iron-sulfur cluster assembly in mitochondrial complexes, and metallothionein metal buffering.
- **Redox cycling**: Cysteine readily interconverts between reduced (thiol) and oxidized (disulfide/cystine) forms. This redox cycling is exploited by cellular antioxidant systems, including the [[Thioredoxin]] and glutaredoxin pathways, which maintain cytoplasmic proteins in their reduced state.
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**: A pharmacologically important derivative where the amino group is acetylated, increasing stability and oral bioavailability. NAC serves as a mucolytic agent and as a precursor that readily deacetylated to cysteine, thereby boosting intracellular [[Glutathione]] levels. NAC is a well-established antidote for [[Acetaminophen]] (paracetamol) hepatotoxicity.

## Biosynthesis & Metabolism

Cysteine is classified as a semi-essential amino acid: it can be endogenously synthesized but dietary intake becomes critical under conditions of rapid growth, oxidative stress, or limited methionine availability.

**Transsulfuration pathway**: The primary endogenous route converts the essential amino acid methionine to cysteine via [[Homocysteine]] as an intermediate. Methionine is first adenosylated to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), which donates its methyl group to become S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), then hydrolyzed to homocysteine. Homocysteine enters the transsulfuration pathway when folate and [[Vitamin B12]]-dependent remethylation is suppressed:

1. **Cystathionine β-synthase (CBS)**: Condenses homocysteine with serine to form cystathionine. This pyridoxal phosphate ([[Vitamin B6]])-dependent enzyme is the committed step and is allosterically activated by SAM.
2. **Cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE)**: Cleaves cystathionine to yield cysteine, α-ketobutyrate, and ammonia.

**Dietary sources**: Cysteine is abundant in high-protein foods—poultry, eggs, dairy, red meat, and legumes. Whey protein is particularly rich in cysteine and is a dietary strategy to raise glutathione levels.

**Catabolism**: Cysteine is primarily oxidized by **cysteine dioxygenase (CDO)**, which adds both atoms of molecular oxygen to the thiol group, forming cysteine sulfinic acid. This metabolite has three fates:

- Reduction back to cysteine via cysteine sulfinate decarboxylase (minor pathway)
- Decarboxylation to hypotaurine, then oxidation to **taurine** (important for bile acid conjugation and osmoregulation)
- Oxidative deamination by transamination to β-sulfinylpyruvate, which spontaneously decomposes to **sulfite**, then further oxidized by sulfite oxidase to **sulfate** (excreted renally)

**Glutathione synthesis**: Cysteine is the rate-limiting substrate for [[Glutathione]] biosynthesis, a two-step ATP-dependent process:
1. γ-Glutamylcysteine synthetase (GCL): condenses glutamate and cysteine
2. Glutathione synthetase: adds glycine to form γ-L-glutamyl-L-cysteinyl-glycine (GSH)

Intracellular cysteine availability—regulated by cystine uptake via the xc⁻ antiporter (SLC7A11) and transsulfuration flux—is the primary determinant of glutathione pools.

## Physiological Function

Cysteine's thiol chemistry underpins its central roles in cellular physiology:

- **Glutathione synthesis**: As the principal cellular antioxidant, glutathione neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS), maintains protein thiol states, and recycles vitamins C and E. Glutathione is consumed by glutathione peroxidases during peroxide detoxification and regenerated by glutathione reductase using NADPH.
- **Protein folding and quality control**: Disulfide bond formation in the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]] is catalyzed by protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), which uses its cysteine active sites to shuffle incorrect disulfides and drive native folding. This is essential for secretory pathway proteins, including antibodies, hormones, and receptors.
- **Iron-sulfur cluster assembly**: Cysteine residues coordinate [2Fe-2S] and [4Fe-4S] clusters in mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes (Complexes I, II, and III) and in enzymes of the TCA cycle and DNA repair. Defective iron-sulfur biogenesis underlies several mitochondrial disorders.
- **Detoxification**: Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) conjugate glutathione (via its cysteine thiol) to electrophilic xenobiotics and reactive metabolites, facilitating renal excretion. This pathway is critical for Phase II hepatic detoxification.
- **Nitric oxide signaling**: Cysteine residues in target proteins can be S-nitrosylated (Cys-NO), serving as both nitric oxide donors and acceptors. This post-translational modification regulates ion channels, transcription factors, and mitochondrial function.
- **Epigenetic regulation**: Cysteine residues in DNA methyltransferases and histone demethylases coordinate zinc or iron cofactors, directly linking cysteine metabolism to [[Epigenetics]] and gene regulation.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Inherited disorders of cysteine metabolism highlight the amino acid's clinical importance:

- **Homocystinuria**: Autosomal recessive [[Cystic Fibrosis]] of CBS (cystathionine β-synthase deficiency) leads to homocysteine accumulation and cysteine depletion. Clinical features include thromboembolism, osteoporosis, ectopia lentis (lens dislocation), and intellectual disability. Treatment involves [[Vitamin B6]] supplementation (pyridoxine-responsive forms), betaine (to promote remethylation), and dietary methionine restriction with cysteine supplementation.
- **Cystinuria**: Impaired renal reabsorption of cysteine, ornithine, lysine, and arginine (COLA amino acids) due to defects in the rBAT/4F2hc transporter (SLC3A1/SLC7A9). Cysteine's low solubility at urinary pH leads to hexagonal cystine crystals and recurrent kidney stones. Management includes hydration, urinary alkalinization, and tiopronin or D-penicillamine (thiol-containing chelators that form soluble mixed disulfides).
- **Cystinosis**: Loss-of-function mutations in cystinosin (CTNS), a lysosomal cystine transporter, cause lysosomal cystine accumulation and crystal formation in multiple tissues. The most severe form presents as renal Fanconi syndrome in infancy, progressing to end-stage renal disease. Cysteamine (a thiol drug that forms a mixed disulfide with cystine, allowing transport via alternative pathways) is the standard treatment.
- **Oxidative stress and chronic disease**: Glutathione depletion—driven by cysteine insufficiency—is implicated in the pathogenesis of [[COPD]], pulmonary fibrosis, [[HIV]]/AIDS progression, and neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's). Aging is associated with declining transsulfuration activity and reduced tissue glutathione.
- **N-acetylcysteine clinical applications**: NAC remains one of the most versatile amino acid-derived therapeutics: (1) **Acetaminophen overdose**: NAC replenishes glutathione to detoxify the reactive metabolite NAPQI, preventing hepatotoxicity. (2) **Mucolytic**: NAC cleaves disulfide bonds in mucus glycoproteins, reducing viscosity in COPD, cystic fibrosis, and bronchitis. (3) **Psychiatric adjunct**: Emerging evidence supports NAC as adjunctive therapy in obsessive-compulsive disorder, substance use disorders, and schizophrenia, potentially via modulation of glutamatergic neurotransmission and oxidative stress.
