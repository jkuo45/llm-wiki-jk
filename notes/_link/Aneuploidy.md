# Aneuploidy

Aneuploidy is the presence of an abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell, deviating from the normal diploid complement. In humans, where the standard karyotype is 46 chromosomes (2n = 46), aneuploid cells may carry 45 (hypodiploid) or 47 (hyperdiploid) chromosomes, though deviations of multiple chromosomes also occur. The condition arises from errors in chromosome segregation during [[Meiosis]] — producing aneuploid gametes that lead to constitutional aneuploidy in offspring — or during mitosis, generating somatic aneuploidy in otherwise normal tissues. Aneuploidy is the leading genetic cause of miscarriage and intellectual disability, and it is a near-universal hallmark of [[Cancer]] cells. The principal categories include monosomy (2n−1), trisomy (2n+1), tetrasomy (2n+2), and nullisomy (2n−2). When an individual harbors a mixture of normal and aneuploid cell populations, the condition is termed mosaic aneuploidy, which can produce milder or variable phenotypes depending on the tissue distribution and lineage of affected cells.

## Causes of Aneuploidy

### Meiotic Errors

The most common mechanism underlying constitutional aneuploidy is nondisjunction — the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during meiosis I or meiosis II. When nondisjunction occurs in meiosis I, homologous pairs fail to disjoin; in meiosis II, sister chromatids remain joined. The result is gametes with one extra or one missing chromosome. After fertilization, the zygote becomes trisomic or monosomic.

The single greatest risk factor for meiotic nondisjunction is advanced maternal age. Oocyte aging is associated with deterioration of the cohesin complexes that hold homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids together, as well as progressive defects in the meiotic spindle apparatus. Recombination defects — particularly reduced or aberrant chiasma formation — also predispose to nondisjunction by weakening the physical linkage between homologs. Errors in kinetochore–microtubule attachment, including premature separation of sister chromatids, further contribute to missegregation in aged oocytes.

### Mitotic Errors

Somatic aneuploidy arises from errors during cell division in mitosis. The most prevalent mechanism is merotelic attachment, in which a single kinetochore becomes attached to microtubules emanating from both spindle poles. This creates tension that can be partially satisfied, allowing the cell to pass the spindle assembly checkpoint, yet the chromosome may lag during anaphase and be missegregated. Monotelic attachment — where one sister chromatid remains entirely unattached — and syntelic attachment — where both sister chromatids attach to the same pole — also contribute, though less commonly. Additional risk factors include centrosome amplification (producing multipolar spindles), defects in the spindle assembly checkpoint, and replication stress causing chromosome breakage.

### Aneuploidy in Cancer

In the context of malignancy, aneuploidy is driven by chromosomal instability (CIN), a condition in which cells exhibit a persistently elevated rate of chromosome gain or loss. CIN can result from weakened spindle assembly checkpoint signaling through mutations or downregulation of checkpoint components such as [[BUB1]], [[BUBR1]], and MAD2. Telomere dysfunction — leading to breakage-fusion-bridge cycles — and replication stress from oncogene-driven aberrant origin firing are additional CIN mechanisms. Defective DNA damage repair, centrosome abnormalities, and altered microtubule dynamics all synergize to sustain CIN in tumors.

## Consequences of Aneuploidy

### Gene Dosage Imbalance

The fundamental molecular consequence of aneuploidy is gene dosage imbalance. Cells carrying an extra chromosome express approximately 150% of the normal dose of genes encoded on that chromosome, while monosomic cells express roughly 50%. This dosage perturbation disrupts the stoichiometry of multi-protein complexes, alters signaling pathway flux, and imposes pervasive stress on cellular systems.

### Proteotoxic Stress

Among the most damaging consequences of gains of whole chromosomes is proteotoxic stress. The excess proteins produced from aneuploid chromosomes overwhelm the [[Proteasome]] and chaperone capacity of the cell, leading to protein aggregation, activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) in the endoplasmic reticulum, and induction of autophagic degradation pathways. This proteotoxic burden is a primary reason that aneuploidy generally reduces cellular fitness and proliferative capacity.

### Metabolic Stress

Aneuploid cells experience metabolic reprogramming as a secondary consequence of gene dosage changes. Altered expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters on gained or lost chromosomes shifts metabolic flux, creating energy imbalance and redox stress. Cells with extra chromosomes often exhibit increased glycolytic dependence, altered amino acid metabolism, and elevated reactive oxygen species production.

### Tumor Suppression and Tumor Promotion

Aneuploidy exerts a paradoxical dual role in cancer. In normal cells with intact [[TP53]], aneuploidy triggers p53-dependent growth arrest or apoptosis, acting as a tumor-suppressive barrier. However, in cells that have lost p53 function — a common early event in tumorigenesis — aneuploidy promotes further genomic instability, accelerates loss of heterozygosity at tumor suppressor loci, and enables the clonal evolution that drives cancer progression and drug resistance.

## Trisomies and Clinical Conditions

### Trisomy 21 (Down Syndrome)

Trisomy 21 is the most common viable autosomal trisomy, occurring in approximately 1 in 700 live births. Clinical features include intellectual disability, characteristic facial morphology (flat facial profile, upslanting palpebral fissures, protruding tongue), congenital heart defects (particularly atrioventricular septal defects), and increased risk of early-onset [[Alzheimer's Disease]] — attributable in part to the amyloid precursor protein ([[APP]]) gene located on chromosome 21. Individuals with Down syndrome also have elevated risk of acute megakaryoblastic leukemia, thyroid dysfunction, and immune dysregulation. Prenatal screening involves maternal serum biomarkers and non-invasive prenatal testing, with confirmatory karyotyping or chromosomal microarray.

### Trisomy 18 (Edwards Syndrome) and Trisomy 13 (Patau Syndrome)

Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome) and trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) are severe chromosomal conditions, each occurring in approximately 1 in 5,000 births. Both are associated with profound intellectual disability, multiple congenital anomalies (including cardiac defects, renal malformations, and craniofacial abnormalities), and very high mortality — the majority of affected individuals do not survive beyond the first year of life. Prenatal detection via ultrasound findings and cell-free fetal DNA screening is now routine.

### Sex Chromosome Aneuploidies

Turner syndrome (45,X) results from monosomy of the X chromosome in phenotypic females, producing short stature, streak gonads, webbed neck, lymphedema, and infertility. Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) affects phenotypic males and is characterized by tall stature, hypogonadism, gynecomastia, and variable cognitive profiles. Both conditions demonstrate that sex chromosome dosage imbalances are generally better tolerated than autosomal aneuploidies, though they still produce clinically significant phenotypes.

## Aneuploidy in Cancer

Approximately 90% of solid tumors and a substantial fraction of hematological malignancies are aneuploid. Chromosomal instability — the ongoing rate of chromosome missegregation — is a key driver of intratumor heterogeneity, enabling tumors to rapidly generate phenotypic diversity upon which selection can act. Through CIN, tumors can achieve loss of tumor suppressor genes on monosomic chromosomes, gain and amplification of oncogenes on trisomic chromosomes, loss of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules for immune evasion, and acquisition of drug resistance through copy number changes at therapeutic target loci.

Therapeutic exploitation of CIN represents an emerging frontier. Conventional agents including [[Taxanes]] and vinca alkaloids target microtubule dynamics to induce mitotic arrest and cell death in rapidly dividing tumor cells. More selective strategies include inhibitors of Aurora kinase A ([[AURKA]]) and Polo-like kinase 1 ([[PLK1]]), which regulate mitotic progression and whose inhibition preferentially kills cells with elevated CIN. Synthetic lethal approaches — targeting vulnerabilities created by specific aneuploid karyotypes, such as sensitivity to [[p53]] loss or dependence on specific signaling pathways — offer promise for precision oncology in aneuploid cancers.
