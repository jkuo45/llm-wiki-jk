## Overview

[[Allosteric Regulation]] is the control of an enzyme or protein's activity by the binding of an effector molecule at a site other than the active (orthosteric) site. This binding induces a conformational change that alters affinity or catalytic rate, enabling fine-tuned, reversible control of metabolism and signaling.

## Biological Role / Mechanism

Allosteric proteins typically exist in equilibrium between active (R) and inactive (T) states. Effectors shift this equilibrium:

- **Allosteric activators** stabilize the R state (e.g., ADP activating isocitrate dehydrogenase).
- **Allosteric inhibitors** stabilize the T state (e.g., ATP and NADH inhibiting key [[TCA cycle]] enzymes).

Classic examples include feedback inhibition in metabolic pathways (e.g., [[ATP]] inhibition of phosphofructokinase-1 in glycolysis) and cooperative oxygen binding by hemoglobin. Allostery underlies [[Enzyme]] kinetics described by the Hill equation and is a major drug-targeting mechanism.

## Physiological / Pathological Relevance

Allosteric dysregulation contributes to metabolic disorders, cancer, and channelopathies. Allosteric modulators offer therapeutic advantages (greater specificity, saturability) over orthosteric drugs. In the wiki, allosteric regulation governs metabolic enzymes tied to [[Sirtuins]] activity and mitochondrial resilience, and informs design of targeted small-molecule interventions.
