# Beta-amyloid

Beta-amyloid (Aβ) refers to a family of 36–43 amino acid peptides generated by the proteolytic processing of the transmembrane protein [[Amyloid Precursor Protein|APP]] (amyloid precursor protein). APP is sequentially cleaved by β-secretase ([[BACE1]]) and the γ-secretase complex (composed of presenilin, nicastrin, APH-1, and PEN-2) to release Aβ peptides into the extracellular space. The most abundant isoforms are Aβ40 (40 residues, less aggregation-prone) and Aβ42 (42 residues, more hydrophobic, aggregation-prone, and neurotoxic). Under physiological conditions, Aβ exists predominantly as soluble monomers. However, Aβ monomers can self-associate through a nucleation-dependent pathway to form soluble oligomers, which then progress to protofibrils, mature fibrils, and ultimately the insoluble amyloid plaques characteristic of [[Alzheimer's Disease]]. Critically, soluble Aβ oligomers — not the fibrillar plaques themselves — are now recognized as the primary neurotoxic species responsible for synaptic dysfunction and cognitive impairment.

## Structure & Domains

The full-length Aβ42 sequence is:

```
DAEFRHDSGYEVHHQKLVFFAEDVGSNKGAIIGLMVGGVVIA
```

Aβ can be divided into distinct structural regions with distinct biophysical properties:

- **N-terminal disordered region (residues 1–16):** The first ~16 residues are largely unstructured and solvent-exposed. This region contains the D1 (Asp1) and D2 (Asp7) hydrophobic sites that interact with multiple receptors, including [[Prion Protein|PrPC]], [[NMDA Receptor]]s, and [[Insulin Receptor|insulin receptors]]. The N-terminus is subject to post-translational modifications including pyroglutamate formation (at Glu3 or Glu11), N-terminal truncation by various peptidases, and methionine oxidation at Met35, all of which modulate aggregation kinetics and toxicity.

- **Central hydrophobic cluster (residues 17–21, KLVFF):** This five-residue stretch (Lys17–Val18–Phe19–Phe20–Val21) constitutes the core self-recognition element that drives β-sheet stacking and fibril formation. The KLVFF motif is necessary and sufficient for Aβ–Aβ interaction and has been used as the basis for aggregation inhibitor design.

- **C-terminal hydrophobic tail (residues 29–42):** The C-terminal region is highly hydrophobic and embeds into lipid membranes. In Aβ42, two additional residues (Ile41, Ala42) extend this hydrophobic tail, increasing its propensity to adopt a β-hairpin turn that stabilizes oligomeric assemblies. Aβ40 lacks these two residues, which partly explains why Aβ42 is significantly more aggregation-prone and toxic.

- **Cross-β sheet architecture:** In mature fibrils, Aβ adopts a characteristic cross-β structure in which β-strands run perpendicular to the fibril axis, stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic packing. Cryo-EM structures of Aβ42 fibrils reveal a U-shaped or S-shaped monomer fold with two β-strands connected by a turn, stacked into protofilaments. In contrast, oligomers are structurally heterogeneous and adopt partially disordered or β-barrel-like conformations that are more disruptive to membranes.

The transition from monomer to toxic oligomer is concentration-dependent and kinetically controlled. Aβ42 forms oligomers more rapidly than Aβ40, and mixed Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios (as altered in familial [[Alzheimer's Disease]] mutations) shift the equilibrium toward toxic species.

## Mechanism of Action & Toxicity

Soluble Aβ oligomers exert neurotoxicity through multiple converging mechanisms:

### Receptor-Mediated Synaptic Toxicity

Aβ oligomers bind with high affinity to several neuronal surface receptors, triggering aberrant signaling cascades:

- [[Prion Protein|PrPC]] (cellular prion protein): Aβ oligomers bind PrPC with nanomolar affinity, activating Fyn kinase and downstream NMDA receptor-dependent excitotoxicity. PrPC acts as a high-affinity receptor that mediates Aβ-induced synaptic depression.
- [[NMDA Receptor]]s: Aβ oligomers enhance NMDA receptor-mediated currents, promoting excitotoxic calcium influx and long-term depression (LTD) while inhibiting long-term potentiation (LTP), the cellular substrate of memory formation.
- [[Insulin Receptor|Insulin receptors]] and [[IGF-1 Receptor|IGF-1 receptors]]: Aβ disrupts insulin signaling in the brain, contributing to cerebral insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism — a feature termed "type 3 diabetes."
- [[mGluR5]] (metabotropic glutamate receptor 5): Aβ binding to mGluR5 activates Fyn kinase and promotes tau phosphorylation.
- [[EphB2]] receptor tyrosine kinase: Aβ promotes EphB2 endocytosis and degradation, disrupting NMDA receptor localization and synaptic function.
- [[p75NTR]] (neurotrophin receptor p75): Aβ binds p75NTR, activating JNK-mediated pro-apoptotic signaling.

### Calcium Dysregulation & Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Aβ oligomers permeabilize neuronal membranes, forming ion-permeable pores or disrupting existing channel function, leading to uncontrolled [[Calcium Ions|calcium]] influx. Elevated intracellular calcium activates calpains, calcineurin, and other calcium-dependent proteases. Aβ also accumulates within mitochondria, where it inhibits Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), disrupts the electron transport chain, increases reactive oxygen species ([[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]]) production, and triggers opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), ultimately activating [[Apoptosis|apoptotic]] cascades.

### Neuroinflammation

Aβ aggregates are recognized by pattern recognition receptors on [[Microglia]] and [[Astrocytes]], including [[TLR2]], [[TLR4]], and [[RAGE]] (receptor for advanced glycation end products). This activates NF-κB signaling, driving production of pro-inflammatory cytokines ([[TNF-alpha|TNF-α]], [[IL-1β|IL-1β]], [[IL-6]]), chemokines, and complement factors. Chronic microglial activation shifts microglia from a neuroprotective (M2-like) to a neurotoxic (M1-like) phenotype, sustaining a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and neurodegeneration.

### Tau Pathology

Aβ oligomers activate several kinases that phosphorylate [[Tau Protein|tau]], including [[GSK3β]] (glycogen synthase kinase 3β) and [[CDK5]] (cyclin-dependent kinase 5). Hyperphosphorylated tau detaches from microtubules, disrupts axonal transport, and forms neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) — the second pathological hallmark of [[Alzheimer's Disease]]. Aβ and tau pathology are synergistic: Aβ drives tau spreading, while tau is required for Aβ-mediated neuronal death.

### Autophagy & Proteostasis Impairment

Aβ disrupts [[Autophagy]] at multiple steps. It impairs autophagosome-lysosome fusion by altering lysosomal pH and cathepsin activity, blocks the chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) pathway by competing with substrate peptides for the LAMP2A receptor, and overwhelms the proteasome system. This creates a feed-forward loop where impaired clearance leads to further Aβ accumulation. The transcription factor [[TFEB]], a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy, is sequestered in the cytoplasm by Aβ-induced mTORC1 activation, further suppressing proteostasis.

### Blood-Brain Barrier Disruption

Aβ accumulates in cerebral blood vessel walls, causing cerebral amyloid angiopathy ([[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy|CAA]]). Aβ damages endothelial cells, compromises tight junction integrity, activates matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and promotes pericyte degeneration, leading to blood-brain barrier (BBB) leakage, reduced cerebral blood flow, and microhemorrhages.

## Physiological Function

Under normal conditions, Aβ is produced constitutively by all cell types, including neurons, and is rapidly cleared from the brain. Endogenous Aβ levels are maintained at picomolar concentrations through a balance of production and degradation. Major clearance pathways include:

- **Enzymatic degradation:** [[Neprilysin]] and [[Insulin-Degrading Enzyme|IDE]] (insulin-degrading enzyme) are the primary endopeptidases responsible for Aβ catabolism. Neprilysin preferentially degrades Aβ40, while IDE can degrade both Aβ40 and Aβ42.
- **Microglial phagocytosis:** Activated [[Microglia]] internalize and degrade Aβ aggregates via receptor-mediated endocytosis, including through TREM2, SCARA1, and CD36 receptors.
- **Glymphatic clearance:** Aβ is cleared from the interstitial fluid via the glymphatic system — a paravascular pathway that facilitates CSF–interstitial fluid exchange — which is most active during deep (slow-wave) [[Sleep]].
- **Transcytosis across the BBB:** Low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) on endothelial cells mediates Aβ efflux from brain to blood.

Emerging evidence suggests Aβ may serve normal physiological roles. Aβ has antimicrobial properties and can kill bacteria, fungi, and viruses *in vitro*, leading to the "antimicrobial protection hypothesis" which posits that Aβ production is an innate immune response. Aβ may also regulate synaptic plasticity at physiological concentrations, modulate neuronal activity during development, and participate in cholesterol transport and lipid metabolism.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

### Alzheimer's Disease

The amyloid cascade hypothesis, first articulated by Hardy and Higgins in 1992, proposes that Aβ accumulation is the primary upstream event driving [[Alzheimer's Disease]] pathology. Genetic evidence strongly supports this framework: all known autosomal dominant mutations causing early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease occur in the *APP* gene (chromosome 21), *PSEN1* (presenilin 1), or *PSEN2* (presenilin 2) genes, all of which increase the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio or total Aβ production. The "Arctic" APP mutation (E693G) enhances protofibril formation, while the "Osaka" mutation (E693Δ) impairs Aβ degradation.

Neuropathologically, Aβ deposits appear as extracellular amyloid plaques in two major forms: diffuse plaques (amorphous, non-fibrillar deposits, possibly early-stage) and neuritic (senile) plaques (dense-core plaques surrounded by dystrophic neurites, activated microglia, and reactive astrocytes). Amyloid deposition follows a stereotypical braak-like pattern, beginning in the neocortical regions and spreading to limbic and subcortical areas. Aβ PET imaging using tracers such as Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB), florbetapir, florbetaben, and flutemetamol allows *in vivo* detection of amyloid plaques and has become a standard diagnostic tool.

### Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy

In [[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy|CAA]], Aβ (predominantly Aβ40) deposits within the walls of cortical and leptomeningeal blood vessels, weakening the vessel wall and predisposing to lobar intracerebral hemorrhage, cortical superficial siderosis, and transient focal neurological episodes. CAA is present in >80% of individuals with [[Alzheimer's Disease]] and is an independent risk factor for hemorrhagic stroke in the elderly.

### Down Syndrome

Trisomy 21 results in three copies of the *APP* gene (located on chromosome 21), leading to lifelong overproduction of Aβ. Virtually all individuals with [[Down Syndrome]] develop Alzheimer's-type neuropathology by age 40, making Down Syndrome a natural model for Aβ-driven neurodegeneration.

### Therapeutic Landscape

The therapeutic landscape for Aβ-targeted therapy has undergone a dramatic transformation:

- **Failed approaches:** BACE inhibitors (verubecestat, atabecestat) and γ-secretase inhibitors (semagacestat, tarenflurbil) failed in Phase III trials, either showing no benefit or worsening cognition. γ-secretase inhibitors also caused NOTCH-related side effects due to off-target inhibition of Notch signaling. The field experienced repeated failures for over two decades.

- **Aducanumab (Aduhelm):** A humanized anti-Aβ monoclonal antibody targeting aggregated Aβ (fibrils and soluble oligomers). FDA approved in 2021 via the accelerated pathway based on amyloid plaque clearance as a surrogate endpoint. The approval was highly controversial, with an FDA advisory committee voting against it and three committee members resigning in protest. Biogen withdrew Aduhelm from the market in early 2024.

- **Lecanemab (Leqembi):** A humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that selectively binds Aβ protofibrils (soluble aggregated species). FDA approved in January 2023 (traditional pathway) based on the Clarity AD Phase III trial, which showed a statistically significant 27% slowing of cognitive decline (CDR-SB) over 18 months. Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) occurred in ~21% of participants (ARIA-E: 12.6%, ARIA-H: 17.3%), with higher risk in *APOE4* carriers.

- **Donanemab (Kisunla):** A monoclonal antibody targeting pyridyllysine-modified Aβ (a post-translational modification found predominantly in established plaques). FDA approved in July 2024 based on the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial, which demonstrated a 35% slowing of cognitive decline in the overall population and 36% in participants with low/medium tau burden. Treatment was discontinued once amyloid was cleared to target levels.

- **Emerging approaches:** Anti-tau immunotherapies, [[NAD+]] precursors, [[Autophagy]] modulators, and combination therapies targeting both Aβ and tau are under active investigation. Gene therapy approaches using CRISPR-based editing of *APP* and antisense oligonucleotides targeting *APP* mRNA are in preclinical development.
