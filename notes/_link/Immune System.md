# Immune System

The immune system comprises two major branches: [[Innate Immune System|innate immunity]] (rapid, non-specific, first line of defense) and adaptive immunity (slow, specific, capable of immunological memory). Together they coordinate pathogen detection, elimination, and resolution of infection while maintaining tolerance to self-tissues. The system's components span physical barriers (skin, mucosal epithelia), innate immune cells (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, NK cells, eosinophils, mast cells), adaptive immune cells (T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes), soluble factors (complement proteins, cytokines, antibodies), and specialized lymphoid organs (bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes, spleen, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue [MALT]). Disruption of any arm — whether through [[Immunosenescence|aging]], genetic defect, or pathogen evasion — can lead to increased susceptibility to [[Inflammation|infection]], autoimmunity, or cancer.

## Innate Immunity

The innate immune system provides immediate, non-specific defense through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect conserved molecular signatures on pathogens. The major PRR families include:

- **Toll-like receptors (TLRs):** 10 members in humans (TLR1–TLR10), expressed on cell surfaces (e.g., TLR4 recognizing LPS from gram-negative bacteria) and in endosomes (e.g., TLR3, TLR7/8, TLR9 detecting viral nucleic acids). Engagement triggers MyD88-dependent or TRIF-dependent signaling cascades.
- **NOD-like receptors (NLRs):** Cytoplasmic sensors including NOD1 and NOD2 (detecting peptidoglycan fragments) and NLRP3, which assembles the inflammasome complex — a multiprotein platform activating [[Caspases|caspase-1]], which cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into active forms.
- **RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs):** Cytoplasmic viral RNA sensors (RIG-I, MDA5) that signal through MAVS to induce type I interferon production, critical for antiviral defense.
- **C-type lectin receptors (CLRs):** Recognize carbohydrate structures on pathogens; Dectin-1 detects fungal β-glucan, while the mannose receptor mediates phagocytosis of diverse microbes.

PRRs recognize two broad classes of molecular patterns: pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from microorganisms and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from damaged or dying host cells (e.g., HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA, ATP). Upon detection, innate immune signaling converges on [[NFKB|NF-κB]] activation, driving transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines including [[TNFα]], IL-1β, and IL-6, collectively orchestrated through the [[Pro-inflammatory Cytokines]] network. The [[NLRP3]] inflammasome represents a critical node linking metabolic stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and sterile inflammation — particularly relevant in aging and metabolic disease.

The complement system provides humoral innate defense through three activation pathways: the classical pathway (antibody-dependent, C1q binding), the alternative pathway (spontaneous C3 hydrolysis on pathogen surfaces), and the lectin pathway (mannose-binding lectin recognizing pathogen carbohydrates). All three converge on C3 convertase formation, leading to C3b opsonization, C3a/C5a anaphylatoxin-mediated chemotaxis, and assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) that lyses pathogen membranes.

## Adaptive Immunity

Adaptive immunity provides antigen-specific responses with immunological memory. **T lymphocytes** mature in the thymus and differentiate into functional subsets:

- **CD4+ helper T cells:** Orchestrate immune responses by secreting cytokines that direct other immune cells. Th1 cells produce [[IFN-gamma]] to activate macrophages and promote cell-mediated immunity against intracellular pathogens. Th2 cells secrete IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 to drive humoral immunity and anti-helminth defense. Th17 cells produce IL-17 and IL-22, critical for mucosal defense against extracellular bacteria and fungi. T follicular helper (Tfh) cells support B cell germinal center reactions. Regulatory T cells (Treg), characterized by FoxP3 expression, suppress excessive immune responses and maintain self-tolerance through IL-10 and TGF-β secretion.
- **CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs):** Recognize intracellular pathogens and transformed cells via MHC class I molecules, killing targets through perforin/granzyme-mediated apoptosis and Fas/FasL interactions.

**B lymphocytes** produce antibodies (immunoglobulins) that neutralize pathogens, opsonize microbes for phagocytosis, and activate complement. The five immunoglobulin classes serve distinct roles: IgM is the first antibody produced during primary responses; IgG provides long-term systemic protection and crosses the placenta; IgA dominates mucosal immunity; IgE mediates anti-helminth defense and allergic responses; IgD functions primarily as a B cell surface receptor. B cells undergo class-switch recombination (CSR) to change antibody isotype and somatic hypermutation coupled with affinity maturation in germinal centers, producing high-affinity antibodies over time.

**Antigen presentation** bridges innate and adaptive immunity. MHC class I molecules present endogenous peptides (from intracellular pathogens or tumor antigens) to CD8+ CTLs, while MHC class II molecules present exogenous peptides (from phagocytosed material) to CD4+ helper T cells. Dendritic cells serve as the primary professional antigen-presenting cells, capturing antigens in peripheral tissues and migrating to lymph nodes to initiate adaptive responses.

**Immunological memory** is the hallmark of adaptive immunity. Following primary infection, long-lived memory T and B cells persist for years to decades, enabling faster, stronger secondary responses upon re-exposure. This principle underlies [[Vaccination|vaccination]] — the most impactful public health intervention in history, responsible for the eradication of smallpox and near-elimination of polio.

## Immunosenescence

[[Immunosenescence]] refers to the age-related decline in immune function, driven by thymic involution (progressive replacement of functional thymic tissue with adipose tissue beginning after puberty), reduced naive T cell output, contraction of T cell receptor diversity, and accumulation of senescent memory cells. These changes contribute to **[[Inflammaging]]** — the chronic, low-grade, sterile inflammation characteristic of aging — creating a paradoxical state of simultaneous immunosuppression and hyperinflammation.

Key hallmarks of immune aging include:

- **Thymic involution:** By age 50, the thymus has largely atrophied, reducing naive T cell production and narrowing the T cell repertoire available for responding to novel antigens.
- **T cell exhaustion and senescence:** Accumulation of CD28− CD57+ senescent T cells with shortened [[Telomere Attrition|telomeres]], reduced proliferative capacity, and a pro-inflammatory secretory profile.
- **Inflammaging:** Elevated basal levels of [[TNFα]], IL-6, and C-reactive protein, driven partly by [[NFKB|NF-κB]] activation from accumulated cellular damage, senescent cell [[SASP]] (senescence-associated secretory phenotype), and persistent CMV infection.
- **Impaired vaccine responses:** Elderly individuals produce lower antibody titers and weaker cellular immunity after vaccination, necessitating high-dose or adjuvanted formulations.
- **Increased susceptibility:** Higher rates of respiratory infections, reactivation of latent viruses (CMV, VZV), reduced cancer immunosurveillance, and poorer outcomes from [[SARS-CoV-2]] and other novel pathogens.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Immune dysfunction underlies a vast spectrum of human disease. **Autoimmune diseases** arise from loss of self-tolerance, with the immune system attacking host tissues: rheumatoid arthritis ([[Rheumatoid Arthritis|RA]]), systemic lupus erythematosus ([[SLE]]), multiple sclerosis ([[Multiple Sclerosis|MS]]), and type 1 diabetes (T1D) represent major autoimmune conditions. These disorders are treated with immunosuppressants, biologics (anti-TNF agents, JAK inhibitors), and emerging targeted therapies.

**Immunodeficiencies** are classified as primary (genetic) or secondary (acquired). Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) and X-linked SCID (due to IL2RG mutations) result in near-complete absence of functional T and B cells, requiring hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. [[HIV]] infection, by depleting CD4+ T cells, causes acquired immunodeficiency (AIDS), rendering individuals vulnerable to opportunistic infections and malignancies.

**Allergic diseases** and anaphylaxis result from IgE-mediated hypersensitivity responses, involving mast cell degranulation and release of histamine, leukotrienes, and other inflammatory mediators. Allergen immunotherapy aims to reprogram T cell responses toward tolerance.

**Transplant rejection** occurs when the recipient's T cells recognize donor MHC molecules as foreign; managing this requires lifelong immunosuppression with attendant infection risks.

**Cancer immunotherapy** has revolutionized oncology. Immune checkpoint inhibitors — monoclonal antibodies targeting CTLA-4 (ipilimumab), PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), or PD-L1 (atezolizumab) — release the brakes on anti-tumor T cell responses, producing durable remissions in melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and many other malignancies. CAR-T cell therapy genetically engineers a patient's own T cells to recognize tumor-specific antigens, achieving remarkable responses in hematological cancers.

**Immunotherapy in aging** is an emerging frontier. Senolytics (e.g., [[Dasatinib]] + [[Quercetin]], [[Navitoclax]]) selectively eliminate senescent cells, reducing SASP-driven [[Inflammation|inflammation]]. [[Rapamycin]] (mTORC1 inhibition) enhances immune function and vaccine responses in the elderly. [[Metformin]] may attenuate inflammaging through AMPK activation and NF-κB suppression. These interventions represent promising strategies for extending healthspan by targeting the immune-aging axis.
