# APP

[[Amyloid Precursor Protein]] (APP) is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein of approximately 100 kDa, consisting of a single transmembrane pass with a large N-terminal extracellular domain and a short C-terminal intracellular tail. APP is expressed ubiquitously across tissues, with the highest expression observed in [[Neurons]], where it localizes to synapses, endoplasmic reticulum, and the [[Golgi Apparatus]]. Alternative splicing of the *APP* gene (located on chromosome 21q21.3) produces three major isoforms: **APP695** (predominantly expressed in neurons and lacking exon 7 and 8), **APP751** (containing exon 7, which encodes a Kunitz-type serine protease inhibitor domain), and **APP770** (containing both exons 7 and 8, predominantly expressed in peripheral tissues). APP functions in cell adhesion, [[Neurite Outgrowth]], [[Synaptogenesis]], [[Copper]] binding, and [[Cholesterol]] transport. Its proteolytic processing by secretase enzymes generates the [[Amyloid Beta Peptide]] (Aβ), the principal component of senile plaques in [[Alzheimer's Disease]]. APP undergoes extensive post-translational modification including N- and O-glycosylation, phosphorylation, and tyrosine sulfation, which regulate its trafficking, stability, and processing. The protein is continuously produced and recycled through the secretory and endosomal pathways, with a turnover half-life of approximately 2–4 hours in neurons (O'Brien & Wong, 2011, PMID: 21816403).

## Structure & Domains

APP contains three major structural regions. The **N-terminal extracellular domain** (~600 residues) includes: the E1 domain, which harbors a growth factor-like region (GFLD) involved in homophilic and heterophilic [[Cell Adhesion]], a copper-binding domain (CuBD) that coordinates Cu²⁺ ions with high affinity (binding residues His677, His681, Tyr682, and His687) and participates in redox chemistry, and an E2 domain containing heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG)-binding motifs that mediate interactions with the extracellular matrix. The E1 domain is also responsible for APP dimerization at the cell surface. The **juxtamembrane region** contains a cluster of acidic residues and the β-secretase cleavage site (Asp671 in APP770 numbering). The **transmembrane domain** (~24 residues, residues 700–723 in APP770) spans the lipid bilayer and contains the γ-secretase cleavage site. Critically, the Aβ sequence spans residues 672–713 (Aβ42) or 672–711 (Aβ40), encompassing 28 residues of extracellular sequence and 12–14 residues within the transmembrane domain. The **C-terminal intracellular domain** (~47 residues, known as APP intracellular domain, AICD) contains the highly conserved YENPTY motif (Tyr728–Asn730–Pro731–Tyr732–Thr733), which serves as a binding site for adaptor proteins Fe65, X11/Mint, and JIP1. The AICD is released by γ-secretase cleavage and may function in transcriptional regulation, though this remains debated (Müller et al., 2017, PMID: 28592474).

## Mechanism of Action & Pathways

APP is processed through two competing proteolytic pathways. The **amyloidogenic pathway** begins with cleavage by [[Beta-Secretase 1]] (BACE1) at the Asp671 site on the luminal side of endosomes, releasing the soluble ectodomain fragment sAPPβ and generating a membrane-tethered C-terminal fragment (CTFβ/C99). BACE1 is an aspartyl protease optimally active at acidic pH (~4.5–5.5), which explains why amyloidogenic processing predominantly occurs in late endosomes and lysosomes. CTFβ is then cleaved within the membrane by the [[Gamma-Secretase]] complex (comprising presenilin 1 or 2, nicastrin, APH-1, and PEN-2), releasing the AICD and generating either Aβ40 (the more abundant, less aggregation-prone species) or Aβ42 (the more hydrophobic, aggregation-prone species). The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio is a critical determinant of amyloid plaque formation. Released Aβ monomers rapidly oligomerize into soluble oligomers (the most neurotoxic species), protofibrils, and eventually insoluble fibrils that deposit as [[Amyloid Plaques]].

The **non-amyloidogenic pathway** involves cleavage by [[ADAM10]] or ADAM17 (α-secretases) within the Aβ sequence (between Lys16 and Leu17 of Aβ), releasing the neuroprotective soluble fragment sAPPα and generating a membrane-tethered CTFα (C83). Subsequent γ-secretase cleavage of C83 produces the p3 peptide (Aβ17), which is non-toxic and does not aggregate. sAPPα activates signaling pathways that promote neuronal survival, synaptogenesis, and long-term potentiation (LTP). The balance between amyloidogenic and non-amyloidogenic processing is regulated by multiple factors including [[Lipid Rafts]], [[Clathrin]]-mediated endocytosis, and the availability of BACE1 versus ADAM10 at the cell surface (Haass et al., 2012, PMID: 22433616).

**Endosomal sorting** plays a central role: full-length APP is synthesized in the ER, trafficked through the Golgi, and delivered to the plasma membrane where it can be cleaved by ADAM10. APP is then internalized via clathrin-coated pits (mediated by the YENPTY motif and adaptor proteins such as AP-2) into early endosomes, where BACE1 cleavage occurs. From early endosomes, CTFβ may be recycled to the Golgi or directed to late endosomes for γ-secretase cleavage. Retrograde axonal transport delivers APP from distal synaptic sites to the soma. [[Lipid Rafts]]—cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched membrane microdomains—compartmentalize BACE1 and γ-secretase, promoting amyloidogenic processing. Cholesterol depletion (e.g., by statins) redirects APP away from lipid rafts, reducing Aβ production (Koo, 1999, PMID: 10548106).

## Physiological Function

Beyond its role as an amyloid precursor, APP serves several normal physiological functions. In **synaptic plasticity**, sAPPα enhances [[Long-Term Potentiation]] (LTP) and promotes dendritic spine formation. APP knockout mice exhibit reduced synaptic density, impaired LTP, and age-dependent cognitive deficits. In **neurite outgrowth**, APP functions as a cell adhesion molecule through homophilic interactions and binding to [[Heparan Sulfate Proteoglycans]], guiding axon extension and branching during development. In **copper homeostasis**, the CuBD of APP binds Cu²⁺ with nanomolar affinity and can reduce Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺, potentially protecting neurons from copper-mediated oxidative damage. APP also mediates **cholesterol efflux** by transferring cholesterol to apolipoprotein E (ApoE), and may regulate membrane lipid composition at synapses. The AICD fragment, upon release by γ-secretase, translocates to the nucleus in complex with Fe65 and Tip60 (histone acetyltransferase), where it may regulate gene expression including *BACE1* and *APP* itself, creating a feedback loop (Mattson, 1997, PMID: 9351715; Müller et al., 2017, PMID: 28592474).

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

### Alzheimer's Disease

APP is central to the **amyloid cascade hypothesis** of [[Alzheimer's Disease]], which posits that Aβ accumulation is the initiating event in AD pathogenesis. **Familial AD** (FAD) mutations in *APP* directly alter processing or Aβ aggregation properties. The **Swedish mutation** (KM670/671NL) is located adjacent to the BACE1 cleavage site, increasing β-secretase cleavage by 6–8-fold and elevating total Aβ production. The **London mutation** (V717I) and **Arctic mutation** (E693G) alter γ-secretase processing, increasing the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio or enhancing protofibril formation, respectively (Mullan et al., 1992, PMID: 1353622; Citron et al., 1992, PMID: 1354729). Mutations in [[Presenilin 1]] and [[Presenilin 2]] (components of the γ-secretase complex) account for the majority of early-onset FAD and similarly shift processing toward Aβ42. **Sporadic AD** involves increased APP expression, impaired Aβ clearance (mediated by [[Neprilin]], insulin-degrading enzyme, and microglial phagocytosis), and reduced sAPPα levels.

### Down Syndrome

Individuals with [[Down Syndrome]] (trisomy 21) carry an extra copy of the *APP* gene, resulting in 1.5-fold APP overexpression. This leads to Aβ accumulation beginning in the third decade of life, and virtually all individuals with Down syndrome develop AD neuropathology by age 40, supporting the amyloid hypothesis (Wisniewski et al., 1985, PMID: 2982823).

### Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy

Aβ deposition in the walls of cerebral blood vessels characterizes **cerebral amyloid angiopathy** (CAA), which causes lobar intracerebral hemorrhage, cortical superficial siderosis, and cognitive impairment in elderly individuals. CAA is found in >80% of AD brains and involves Aβ40 preferentially depositing in vascular smooth muscle and pericyte basement membranes.

### Therapeutic Targets

Multiple therapeutic strategies target the APP–Aβ axis:

- **BACE inhibitors** (verubecestat, lanabecestat, atabecestat): Reduce Aβ production but failed in Phase III trials due to cognitive worsening, likely from off-target effects on other BACE1 substrates including neuregulin-1 (Egan et al., 2018, PMID: 29746300).
- **γ-Secretase inhibitors** (semagacestat, tarenflurbil): Also failed in trials; semagacestat increased mortality and cognitive decline, partly due to Notch pathway inhibition.
- **Anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies**: [[Aducanumab]] (FDA approved 2021, accelerated approval), [[Lecanemab]] (FDA approved 2023, full approval), and [[Donanemab]] (FDA approved 2024) target aggregated Aβ species. Lecanemab and donanemab demonstrated modest but statistically significant slowing of cognitive decline (27–35% reduction in decline over 18 months) in Phase III trials (van Dyck et al., 2023, PMID: 36882058; Sims et al., 2023, PMID: 37310918).
- **APP-targeting antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)**: Under preclinical and early clinical investigation to reduce APP expression at the mRNA level.
