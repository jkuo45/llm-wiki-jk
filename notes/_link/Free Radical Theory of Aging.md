## Overview

The free radical theory of aging was proposed by [[Denham Harman]] in 1955, postulating that [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species (ROS)]] produced as byproducts of [[Aerobic Respiration|aerobic respiration]] cause cumulative oxidative damage to [[DNA]], proteins, and [[Lipid|lipids]], leading to progressive cellular dysfunction and the aging phenotype. The theory was later refined as the [[Mitochondrial Free Radical Theory of Aging]] to specifically implicate [[Mitochondria]] as the primary source and target of ROS.

## State of the Field

> [!info] Source: [[_document_ - Mitochondrial dysfunction in cellular senescence a bridge to neurodegenerative disease]]
> Efforts to validate the MFRTA have yielded mixed results. While longer-lived species generally produce fewer [[Superoxide|superoxide]] and [[Hydrogen Peroxide|H2O2]], [[Antioxidant]] supplementation and genetic manipulation of antioxidant enzymes have not consistently extended lifespan across model organisms. The field has since shifted to view oxidative damage as one of many interconnected [[Hallmarks of Aging|aging hallmarks]] rather than a sole primary driver, with ROS also serving important signaling functions.
