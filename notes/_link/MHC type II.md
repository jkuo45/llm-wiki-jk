# MHC type II

Major Histocompatibility Complex class II (MHC II) molecules are heterodimeric cell-surface glycoproteins composed of an α chain (~33 kDa) and a β chain (~28 kDa), expressed primarily on professional [[Antigen Presenting Cells]] (APCs): [[Dendritic Cells]], [[Macrophages]], and [[B Cells]]. Their central function is to bind peptide fragments derived from exogenous proteins (typically 13–25 amino acids in length) and present them on the cell surface for recognition by [[CD4+]] T helper cells, thereby initiating and orchestrating [[Adaptive Immunity]]. In humans, the MHC II locus is located on chromosome 6p21 within the HLA-D region and encodes classical molecules ([[HLA-DR]], [[HLA-DP]], [[HLA-DQ]]) as well as non-classical, regulatory molecules ([[HLA-DM]], [[HLA-DO]]). MHC II is indispensable for thymic selection of the CD4+ T cell repertoire and for peripheral immune responses against extracellular pathogens, allergens, and tumor antigens.

## Structure & Domains

Each MHC II molecule is a heterodimer of non-covalently associated α and β polypeptide chains. Both chains span the membrane once and contribute to the extracellular peptide-binding groove. The extracellular portion is organized into four domains: the peptide-binding groove is formed by the α1 and β1 domains at the membrane-distal end, while the α2 and β2 domains are membrane-proximal immunoglobulin-like (Ig-like) C1-type domains that provide structural stability and serve as the docking site for the [[CD4]] co-receptor on T cells. Below the α2 domain, each chain has a short transmembrane helix and a cytoplasmic tail involved in intracellular trafficking and signaling.

The peptide-binding groove of MHC II is open-ended at both ends, unlike the closed groove of [[MHC type I]], which allows it to accommodate peptides of variable length (13–25 residues or more). Peptides are held in an extended conformation by hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts along the groove floor. Specific peptide side chains insert into designated pockets along the groove, with the P1, P4, P6, and P9 pockets serving as the principal anchors. The P1 pocket is the largest and most influential determinant of peptide-binding specificity, typically accommodating large hydrophobic or aromatic residues.

The three major classical MHC II isotypes differ in their chain composition and polymorphism patterns. [[HLA-DR]] is the most abundantly expressed and most polymorphic isotype: the α chain (DRA) is essentially monomorphic across the human population, whereas the β chain (DRB1, and other DRB loci) is highly polymorphic. HLA-DR thus displays enormous allelic diversity at the protein level, with over 2,000 known DRB1 alleles. [[HLA-DQ]] features polymorphism in both the α chain (DQA1) and the β chain (DQB1), and DQ molecules can form both cis- and trans-heterodimers, further expanding peptide-binding diversity. [[HLA-DP]] also has polymorphic α (DPA1) and β (DPB1) chains, with DP molecules playing a less prominent but non-negligible role in antigen presentation. The non-classical molecule [[HLA-DM]] (DMA/DMB) lacks a classical peptide-binding groove of its own but instead catalyzes peptide exchange on classical MHC II molecules. [[HLA-DO]] (DOA/DOB) is a regulatory heterodimer that modulates HLA-DM activity.

## Antigen Processing & Presentation

MHC II molecules present peptides derived from exogenous antigens — proteins internalized by endocytosis, phagocytosis, or receptor-mediated uptake. After internalization, antigens are delivered to progressively acidifying endosomal and lysosomal compartments, where they are degraded by a battery of acid-dependent proteases, principally [[Cathepsin]] family members (cathepsins S, L, B, D, and H). The resulting peptide fragments are trimmed to optimal length for loading onto MHC II.

MHC II αβ chains are synthesized in the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]] (ER) and associate with a non-polypeptide-invariant chain (Ii, encoded by the [[CD74]] gene). The invariant chain serves three critical functions: (1) it blocks premature peptide binding to the groove by inserting a segment called CLIP (Class II-associated Invariant chain Peptide) into the binding cleft; (2) it directs MHC II trafficking from the ER through the Golgi to the endosomal/lysosomal compartment via its cytoplasmic tail sorting signals; and (3) it promotes proper folding and oligomerization of MHC II complexes. Within the endosomal compartment, the invariant chain is sequentially degraded by cathepsins, leaving only the CLIP fragment occupying the peptide-binding groove.

[[HLA-DM]] (encoded by DMA and DMB genes) is a non-classical MHC II molecule that functions as a peptide editor. It catalyzes the release of CLIP and facilitates the binding of high-affinity antigenic peptides. HLA-DM operates in the endosomal compartment (MIIC, MHC class II compartment), where it samples MHC II–peptide complexes and preferentially removes low-affinity or kinetically unstable peptides, thereby enriching for the most stable and immunogenic peptide–MHC II combinations. This peptide-editing function is essential for generating a repertoire of stably loaded complexes that can withstand the rigors of T cell recognition. [[HLA-DO]] (encoded by DOA and DOB) is a non-classical heterodimer that associates with HLA-DM and modulates its peptide-editing activity. HLA-DO acts as a negative regulator of DM under certain conditions, particularly at neutral pH, and its expression varies among cell types and developmental stages.

Once loaded with antigenic peptide, the stable MHC II–peptide complex is transported to the cell surface. There, it is recognized by the [[T Cell Receptor]] (TCR) on [[CD4+]] T helper cells. Effective T cell activation requires two signals: Signal 1 is the TCR–peptide–MHC II interaction, and Signal 2 is co-stimulation, primarily through [[CD80]]/[[CD86]] on the APC engaging [[CD28]] on the T cell. Without co-stimulation, TCR engagement leads to anergy or tolerance rather than activation.

## Physiological Function

The fundamental physiological role of MHC II is the initiation and regulation of [[CD4+]] T cell–mediated adaptive immune responses. CD4+ T cells activated by MHC II–peptide complexes on APCs differentiate into functionally distinct subsets, each orchestrating distinct arms of immunity:

- **[[Th1]] responses**: Driven by [[IL-12]] and [[IFN-gamma]], Th1 cells activate macrophages via IFN-γ to enhance intracellular killing of phagocytosed pathogens. This arm is critical for defense against intracellular bacteria and parasites.
- **Th2 responses**: Driven by [[IL-4]], Th2 cells promote B cell class switching to [[IgE]], eosinophil activation, and mucus secretion — essential for defense against helminths and implicated in allergic disease.
- **Th17 responses**: Driven by [[IL-6]], [[TGF-beta]], and [[IL-23]], Th17 cells recruit neutrophils and promote antimicrobial peptide production at mucosal surfaces, serving as a first line of defense against extracellular bacteria and fungi.
- **T Follicular Helper (Tfh) cells**: Localized in germinal centers, Tfh cells provide critical help to [[B Cells]] for affinity maturation, class-switch recombination, and the generation of long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells.
- **Regulatory T cells ([[Treg]])**: Induced by [[TGF-beta]] and [[IL-2]], Tregs suppress excessive immune responses and maintain self-tolerance, preventing autoimmunity.

MHC II is also upregulated on virtually all cell types by [[IFN-gamma]], allowing non-professional APCs (e.g., epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts) to present antigens under inflammatory conditions, potentially broadening the scope of immune surveillance.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

### Autoimmune Disease

Extensive genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and historical linkage analyses have firmly established MHC II as the single greatest genetic determinant of autoimmune disease susceptibility. Specific HLA-D alleles confer risk or protection for numerous autoimmune conditions by shaping the repertoire of self-peptides presented to CD4+ T cells:

- **Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)**: HLA-DRB1 alleles encoding the *shared epitope* — a conserved amino acid sequence (positions 70–74) in the DRβ1 chain — confer strong susceptibility. The shared epitope alters the P4 pocket chemistry, favoring presentation of citrullinated self-peptides. [[Citrullination]] of arginine residues in proteins such as fibrinogen and vimentin generates neo-antigens recognized by autoreactive CD4+ T cells, driving the anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA)–positive form of RA.
- **Celiac Disease**: HLA-DQ2 (DQA1*05:01/DQB1*02:01) and HLA-DQ8 (DQA1*03:01/DQB1*03:02) are required (though not sufficient) for disease. Deamidated gluten peptides (modified by tissue transglutaminase) bind with high affinity to the DQ2/DQ8 groove, triggering T cell–mediated intestinal inflammation.
- **Type 1 Diabetes**: HLA-DR3/DR4 and the trans-encoded DQ2/DQ8 heterodimers confer the highest risk. Specific polymorphisms in the P9 pocket alter presentation of islet autoantigens (insulin, GAD65, IA-2).
- **Multiple Sclerosis**: HLA-DRB1*15:01 is the strongest genetic risk factor, associated with presentation of myelin-derived peptides (myelin basic protein, myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein).

### Transplant Rejection

MHC II mismatch between donor and recipient is a major driver of [[Allograft Rejection]]. Donor-derived APCs (passenger leukocytes) expressing mismatched MHC II can directly activate recipient CD4+ T cells (direct allorecognition), while recipient APCs that capture and present donor MHC II molecules (indirect allorecognition) also contribute. Preformed anti-MHC II antibodies (donor-specific antibodies, DSA) are a significant risk factor for antibody-mediated rejection, particularly in kidney transplantation.

### MHC II Deficiency (Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome Type II)

Homozygous loss-of-function mutations in genes encoding MHC II transactivators (CIITA, RFXANK, RFX5, RFXAP) or in the MHC II structural genes themselves cause Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome type II (BLS II), a rare autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. Patients lack MHC II expression on APCs and consequently have severely impaired CD4+ T cell development and function, resulting in profound susceptibility to opportunistic infections and early mortality without hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

### Tumor Immune Evasion and Immunotherapy

Many cancers downregulate MHC II expression on tumor cells as an immune evasion strategy, reducing CD4+ T cell recognition and辅助 support for CD8+ cytotoxic T cell responses. Administration of [[Interferon-gamma]] (IFN-γ) or [[Interferon-alpha]] can upregulate MHC II on tumor cells, enhancing their immunogenicity. In the context of [[Checkpoint Inhibitor]] therapy, MHC II expression on tumors is correlated with improved response rates and better prognosis in several cancer types, including [[Melanoma]] and renal cell carcinoma. Understanding the MHC II–peptide–TCR axis continues to inform peptide vaccine design and adoptive T cell therapies targeting CD4+ epitopes.
