# Glucagon

## Overview

Glucagon is a 29-amino-acid peptide hormone secreted by the alpha cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans. It is encoded by the *GCG* gene (proglucagon) and is the principal counter-regulatory hormone to [[Insulin]], maintaining fasting [[Glucose]] homeostasis during hypoglycemia, fasting, and exercise. Glucagon signals through the G protein-coupled glucagon receptor (GCGR), expressed predominantly in the [[Liver]], kidney, adipose tissue, and heart.

## Mechanism of Function

Upon binding GCGR, glucagon activates the Gs–adenylyl cyclase–cAMP–PKA cascade, leading to phosphorylation and inhibition of glycogen synthase and activation of glycogen phosphorylase via phosphorylase kinase. This drives hepatic glycogenolysis. cAMP also induces the transcription of gluconeogenic enzymes (PEPCK, G6Pase) via CREB and the co-activator PGC-1α, increasing gluconeogenesis. Additional effects include stimulation of hepatic ketogenesis and lipolysis in [[Adipose Tissue]]. The glucagon–insulin ratio thus sets the net direction of hepatic glucose output; a high ratio favors glucose production, while a low ratio favors storage.

## Pathological and Clinical Relevance

In [[Metabolic Syndrome]] and [[Obesity]], alpha-cell dysfunction and relative glucagon excess contribute to fasting hyperglycemia and postprandial glucose dysregulation. Glucagon suppression is a therapeutic goal in type 2 diabetes, achieved by [[Glucagon-like Peptide-1]] (GLP-1) receptor agonists that inhibit glucagon secretion. Conversely, pharmacologic glucagon is used to treat severe hypoglycemia and is under investigation as a treatment for glycogen storage disease and obesity (glucagon receptor antagonism promotes weight loss). Dysregulated glucagon signaling is also implicated in the hypercatabolic state of burn and trauma.
