# Complex IV

**Overview:** [[Complex IV]] (cytochrome c oxidase, COX) is the terminal enzyme of the [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] [[Electron Transport Chain|electron transport chain]], embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It receives electrons from reduced [[Cytochrome c]] and reduces molecular oxygen to water.

**Structure and Mechanism:** Mammalian Complex IV is a 13-subunit metalloenzyme containing copper centers (Cu_A, Cu_B) and heme a and heme a₃. Electrons pass from cytochrome c → Cu_A → heme a → heme a₃–Cu_B binuclear center, where O₂ is bound and sequentially reduced to two H₂O molecules. This redox chemistry pumps four protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space per O₂, contributing to the proton-motive force that drives [[ATP Synthase|ATP synthesis]].

**Physiological Function:** As the point where the chain consumes O₂, Complex IV is the ultimate electron sink of aerobic respiration and a major determinant of [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]] capacity. Its activity integrates with [[Mitohormesis]]—mild respiratory inhibition can trigger adaptive stress responses.

**Pathology and Clinical Relevance:** Inherited COX deficiencies (due to mitochondrial or nuclear subunit mutations) cause myopathies, encephalopathies, and [[Leigh Syndrome]]. Acquired dysfunction elevates [[Mitochondrial ROS]] and is implicated in [[Neurodegenerative Diseases]] ([[Parkinson's Disease]], [[Alzheimer's Disease]]) and aging. Pharmacologic modulators include [[Methylene Blue]] (electron cycler that can bypass proximal ETC blocks) and compounds influencing redox tone.
