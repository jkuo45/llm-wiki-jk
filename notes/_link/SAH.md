## Overview

[[SAH]] (S-adenosylhomocysteine) is the hydrolysis product formed when S-adenosylmethionine ([[SAM]]) donates its methyl group in transmethylation reactions. It is a potent competitive inhibitor of nearly all methyltransferases, making the SAM/SAH ratio a critical determinant of cellular methylation capacity.

## Biological Role / Mechanism

After methyl transfer, SAM becomes SAH, which is hydrolyzed by S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase (SAHH) into homocysteine and adenosine. The removal of SAH (via the methionine cycle and remethylation/transsulfuration) maintains a high SAM:SAH ratio required for efficient methylation of DNA, histones, phospholipids, and small molecules. Accumulation of SAH (e.g., via SAHH inhibition by [[3-Deazaadenosine]] or [[Neplanocin A]]) globally suppresses methylation and perturbs epigenetic and metabolic homeostasis.

## Physiological / Pathological Relevance

Elevated SAH is associated with hyperhomocysteinemia, cardiovascular risk, and altered epigenetic regulation in aging and neurodegeneration. Modulating SAH levels influences [[Histone Methylation]] and DNA methylation. In the wiki, SAH is tracked among senescent-compound datasets as a methylation-intermediate relevant to epigenetic and metabolic interventions.
