# Aminoguanidine HCL

**Aminoguanidine HCL** (aminoguanidine hydrochloride) is the hydrochloride salt form of [[Aminoguanidine]], with the molecular formula CH₆N₄·HCl and a molecular weight of 110.57 g/mol (free base: 74.09 g/mol). It is the preferred salt for aqueous and supplemental formulations due to its vastly superior water solubility compared to the [[Aminoguanidine Bicarbonate|bicarbonate salt]].

In the adrenochrome protocol, aminoguanidine HCL serves two primary roles: **controlling [[Adrenochrome|adrenochrome]] quinone reactivity** by forming the stable derivative [[Adrenochrome monoaminoguanidine]], and **inhibiting [[Advanced Glycation End Products]] (AGEs)** as part of the [[Glyco-Oxidative Proteostasis Shield]] (GOPS) strategy.

> [!info] HCL vs. Bicarbonate salt
> Aminoguanidine hydrochloride is highly soluble in water, making it preferred for water-based formulations and oral supplementation where rapid dissolution is required. The bicarbonate form (CAS 2582-30-1) offers higher shelf stability and is chloride-free, but its lower solubility makes it less practical for oral use. Both salts deliver the identical active aminoguanidine moiety. The HCL form is hygroscopic and must be stored below +30°C in a sealed container.

## Mechanism of Action

### AGE Inhibition (Carbonyl Trapping)

Aminoguanidine is the **prototype therapeutic agent** for preventing AGE formation. It acts as a nucleophilic carbonyl trap, reacting rapidly with reactive α,β-dicarbonyl intermediates — primarily [[Methylglyoxal]], [[Glyoxal]], and 3-deoxyglucosone — to form stable, non-toxic 3-amino-1,2,4-triazine adducts. By intercepting these reactive intermediates before they can cross-link with proteins, aminoguanidine prevents the accumulation of AGEs that drive vascular stiffening, basement membrane thickening, and tissue damage in aging and diabetes.

> [!warning] Selectivity threshold
> At concentrations above ~500 μM *in vitro*, aminoguanidine loses selectivity and begins reacting with pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6), pyruvate, glucose, and malondialdehyde. Peak plasma concentration in clinical therapy was ~50 μM, well below this threshold (Thornalley, 2003).

### iNOS Inhibition (Upstream NO Blockade)

Aminoguanidine selectively inhibits [[Inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase]] (iNOS), the isoform responsible for pathological NO overproduction during inflammation. It acts **upstream** on the NO–cGMP vasodilation cascade, blocking NO synthesis at the source. This complements [[Methylene Blue]], which acts **downstream** by inhibiting [[Soluble Guanylyl Cyclase]] to block cGMP generation even when NO is already present.

> [!info] Selective iNOS inhibition
> Because aminoguanidine spares the constitutive endothelial isoform ([[Endothelial Nitric Oxide Synthase|eNOS]]), it is theorized to halt inflammatory vasodilation without abolishing normal blood-pressure regulation — unlike non-selective NOS blockade (Corbett et al., 1992).

### DAO / SSAO Inhibition

Aminoguanidine inhibits [[Diamine Oxidase]] (DAO) and semicarbazide-sensitive amine oxidase (SSAO/VAP-1). DAO inhibition in the gut is the primary mechanism behind the histamine intolerance side effect (see Safety). SSAO inhibition reduces H₂O₂ production in vascular endothelium, which may contribute anti-atherosclerotic effects but also complicates interpretation of anti-glycation studies.

### Quinone Stabilization (Adrenochrome Protocol)

Aminoguanidine reacts with the unstable o-quinone structure of [[Adrenochrome]] to form [[Adrenochrome monoaminoguanidine]] (AMM), a stable crystalline derivative. This reaction:
- **Stabilizes adrenochrome** by trapping the reactive quinone with the hydrazine group
- **Preserves the adrenochrome moiety's** hormetic redox activity while eliminating uncontrolled reactivity
- **Adds AGE inhibition** via the aminoguanidine arm of the hybrid molecule

AMM is the central modulator component of the [[SASP-Remodeling Aminochrome Complex]] (SRAC) strategy, replacing free adrenochrome (which required nanoparticle delivery) with a pre-formed complex that provides biochemical selectivity via carbonyl trapping in senescent cells.

## Role in the Adrenochrome Protocol

In the adrenochrome (MB, AG) protocol, aminoguanidine HCL is paired with [[Methylene Blue]] to achieve complementary control over the NO–cGMP cascade:

- **Aminoguanidine (upstream):** iNOS inhibition → reduces pathological NO production
- **Methylene Blue (downstream):** sGC inhibition → blocks cGMP-mediated vasodilation

Together with adrenochrome's hormetic ROS pulse (via the [[Mitohormetic Redox-Relay]]), aminoguanidine HCL provides the **GOPS (Glycation and AGEs, ER, peroxisomes)** arm of the protocol — controlling glyco-oxidative damage and quinone reactivity while methylene blue provides the **MRR (Mitohormetic Redox Relay)** arm.

## Clinical History (Pimagedine)

Aminoguanidine was studied under the developmental name **Pimagedine** (INN) for the prevention of diabetic complications, particularly [[Diabetic Nephropathy]]. Two landmark Phase III trials were conducted:

### ACTION I (Type 1 Diabetes)

- 150 mg twice daily (300 mg/day) or 300 mg twice daily (600 mg/day) vs. placebo
- **Primary endpoint** (time to doubling of serum creatinine): not significant (p=0.099)
- **Secondary findings:** Slower decline in eGFR (p=0.05); significant reduction in 24-hour proteinuria (p<0.001 at 150 mg BID); delayed retinopathy progression (p=0.03)
- Trial completed but did not meet primary endpoint

### ACTION II (Type 2 Diabetes)

- Terminated prematurely due to futility and higher incidence of adverse events
- Safety concerns: flu-like symptoms, gastrointestinal events, impaired liver function, lupus-like syndrome, ANCA-associated vasculitis

### Outcome

Pimagedine development was discontinued in 1999. The compound provided proof-of-concept that AGE inhibition could slow diabetic complications, but safety liabilities prevented regulatory approval. It remains an experimental/research tool.

## Pharmacokinetics

- **Oral absorption:** Well absorbed from the GI tract; historically administered as tablets
- **Peak plasma concentration:** ~50 μM at therapeutic doses (150–300 mg BID)
- **Metabolism:** Partially metabolized in the liver; reacts with many biological nucleophiles
- **Typical clinical dose:** 2–8 mg/kg/day (approximately 150–300 mg twice daily)
- **Animal research doses:** 50–150 mg/kg/day (IV or IP)

## Safety and Side Effects

> [!warning] Histamine intolerance (oral)
> Oral aminoguanidine potently inhibits gut [[Diamine Oxidase]], the enzyme that degrades dietary histamine. This can cause severe acute [[Histamine Intolerance]] — headaches, flushing, hives, and tachycardia after histamine-rich foods (aged cheese, cured meats, fermented foods). A low-histamine diet is essential during oral use.

Additional safety concerns from clinical trials:

- **[[Vitamin B6]] depletion** — aminoguanidine reacts with pyridoxal phosphate at high concentrations; B6 supplementation may be warranted
- **Pro-oxidant activities** — at high concentrations, can generate free radicals via NOS inhibition
- **Autoimmune reactions** — ANCA-associated vasculitis and lupus-like syndrome reported in Phase III trials
- **Liver function impairment** — elevated liver enzymes observed in ACTION II
