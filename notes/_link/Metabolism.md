# Metabolism

Metabolism encompasses all chemical reactions occurring within living organisms to sustain life, broadly divided into [[Catabolism]] (the breakdown of complex molecules to release energy and provide simpler building blocks) and [[Anabolism]] (the synthesis of cellular components from simpler precursors, requiring energy input). These two arms operate simultaneously and are tightly coordinated to maintain cellular and organismal homeostasis.

Central metabolic pathways include [[Glycolysis]], the [[TCA Cycle]], [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]], [[Fatty Acid Oxidation]], fatty acid synthesis, [[Amino Acid]] metabolism, and [[Nucleotide]] metabolism. The overall metabolic rate of an organism is quantified as the basal metabolic rate (BMR), typically ranging from 1,500–2,000 kcal/day in adults. BMR is influenced by age, sex, body composition, hormonal status (particularly [[Thyroid Hormones]] T3/T4), and environmental temperature. Metabolic demand increases during exercise, infection, growth, and pregnancy, reflecting the dynamic nature of energy expenditure.

## Central Metabolic Pathways

**Glycolysis** is the universal glucose catabolic pathway occurring in the cytoplasm. One molecule of [[Glucose]] is converted into two molecules of pyruvate, yielding a net gain of 2 ATP (via substrate-level phosphorylation) and 2 NADH. Pyruvate then enters the mitochondria where it is oxidized to [[Acetyl-CoA]] by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, feeding into the [[TCA Cycle]] (also known as the citric acid cycle or Krebs cycle). Each turn of the TCA cycle, occurring in the mitochondrial matrix, produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, and 1 GTP (equivalent to 1 ATP), while completely oxidizing acetyl-CoA to CO₂. The reduced cofactors NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to the [[Electron Transport Chain]] (Complexes I–IV), which establishes a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. This gradient drives ATP synthase (F₁F₀-ATPase) to produce approximately 30–32 ATP molecules per glucose molecule through [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]].

**Fatty acid β-oxidation** sequentially removes two-carbon units from fatty acyl-CoA chains in the mitochondrial matrix (and peroxisomes for very-long-chain fatty acids), generating acetyl-CoA, NADH, and FADH₂. The acetyl-CoA produced enters the TCA cycle, making fatty acids a highly efficient energy source. **Fatty acid synthesis** occurs in the cytoplasm, where acetyl-CoA is carboxylated to malonyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase and then elongated by fatty acid synthase. The **pentose phosphate pathway** (hexose monophosphate shunt) branches from glycolysis at glucose-6-phosphate, generating NADPH for reductive biosynthesis and [[Antioxidant]] defense (glutathione reduction), as well as ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis.

**Amino acid metabolism** involves transamination, deamination, and the urea cycle to handle nitrogen disposal. Glucogenic amino acids feed into TCA cycle intermediates, while ketogenic amino acids yield acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate. **Nucleotide metabolism** encompasses both de novo synthesis and salvage pathways for purines and pyrimidines, essential for DNA replication and RNA transcription.

## Metabolic Regulation

Metabolic flux is regulated at multiple levels—allosteric, hormonal, transcriptional, and via energy-sensing networks.

**Allosteric regulation** provides rapid, moment-to-moment control. Key examples include [[Phosphofructokinase-1]] (PFK-1), the rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis, which is allosterically activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is regulated by product inhibition (NADH, acetyl-CoA) and by phosphorylation/dephosphorylation controlled by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase and phosphatase.

**Hormonal control** coordinates metabolism at the organismal level. [[Insulin]], secreted by pancreatic β-cells in the fed state, promotes anabolic processes: glucose uptake (via GLUT4 translocation), glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis, and protein synthesis. It suppresses catabolic pathways such as gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, and lipolysis. In contrast, [[Glucagon]] (from α-cells during fasting) and [[Cortisol]] (a glucocorticoid) drive catabolic and gluconeogenic pathways. Epinephrine stimulates glycogenolysis and lipolysis during stress.

**Energy sensing** is mediated by several kinase and deacetylase networks. [[AMPK]] (AMP-activated protein kinase) acts as a cellular fuel gauge; when the AMP:ATP ratio rises (energy deficit), AMPK is activated and stimulates catabolic pathways (fatty acid oxidation, glucose uptake, autophagy) while inhibiting anabolic processes (lipogenesis, protein synthesis). [[mTORC1]] integrates signals from amino acids, growth factors, and energy sufficiency to promote anabolic processes including protein synthesis, lipid synthesis, and cell growth. When nutrients are scarce, mTORC1 is suppressed, allowing catabolic recycling through [[Autophagy]].

[[Sirtuins]] (SIRT1–SIRT7) are NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases that link metabolic status to epigenetic regulation and stress responses. SIRT1 deacetylates PGC-1α to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and enhances insulin sensitivity. SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and ROS detoxification. SIRT6 suppresses lipogenesis and promotes DNA repair.

**Transcriptional regulation** involves key nuclear receptors and transcription factors. [[PGC-1α]] (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha) is the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, activated by exercise, cold exposure, and fasting. [[HIF-1α]] (hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha) shifts metabolism toward glycolysis under low oxygen conditions and is frequently upregulated in [[Cancer]]. SREBP (sterol regulatory element-binding proteins) activate genes for cholesterol and fatty acid synthesis. ChREBP (carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein) drives glucose-responsive lipogenesis in liver and adipose tissue.

## Metabolic Flexibility

Metabolic flexibility refers to the ability of cells and organisms to efficiently switch between glucose and fatty acid oxidation depending on nutrient availability and energy demands. In the fed state, tissues preferentially oxidize glucose; during fasting, a metabolic shift toward fatty acid oxidation occurs, sparing glucose for glucose-dependent tissues like the brain.

Loss of metabolic flexibility—metabolic inflexibility—is a hallmark of [[Insulin Resistance]] and [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]]. In insulin-resistant states, impaired glucose uptake by muscle and adipose tissue leads to compensatory increases in fatty acid oxidation, elevating circulating free fatty acids. Excess lipid accumulation in non-adipose tissues (liver, muscle, pancreas) drives lipotoxicity, contributing to hepatic steatosis, β-cell dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease.

**Tissue-specific metabolism** reflects specialized physiological roles. The [[Brain]] depends primarily on glucose under normal conditions but can utilize ketone bodies (β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate) during prolonged fasting or ketogenic diets. The [[Heart]] derives approximately 60–70% of its ATP from fatty acid oxidation, making it exquisitely sensitive to metabolic perturbations. The [[Liver]] plays a central role in glucose homeostasis through gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, while also serving as the primary site of de novo lipogenesis and cholesterol metabolism. Skeletal [[Muscle]] is metabolically flexible, shifting between glucose and fatty acid oxidation depending on contraction state and nutrient availability.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Metabolic dysfunction underlies many of the most prevalent diseases worldwide. **Metabolic syndrome** is a cluster of interrelated conditions—central [[Obesity]], insulin resistance, dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol), and [[Hypertension]]—that collectively increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]]. The pathophysiology involves chronic low-grade inflammation, ectopic lipid deposition, and mitochondrial dysfunction.

**Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)**, now increasingly referred to as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), ranges from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH/MASH), with potential progression to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. It represents the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome.

**Inborn errors of metabolism** are genetic disorders affecting specific metabolic enzymes. Phenylketonuria (PKU) results from deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase, leading to intellectual disability if untreated. Maple syrup urine disease involves branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase deficiency. Galactosemia results from galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase deficiency. Newborn screening and dietary management have transformed outcomes for many of these conditions.

**Cancer metabolism** is characterized by the [[Warburg Effect]]—the preference of many tumor cells for aerobic glycolysis (glycolysis with lactate production even in the presence of adequate oxygen). This metabolic reprogramming supports rapid biomass accumulation and provides biosynthetic intermediates for nucleotide, amino acid, and lipid synthesis. Oncogenic signaling through [[PI3K]]/[[Akt]]/[[mTOR]] and [[HIF-1α]] drives metabolic rewiring.

**Mitochondrial diseases** arise from mutations in mitochondrial DNA or nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial proteins, impairing oxidative phosphorylation and causing multisystem disorders with elevated lactate, neuromuscular dysfunction, and organ failure.

**Aging-associated metabolic decline** involves reduced mitochondrial function, decreased [[NAD+]] levels, impaired [[AMPK]] signaling, and reduced [[Autophagy]] efficiency. These changes contribute to insulin resistance, sarcopenia, and increased susceptibility to metabolic disease in older adults.

**Therapeutic targeting** of metabolic pathways is an active area of pharmacology. [[Metformin]], a first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes, activates AMPK and inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis. [[Rapamycin]] (sirolimus) inhibits [[mTORC1]], extending lifespan in model organisms and currently under investigation for anti-aging effects. Sirtuin-activating compounds (STACs) such as resveratrol activate SIRT1, though clinical translation remains challenging. NAD⁺ precursors including nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) aim to restore age-depleted NAD⁺ levels, with promising preclinical and early clinical data.
