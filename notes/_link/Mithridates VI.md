# Mithridates VI

**Mithridates VI Eupator** (c. 134–63 BCE), ruler of the Kingdom of Pontus in northern Anatolia, is one of history's most famous figures in toxicology and pharmacology. Fearing assassination by poisoning, he reportedly ingested gradually increasing, sub-lethal doses of toxins — a regimen later called [[Mithridatism]] — intending to render himself immune to lethal poisons.

## Historical & Scientific Significance

Mithridates' practice is the archetypal anecdote behind the biological principle of [[Hormesis]]: that low doses of a stressor or toxin can induce adaptive protective responses, whereas high doses are harmful. His legendary universal antidote, the [[Antidotum Mithridaticum]], blended dozens of botanical and animal substances and influenced antidotal medicine for centuries. The episode also resonates with [[Xenohormesis]] — the idea that organisms sense and benefit from plant stress metabolites.

## Legacy

Although Mithridates' actual immunity is debated (he ultimately failed to poison himself and died by sword), his story seeded enduring concepts in toxicology, tolerance, and preconditioning. Modern hormesis research — including [[Mitohormesis]] — formalizes the same intuition: controlled low-level stress (oxidative, caloric, exercise) stimulates resilience and extends healthspan.
