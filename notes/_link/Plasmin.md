# Plasmin

**Plasmin** is a broad-spectrum serine protease of the trypsin-like family, generated from its inactive zymogen [[Plasminogen]] by tissue-type (tPA) and urokinase-type ([[uPA]]) plasminogen activators. It is the central effector of the fibrinolytic system, responsible for degrading fibrin clots, but also plays critical roles in [[Extracellular Matrix|ECM]] remodelling, [[Inflammation|inflammatory]] cell recruitment, complement activation, and pro-matrix metalloproteinase processing.

## Gene & Protein Structure

- **Gene:** *PLG* is located on chromosome 6q26, spanning approximately 52 kb with 19 exons.
- **Protein:** Plasminogen is a 92 kDa single-chain glycoprotein synthesised in the liver and secreted into plasma (plasma concentration ~2 µM). It circulates in a closed, looped conformation held together by internal lysine-binding sites.
- **Activation:** Plasminogen is converted to the active two-chain plasmin by cleavage at Arg561-Val562. tPA cleaves plasminogen efficiently only when it is bound to fibrin (fibrin-specific fibrinolysis), whereas [[uPA]] activates plasminogen at the cell surface independent of fibrin.
- **Active form:** Plasmin consists of a heavy chain (kringles 1-5, containing lysine-binding sites) and a light chain (catalytic domain). The lysine-binding sites mediate binding to fibrin, α2-antiplasmin, and cell-surface receptors.

> [!important] Fibrin-Specific Activation
> tPA-mediated plasminogen activation is dramatically enhanced (~500-fold) when plasminogen is bound to fibrin, ensuring that fibrinolysis is localised to the clot surface rather than occurring systemically.

## Substrates & Catalytic Activity

Plasmin has broad substrate specificity:

- **Fibrin:** Primary substrate. Plasmin cleaves cross-linked fibrin at multiple sites, generating fibrin degradation products (FDPs) including D-dimer, a clinical biomarker of fibrinolysis and thrombosis.
- **Fibrinogen:** Degrades fibrinogen, preventing its polymerisation.
- **ECM components:** Degrades fibronectin, laminin, proteoglycans, and collagens (types IV and V), facilitating cell migration and tissue remodelling.
- **Pro-MMPs:** Plasmin is the principal physiological activator of several pro-MMPs, including [[MMP-3]] (stromelysin-1), [[MMP-9]] (gelatinase B), and [[MMP-12]] (macrophage metalloelastase). This connects the fibrinolytic system to collagenolysis and basement-membrane breach.
- **Complement components:** Cleaves C3 and C5, generating active complement fragments and linking fibrinolysis to innate immunity.
- **Growth factors:** Releases matrix-bound latent forms of TGF-β, VEGF, and FGF-2, promoting angiogenesis and tissue repair.
- **Other:** Cleaves fibrinogen-related peptide (FREP) and activates prourokinase, creating a positive feedback loop of plasmin generation.

## Regulation

Plasmin activity is tightly regulated at multiple levels:

- **α2-Antiplasmin (α2-AP):** The primary rapid-acting inhibitor of plasmin. Forms a 1:1 stoichiometric complex with free plasmin in plasma (half-life ~0.1 seconds), ensuring that plasmin generated systemically is immediately neutralised. This prevents widespread proteolysis outside the clot microenvironment.
- **[[PAI-1]]:** Inhibits [[uPA]] and tPA, indirectly controlling plasmin generation. PAI-1 is itself a key [[Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype|SASP]] factor, linking plasmin regulation to senescence and ageing.
- **PAI-2:** Inhibits uPA less efficiently than PAI-1; expressed in placenta and monocytes.
- **Thrombin-Activatable Fibrinolysis Inhibitor (TAFI):** Removes C-terminal lysine residues from partially degraded fibrin, reducing plasminogen and tPA binding and thus attenuating fibrinolysis.
- **Fibrin itself:** Paradoxically, fibrin serves as both the substrate and a cofactor that accelerates plasminogen activation by tPA, creating a localised positive feedback at the clot surface.

## Physiological Functions

### Fibrinolysis
Plasmin's primary physiological role is the dissolution of fibrin clots (fibrinolysis) once tissue repair is complete. This is essential for maintaining vascular patency and preventing pathological thrombus persistence. The fibrin-specific activation of plasminogen by tPA ensures that clot lysis is spatially controlled.

### ECM Remodelling & Cell Migration
Plasmin degrades multiple ECM components (fibronectin, laminin, collagen IV) and activates pro-MMPs, facilitating cell migration through tissue barriers. This is critical for wound healing, immune cell trafficking, and embryonic development.

### Inflammation & Immunity
Plasmin activates complement components (C3, C5) and generates chemotactic fragments that recruit [[Neutrophils]] and [[Macrophages]] to sites of tissue injury. It also processes pro-inflammatory cytokines, modulating the inflammatory response.

### Growth Factor Release
By cleaving matrix sequestering domains, plasmin liberates latent [[VEGF]], TGF-β, and FGF-2 from the ECM, making them available for receptor binding. This links fibrinolysis to angiogenesis and tissue repair signalling.

### Pregnancy & Development
Plasminogen is essential for embryonic development (PLG-knockout mice show growth retardation and death by 3 weeks). In pregnancy, the fibrinolytic system is modulated to prevent excessive bleeding at implantation sites while maintaining uterine vascular patency.

## Role in Senescence / SASP

Plasmin generation is tightly linked to the [[Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype|SASP]]:

- **uPA/PAI-1 axis:** [[uPA]] is secreted by [[Senescent Cells]] and converts plasminogen to plasmin at the cell surface. Its inhibitor [[PAI-1]] is one of the most robust SASP biomarkers, creating a regulatory loop that controls plasmin generation in the senescent microenvironment.
- **Pro-MMP activation:** Plasmin activates latent MMPs (MMP-3, MMP-9, MMP-12), amplifying the ECM-degrading capacity of the SASP beyond what any single protease achieves alone.
- **Paracrine senescence:** Plasmin-mediated ECM degradation disrupts fibroblast-ECM mechanosignalling, promoting propagation of the senescent phenotype to neighbouring cells.
- **Growth factor release:** Liberation of matrix-bound TGF-β and VEGF by plasmin contributes to the pro-angiogenic and pro-fibrotic output of the SASP.

> [!tip] Therapeutic Relevance
> Modulating the uPA/plasmin axis (e.g., with uPA inhibitors like upamostat) offers a route to limit senescence-driven ECM degradation and invasion without broad MMP inhibition, potentially reducing side effects seen with pan-MMP inhibitors.

## Pathological Roles

### Thrombosis & Haemorrhage
- **Deficient fibrinolysis:** Reduced plasmin generation or excessive PAI-1 activity contributes to thrombotic disease, including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction.
- **Excessive fibrinolysis:** Uncontrolled plasmin activity (e.g., in disseminated intravascular coagulation, DIC) causes systemic bleeding through fibrinogen and fibrin degradation.

### Cancer
Plasmin promotes tumour invasion and metastasis by:
- Degrading ECM barriers (basement membrane and interstitial matrix)
- Activating pro-MMPs that further degrade structural proteins
- Releasing matrix-bound growth factors (VEGF, TGF-β) that promote angiogenesis and tumour growth
- Facilitating tumour cell detachment and migration

Elevated uPA and low PAI-1 levels are established poor-prognosis markers in breast and other cancers.

### Neurological Diseases
Plasmin participates in blood-brain barrier (BBB) remodelling following [[Ischemic Stroke|ischaemic stroke]]. tPA (alteplase) is the primary thrombolytic agent used clinically, but excessive plasmin generation can exacerbate haemorrhagic transformation and neuronal damage.

### Wound Healing & Fibrosis
Plasmin is essential for normal wound healing (ECM remodelling, re-epithelialisation), but dysregulated plasmin activity contributes to pathological fibrosis by activating latent TGF-β and sustaining fibroblast proliferation.

### Kidney Disease
Plasmin-mediated ECM degradation contributes to glomerular basement membrane damage in inflammatory renal diseases, including anti-GBM nephritis.

## Clinical Relevance

- **Thrombolytic therapy:** tPA (alteplase, tenecteplase) is used clinically to generate plasmin at clot surfaces for treatment of acute ischaemic stroke, myocardial infarction, and pulmonary embolism.
- **D-dimer:** Fibrin degradation products (particularly D-dimer) generated by plasmin are used clinically as biomarkers for deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and DIC.
- **Cancer prognosis:** High tumour uPA and low PAI-1 levels predict poor outcome in breast cancer and guide adjuvant therapy decisions.
- **PAI-1 as SASP biomarker:** Circulating PAI-1 levels reflect the senescent burden and predict all-cause mortality in ageing cohorts.
- **Drug targets:** uPA inhibitors (upamostat) and PAI-1 inhibitors (TM5441) are under investigation for cancer and age-related pathologies, respectively.
