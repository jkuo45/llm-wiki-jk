# Catalase
Catalase is a key antioxidant enzyme that catalyzes the decomposition of [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] into water and oxygen. It is a critical component of the cell's defense against [[Oxidative Stress]].
## Inhibition by Glycation
The activity of catalase can be reduced by [[Glycation]] modifications. Intracellular [[Advanced Glycation End Products]] (AGEs) accumulation impairs the enzymatic function of catalase, thereby increasing cellular oxidative stress and contributing to the [[Aging]] process.
## Role in Mitohormesis
In the context of [[Mitohormesis]], mild increases in mitochondrial [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] (mROS) can lead to the adaptive upregulation of catalase. This increased antioxidant capacity provides protection against subsequent, more severe oxidative insults.
## Catalytic Mechanism
Catalase operates through a two-step peroxidatic cycle. In the first step, the heme iron (Fe³⁺) in resting catalase reacts with H₂O₂ to form compound I (an oxyferryl species with a porphyrin π-cation radical, [Fe⁴⁺=O]•⁺) and water. In the second step, compound I reacts with a second H₂O₂ molecule, returning catalase to its resting state while producing water and molecular oxygen. The overall reaction (2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂) proceeds with a near-diffusion-limited turnover number (~10⁷ M⁻¹·s⁻¹), making catalase one of the most efficient enzymes known.
## Peroxidatic Activity
Beyond its canonical catalatic function, catalase also exhibits peroxidatic activity, oxidizing small-molecule substrates (e.g., ethanol, methanol, formaldehyde, and nitrite) using H₂O₂ as an electron donor. This broad substrate scope allows catalase to participate in the detoxification of diverse endogenous and xenobiotic compounds.
## Subcellular Localization and Regulation
Catalase is predominantly localized in [[Peroxisomes]], where it neutralizes H₂O₂ generated by peroxisomal oxidases (e.g., acyl-CoA oxidase, D-amino acid oxidase). Catalase activity is regulated by post-translational modifications including [[Glycation]]-induced inactivation, oxidation of critical cysteine residues, and proteolytic cleavage during aging. The enzyme's half-life is modulated by binding to chaperones and its susceptibility to [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]]-mediated damage creates a feed-forward loop wherein oxidative stress further impairs antioxidant capacity.
## Role in Catecholamine Oxidation Protection
In the context of the [[Adrenochrome Pathway]], catalase serves a critical protective role by scavenging H₂O₂ produced during [[Catecholamines|catecholamine]] autoxidation and MAO-dependent deamination. Elevated H₂O₂ can drive the oxidative cyclization of [[Epinephrine]] to [[Adrenochrome]] via metal-catalyzed Fenton chemistry. By maintaining low steady-state H₂O₂ levels, catalase limits non-enzymatic catecholamine oxidation and the accumulation of neurotoxic aminochromes.
## Connection to Mitohormesis
Within the [[Mitohormesis]] framework, catalase expression is upregulated as an adaptive response to mild mitochondrial ROS signals. This hormetic induction reinforces cellular resilience by preventing the transition from physiological ROS signaling to pathological oxidative damage. The interplay between catalase and mitochondrial [[Manganese superoxide dismutase|SOD2]] establishes a coordinated antioxidant network that is central to the mitohormetic stress response.

## Definition
Catalase is a common enzyme found in nearly all living organisms exposed to oxygen. It catalyzes the decomposition of [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] to water and oxygen.
## Function
It is a very important enzyme in protecting the cell from oxidative damage by [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] (ROS).
## Structure and Catalytic Mechanism
Catalase is a tetrameric heme-containing enzyme (MW ~240 kDa) with each subunit harboring a ferriprotoporphyrin IX (heme b) active site. The enzyme exhibits one of the highest turnover numbers known in biochemistry ($k_{cat} \approx 10^7$ s⁻¹), enabling it to decompose millions of $H_2O_2$ molecules per second. The catalytic cycle proceeds in two steps. In the first step, $H_2O_2$ oxidizes the ferric heme iron (Fe³⁺) to an oxyferryl intermediate (Compound I, Fe⁴⁺=O porphyrin π-cation radical), releasing water. In the second step, a second $H_2O_2$ molecule acts as a reducing substrate, donating two electrons to regenerate the resting ferric enzyme while producing O₂ and H₂O: $2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2$. This "catalatic" reaction distinguishes catalase from other peroxidases in that it uses $H_2O_2$ both as oxidant and reductant, avoiding the need for exogenous reducing cofactors such as [[Glutathione]] or NADPH.
The enzyme also exhibits peroxidase activity (using organic substrates such as ethanol, methanol, or phenols as electron donors) in the presence of low steady-state $H_2O_2$ concentrations. NADPH binding to catalase (four molecules per tetramer) protects the enzyme from inactivation by its own Compound I intermediate, particularly under conditions of oxidative stress. The mammalian catalase gene (CAT) is located on chromosome 11p13 and encodes a 527-amino-acid protein that is targeted primarily to [[Peroxisomes]] via a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting signal 1 (PTS1; Ser-Lys-Leu).
## Cellular Localization and Compartmentalization
In mammalian cells, catalase is predominantly localized within [[Peroxisomes]], organelles that also house $H_2O_2$-generating oxidases (e.g., acyl-CoA oxidase in [[Fatty acid oxidation]], D-amino acid oxidase, and urate oxidase). This spatial arrangement allows catalase to capture $H_2O_2$ at its source, preventing its diffusion to the [[Cytosol]] and [[Mitochondria]] where it could participate in [[Fenton Reaction]] chemistry. Lower catalase activity is present in the cytosol and [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] matrix, where [[Glutathione Peroxidase]] (GPx1) serves as the primary $H_2O_2$ scavenger. Erythrocytes contain high catalase activity, reflecting their need to detoxify the large flux of $H_2O_2$$ generated by the spontaneous dismutation of [[Superoxide Radicals]] produced during hemoglobin autoxidation.
## Clinical Significance
**Acatalasemia** ([[Takahara's Disease]]) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the CAT gene, resulting in absent or severely deficient catalase activity. Most patients are asymptomatic, but up to 50% develop progressive oral gangrene (Takahara's disease) due to the inability of oral [[Streptococci]] and accumulated $H_2O_2$ to be detoxified, leading to tissue necrosis. This condition is most prevalent in Japanese and Korean populations (carrier frequency ~1.5%). In contrast to the mild phenotype of acatalasemia, catalase overexpression in transgenic models confers protection against [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury]], [[Diabetes Mellitus]]-induced nephropathy, and [[Atherosclerosis]], suggesting that $H_2O_2$ detoxification capacity is rate-limiting for oxidative stress resistance in many tissues.
In [[Cancer]], catalase expression is frequently downregulated in tumor cells compared to their normal counterparts, particularly in [[Breast Cancer]], [[Prostate Cancer]], and [[Hepatocellular Carcinoma]]. This reduction in catalase activity elevates steady-state $H_2O_2$ levels, which paradoxically may promote proliferation by activating redox-sensitive transcription factors ([[NFKB]], [[AP-1]]) while simultaneously increasing genomic instability. The "catalase paradox" in aging is notable: [[Caloric Restriction]] upregulates catalase expression in several tissues, and mice overexpressing human catalase targeted to [[Mitochondria]] (MCAT mice) show a ~20% increase in median lifespan and delayed cardiac and cataract pathology, directly implicating mitochondrial $H_2O_2$ in the aging process.
## Interactions with Other Antioxidant Systems
Catalase operates in parallel with the [[Glutathione Peroxidase]] (GPx) and [[Thioredoxin Peroxidase]] ([[Peroxiredoxin]]) systems. While GPx has a higher affinity for $H_2O_2$ ($K_m$ ~1–10 μM) than catalase ($K_m$ ~25–100 mM), catalase has a vastly higher $V_{max}$ and does not consume NADPH or glutathione. Thus, at low $H_2O_2$ fluxes, GPx predominates; at high fluxes (e.g., during the [[Respiratory Burst]] or in [[Peroxisomes]]), catalase becomes the dominant scavenger. The interplay between these systems is essential for maintaining the low steady-state $H_2O_2$ (~1–10 nM) typical of most cell types.
