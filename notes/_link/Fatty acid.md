# Fatty acid

Fatty acids are carboxylic acids with aliphatic chains, usually 4–28 carbons in length. They are classified by chain length into short-chain (SCFA, 2–6C), medium-chain (MCFA, 6–12C), long-chain (LCFA, 12–22C), and very-long-chain (VLCFA, >22C) fatty acids. Saturated fatty acids contain no double bonds, while unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more double bonds in cis or trans configuration. The essential fatty acids — linoleic acid (LA, ω-6) and α-linolenic acid (ALA, ω-3) — must be obtained from the diet. Fatty acids provide 9 kcal/g and serve as the primary substrate for β-oxidation.

## Structure & Nomenclature

Systematic nomenclature specifies chain length and number of double bonds (e.g., 18:2n-6 for linoleic acid). Common names include palmitic acid (16:0), stearic acid (18:0), oleic acid (18:1n-9), linoleic acid (18:2n-6), α-linolenic acid (18:3n-3), arachidonic acid (20:4n-6), eicosapentaenoic acid / EPA (20:5n-3), and docosahexaenoic acid / DHA (22:6n-3). Cis double bonds introduce kinks in the acyl chain that increase membrane fluidity, whereas trans double bonds — produced during industrial hydrogenation — confer rigidity and are associated with adverse health outcomes.

## Metabolism

Fatty acid synthesis (lipogenesis) proceeds from acetyl-CoA through carboxylation to malonyl-CoA, followed by elongation to palmitate via fatty acid synthase (FASN). Further elongation and desaturation occur in the endoplasmic reticulum. Fatty acid oxidation occurs primarily through mitochondrial β-oxidation; very-long-chain fatty acids are initially chain-shortened in [[Peroxisomes]]. Omega-3 and omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) serve as precursors for eicosanoids — including [[Prostaglandins]], [[Leukotrienes]], and thromboxanes — via cyclooxygenase ([[Cyclooxygenases|COX]]) and [[Lipoxygenases|lipoxygenase (LOX) pathways]]. [[DHA]] is enriched in the brain and retina and is critical for neuronal function and visual signal transduction.

## Physiological Function

Fatty acids are fundamental components of membrane [[Phospholipids]], where their degree of unsaturation governs bilayer fluidity and lipid raft formation. As triglycerides, they constitute the primary energy reserve in [[Adipose Tissue]]. Oxidized derivatives — eicosanoids, resolvins, protectins, and maresins — act as potent signaling molecules in [[Inflammation]] and its resolution. Post-translational [[Protein modification]] by fatty acids (palmitoylation, myristoylation) regulates membrane tethering and protein trafficking. Fatty acids also serve as endogenous ligands for nuclear receptors including [[PPARα]], [[PPARγ]], LXR, and FXR. Short-chain fatty acids ([[Acetate]], [[Propionate]], [[Butyrate]]) produced by the [[Gut Microbiome]] act as [[Histone deacetylase]] (HDAC) inhibitors and activate GPCRs such as GPR41 and GPR43, linking diet to epigenetic regulation and immune function.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Dietary saturated fat elevates [[LDL]] cholesterol and is associated with increased [[Cardiovascular Disease]] risk, while industrially produced trans fats both raise LDL and lower HDL. Polyunsaturated fats, particularly ω-3 PUFAs, reduce cardiovascular risk. Elevated circulating free fatty acids drive [[Lipotoxicity]], [[Insulin Resistance]], and [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]] through ectopic lipid accumulation in [[Liver]], [[Skeletal Muscle]], and pancreas. Hepatic steatosis ([[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|NAFLD]] / [[NASH]]) is characterized by excessive triglyceride deposition. Essential fatty acid deficiency presents as scaly dermatitis, poor wound healing, and growth retardation. Peroxisomal biogenesis disorders (Zellweger spectrum) lead to toxic VLCFA accumulation. Clinically, ω-3 supplements (fish oil, Lovaza) lower triglycerides; [[Statins]] inhibit cholesterol synthesis; fibrates activate [[PPARα]] to promote fatty acid oxidation; and PCSK9 inhibitors enhance LDL receptor recycling.
