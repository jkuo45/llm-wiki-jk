# Acid Sphingomyelinase

Acid sphingomyelinase (ASM; EC 3.1.4.12) is a zinc-dependent hydrolase that catalyzes the catabolism of sphingomyelin into ceramide and phosphocholine at acidic pH. Encoded by the *SMPD1* gene, ASM is primarily localized to the [[Lysosome]], where it plays a central role in [[Sphingolipid]] metabolism and membrane turnover. Beyond its catabolic function, ASM is a key regulator of ceramide-mediated cell signaling, influencing [[Apoptosis]], stress responses, and the biophysical properties of cellular membranes. Deficiency in ASM causes Niemann-Pick disease, a severe lysosomal storage disorder, and the enzyme has been implicated in the pathogenesis of [[Alzheimer's Disease]], [[Atherosclerosis]], and [[Depression]].

## Structure & Domains

The human *SMPD1* gene encodes a 631-amino acid precursor protein with a predicted molecular mass of approximately 75 kDa. The nascent polypeptide contains an N-terminal signal peptide for endoplasmic reticulum targeting, a propeptide region, a catalytic domain, and a C-terminal saposin B (SapB) domain. The proform undergoes proteolytic processing in the Golgi and endosomal compartments, yielding mature lysosomal forms of 70 kDa and 75 kDa, with the size difference attributable to differential glycosylation.

ASM belongs to the acid sphingomyelinase/diacylglycerol lipase family of zinc metalloenzymes. The catalytic domain coordinates a divalent zinc ion (Zn²⁺) essential for enzymatic activity. The saposin B domain at the C-terminus functions as a lipid-binding module that targets the enzyme to membrane surfaces and facilitates access to the lipid substrate within the bilayer. This domain is also critical for protein folding and stability in the acidic lysosomal environment.

## Mechanism of Action

ASM hydrolyzes the phosphodiester bond of sphingomyelin, yielding ceramide and phosphocholine as products. The reaction requires an acidic pH optimum (pH 4.5–5.5), consistent with the low pH of the lysosomal lumen. Catalysis is dependent on the presence of lipid cofactors that perturb the lipid bilayer and present the substrate in an accessible orientation. Key cofactors include bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (BMP), also known as lysobisphosphatidic acid, and cardiolipin, both of which enhance enzymatic activity by altering membrane curvature and fluidity.

Two principal catalytic forms of ASM have been described. The secretory form (S-SMase) is released from cells into the extracellular space and is dependent on Zn²⁺ for activity; it is activated by Zn²⁺ binding to an extracellular regulatory site. The lysosomal form (L-SMase) resides within the lysosomal compartment and functions independently of exogenous Zn²⁺, relying instead on the zinc ion incorporated during biosynthesis. Both forms share the same catalytic mechanism but differ in their regulation, trafficking, and physiological roles.

## Physiological Function

ASM is essential for sphingolipid homeostasis, catalyzing the first committed step in the degradation of sphingomyelin within the lysosome. Sphingomyelin is a major structural phospholipid of cell membranes, particularly abundant in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, myelin sheaths, and pulmonary surfactant. By hydrolyzing sphingomyelin, ASM regulates membrane composition and turnover, contributing to lipid raft dynamics and the biophysical properties of cellular membranes.

Beyond catabolism, the ceramide generated by ASM serves as a bioactive lipid second messenger. Ceramide mediates membrane reorganization into ceramide-enriched microdomains that serve as platforms for signal transduction. ASM-derived ceramide activates downstream pathways involved in [[Apoptosis]], cell cycle arrest, and the cellular stress response, particularly in response to ionizing radiation, TNF-α, oxidative stress, and Fas ligand stimulation. ASM also contributes to pulmonary surfactant catabolism in alveolar type II cells, a process critical for maintaining surfactant homeostasis and lung function.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Loss-of-function mutations in *SMPD1* cause Niemann-Pick disease (NPD), a lysosomal storage disorder inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. The clinical spectrum is divided into two principal forms:

- **Niemann-Pick type A** presents in infancy with severe neurodegeneration, hepatosplenomegaly, cherry-red macular spots, and progressive psychomotor deterioration. Most affected individuals do not survive beyond early childhood (death by age 3). Type A patients have near-complete loss of ASM activity, resulting in massive sphingomyelin accumulation in neurons, visceral organs, and the reticuloendothelial system.
- **Niemann-Pick type B** is a non-neuronopathic form characterized primarily by hepatosplenomegaly, pulmonary insufficiency, and lipid accumulation in macrophages. Neurological function is preserved, and patients may survive into adulthood. Pulmonary involvement, including interstitial infiltrates and progressive respiratory failure, is the major cause of morbidity and mortality. Type B patients retain partial ASM activity (typically 10–20% of normal).

The broader designation of acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) encompasses both type A and type B forms, as well as intermediate phenotypes. Beyond NPD, reduced ASM activity or dysregulated ceramide signaling has been implicated in several disease contexts. In [[Alzheimer's Disease]], ceramide accumulation has been observed in brain tissue, and ASM activity is elevated in cerebrospinal fluid, suggesting a role in neurodegeneration and amyloidogenic processing. In [[Atherosclerosis]], macrophage-derived ASM contributes to foam cell formation and plaque instability through ceramide-mediated signaling. ASM is also a mediator of radiation-induced cell death, where ionizing radiation activates ASM to generate ceramide and trigger [[Apoptosis]]. In the central nervous system, ceramide signaling downstream of ASM has been linked to stress-induced neuronal loss and has been associated with the pathophysiology of major depressive disorder.

## Therapeutic Approaches

Olipudase alfa is a recombinant human acid sphingomyelinase approved for the treatment of non-central nervous system manifestations of Niemann-Pick type B. It functions as enzyme replacement therapy, reducing sphingomyelin accumulation in visceral organs and improving pulmonary function. Miglustat, a substrate reduction therapy that inhibits glucosylceramide synthase, has been explored as an adjunct treatment in Niemann-Pick disease to attenuate sphingolipid biosynthesis. Gene therapy approaches targeting *SMPD1* are under investigation for both type A and type B forms.
