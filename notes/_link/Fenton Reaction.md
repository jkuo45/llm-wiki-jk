# Fenton Reaction
The [[Fenton Reaction]] is a chemical process in which [[Iron]] (Fe²⁺) reduces hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) to generate the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (•OH), a potent [[Reactive Oxygen Species]]. In the brain, iron sequestered by [[Neuromelanin]] can participate in Fenton chemistry, contributing to [[Oxidative Stress]] and neurodegeneration in [[Parkinson's Disease]].
The Fenton reaction is a catalytic process that involves the reaction of [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] with ferrous iron (Fe2+) to produce highly reactive [[Hydroxyl Radicals]].
## Chemical Mechanism
The classical Fenton reaction proceeds as: Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + •OH + OH⁻. The hydroxyl radical (•OH) produced is among the most potent oxidizing agents in biology, with a reduction potential of ~2.8 V and a diffusion-limited reaction rate with virtually all organic molecules. The reaction is catalytic in iron: Fe³⁺ can be reduced back to Fe²⁺ by [[Superoxide Radicals]] (the iron-catalyzed Haber-Weiss cycle), [[Ascorbic Acid]], or other cellular reductants, perpetuating hydroxyl radical production.
The overall Haber-Weiss reaction (superoxide-driven Fenton) is: O₂⁻• + H₂O₂ → •OH + OH⁻ + O₂, but this proceeds negligibly in the absence of iron. The true biological pathway couples spontaneous superoxide-mediated reduction of Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ with Fenton chemistry, yielding a catalytic iron cycle that amplifies ROS production.
## Biological Significance
The Fenton reaction is the primary mechanism by which [[Hydrogen Peroxide]], a relatively stable and diffusible signaling molecule, is converted into the indiscriminate and destructive [[Hydroxyl Radicals]]. This conversion is site-specific because iron is largely bound to proteins and nucleic acids — when H₂O₂ encounters iron bound to DNA, the resulting •OH inflicts damage at that precise locus, producing single-strand breaks and base modifications such as [[8-oxo-2'-deoxyguanosine]]. Similarly, iron associated with [[Cell Membranes]] or proteins catalyzes local [[Lipid Peroxidation]] and protein carbonylation.
## Iron Homeostasis and Its Regulation
Because of the destructive capacity of the Fenton reaction, cellular iron is stringently regulated. Free (labile) iron pools are maintained at extremely low concentrations (~1 μM in the cytosol) through sequestration by [[Ferritin]], the iron-storage protein that can accommodate up to 4,500 Fe³⁺ atoms per molecule. The transcription factor [[Iron Regulatory Protein]] (IRP) system post-transcriptionally controls ferritin and [[Transferrin receptor]] expression in response to iron availability. Under [[Oxidative Stress]], [[NRF2]] induces ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) expression, which possesses ferroxidase activity that converts Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ for safe storage, thereby limiting Fenton chemistry. [[Heme oxygenase-1]] ([[HO-1]]), also NRF2-regulated, degrades pro-oxidant heme to release Fe²⁺ (which is then sequestered by ferritin), biliverdin/bilirubin (antioxidants), and carbon monoxide (anti-inflammatory).
## Clinical Relevance
**Neurodegeneration:** The [[Substantia Nigra]] in [[Parkinson's Disease]] accumulates iron with age, and this iron burden correlates with oxidative damage markers. Neuromelanin, which normally chelates iron, may become saturated and release free iron, promoting localized Fenton chemistry in dopaminergic neurons.
**Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury:** Upon reperfusion of ischemic tissue, the sudden reintroduction of oxygen provides substrate for [[Xanthine Oxidase]], which generates superoxide and H₂O₂. Simultaneously, acidic pH from ischemia mobilizes iron from ferritin and transferrin, creating the ideal conditions for Fenton-driven oxidative damage.
**Cancer:** Tumors accumulate iron through upregulated [[Transferrin receptor 1]] (TfR1) expression and downregulated ferritin. This elevated labile iron pool sensitizes cancer cells to ferroptosis, a non-apoptotic cell death modality driven by iron-dependent [[Lipid Peroxidation]]. Therapeutic strategies targeting iron metabolism (e.g., iron chelators, ferroptosis inducers) exploit the Fenton reaction to selectively kill cancer cells.
**Aging:** Iron accumulates in multiple tissues with age, including the brain, liver, and cardiovascular system. This progressive iron loading is thought to contribute to age-associated oxidative damage, and interventions that reduce iron burden (phlebotomy, dietary restriction, chelation) extend lifespan in several model organisms.
