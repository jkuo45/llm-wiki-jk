# Glucose Intolerance

[[Glucose Intolerance]] is a metabolic phenotype in which the body fails to maintain normal post-prandial or fasting glucose concentrations, falling short of the diagnostic thresholds for [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]] but representing a key pre-diabetic state. It reflects reduced insulin sensitivity and/or impaired insulin secretion.

## Rapamycin-Associated Glucose Intolerance

Chronic administration of [[Rapamycin]] and [[Rapalog|rapalogs]] can produce glucose intolerance and [[Insulin Resistance]], an effect mechanistically separable from the longevity benefits of [[mTORC1]] inhibition. In some rodent models, chronic rapamycin upregulates hepatic gluconeogenesis and impairs adipose lipid deposition, yielding both glucose intolerance and [[Hyperlipidemia]] ([Houde et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20299475/)). In healthy elderly humans on low-dose or intermittent schedules, however, hyperglycemia is typically absent or lower than placebo ([Mannick et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25540326/); [Mannick et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29997249/)).

> [!info] mTORC1 vs mTORC2
> The metabolic side effects of rapamycin are partly attributable to unintended inhibition of [[mTORC2]], which disrupts Akt-mediated insulin signaling and induces insulin resistance, whereas mTORC1 inhibition is the geroprotective arm ([Lamming et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22461615/)).

The opinion literature frames diet- and drug-induced glucose intolerance as a benign, reversible "starvation pseudo-diabetes," arguing it should not preclude anti-aging use when dosing is personalized ([Blagosklonny, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6814615/)).
