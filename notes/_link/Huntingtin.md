# Huntingtin

Huntingtin (HTT) is a large scaffold protein (~350 kDa) encoded by the *HTT* gene located on chromosome 4p16.3. It is essential for embryonic development — constitutive *HTT* knockout in mice is embryonic lethal, with death occurring around embryonic day 7.5 due to defective gastrulation and impaired [[Transcription|transcriptional]] regulation. In the adult nervous system, HTT functions as a multifunctional scaffold that orchestrates vesicular transport, [[Autophagy|autophagy]], endocytosis, [[BDNF|brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)]] transport, and [[Transcription|transcriptional regulation]]. The protein contains a polymorphic CAG trinucleotide repeat in exon 1 that encodes a polyglutamine (polyQ) tract. In healthy individuals, this repeat ranges from 6 to 35 CAGs; repeats of 36 or more cause [[Huntington's Disease]] with full penetrance at ≥40 CAGs. Wild-type HTT is subject to extensive post-translational modification — phosphorylation at Ser13, Ser16, and Ser421, among other sites — which modulates its subcellular localization, protein–protein interactions, and propensity to aggregate.

## Structure & Domains

Huntingtin is a massive, predominantly α-helical protein with several functionally distinct domains arranged from N-terminus to C-terminus:

- **N-terminal polyQ tract**: Encoded by the CAG repeat in exon 1. In the wild-type protein, this tract is unstructured and soluble. When expanded beyond ~36 glutamine residues, the polyQ stretch adopts a β-sheet-rich conformation that drives intermolecular hydrogen bonding, leading to protein misfolding and aggregation into amyloid-like fibrils. The N-terminal fragment containing the expanded polyQ (particularly the exon 1 fragment) is the most cytotoxic species in [[Huntington's Disease]].

- **Heptapeptide repeat (HR) region**: Immediately C-terminal to the polyQ tract, the HR region contains arrays of a 7-amino-acid repeating unit that mediates huntingtin self-association. This region is critical for oligomerization and may influence the kinetics of polyQ aggregation.

- **HEAT repeat domains**: HTT contains three large clusters of α-helical HEAT (Huntingtin, Elongation factor 3, PR65/A, TOR) repeats — HEAT-A (residues ~54–98), HEAT-B (~220–480), and HEAT-C (~1100–2200). HEAT repeats form solenoid-like structures that serve as platforms for protein–protein interactions. These domains enable HTT to bind a remarkably diverse interactome of over 100 proteins, including [[Dopamine|dopaminergic]] signaling components, motor proteins, and scaffolding adaptors.

- **C-terminal region**: Contains additional interaction motifs, including a clathrin-binding site, WW domain-binding motifs, and several proline-rich segments. The C-terminus also harbors nuclear localization and export signals that regulate HTT shuttling between the cytoplasm and nucleus.

Post-translational modifications are abundant on HTT. Phosphorylation at Ser13 and Ser16 (near the N-terminus) is particularly important: these modifications inhibit polyQ aggregation and promote clearance of mutant HTT by the [[Proteasome|proteasome]] and [[Autophagy|autophagy]] pathways. Phosphorylation at Ser421, regulated by [[mTOR|AKT/PKB]], promotes HTT association with HAP1 and enhances anterograde axonal transport.

## Mechanism of Action & Pathways

HTT functions primarily as a molecular scaffold and cargo adaptor, integrating multiple cellular pathways:

- **Protein–protein interactions**: HTT interacts with over 100 identified binding partners. Key interactors include HAP1 (huntingtin-associated protein 1), HIP1 (huntingtin-interacting protein 1), clathrin heavy chain, the dynein/dynactin complex, kinesin family motor proteins, and the [[Proteasome|proteasome]] subunit Rpt6. The breadth of this interactome positions HTT as a central hub coordinating cytoskeletal dynamics, membrane trafficking, and [[Transcription|transcriptional]] control.

- **Vesicular and axonal transport**: The HTT–HAP1 complex physically links intracellular cargo to microtubule-based motor proteins. In neurons, this complex is essential for the anterograde transport of [[BDNF]]-containing vesicles along axons. HTT acts as a processivity factor for kinesin-1, preventing premature cargo release and enabling long-range transport from the cell body to synaptic terminals. Disruption of this transport — as occurs with polyQ expansion — impairs [[BDNF]] delivery to [[Striatum|striatal]] neurons, contributing to the selective vulnerability of medium spiny neurons in [[Huntington's Disease]].

- **Transcriptional regulation**: HTT shuttles between the cytoplasm and nucleus and directly interacts with transcriptional co-activators including CBP (CREB-binding protein)/p300, Sp1, and TAFII130. Wild-type HTT facilitates the [[Transcription|transcription]] of *BDNF* and other neurotrophic genes by bridging transcription factors to the basal transcriptional machinery. Mutant HTT with expanded polyQ sequesters CBP into intranuclear inclusions, depleting the pool of functional CBP and broadly disrupting [[Transcription|transcriptional]] programs required for neuronal survival.

- **Autophagy regulation**: Phosphorylation of HTT at Ser421 recruits the [[Autophagy|autophagy]] initiation complex components Beclin-1 and ULK1 to the cargo, linking nutrient sensing to cargo-specific autophagic degradation. Wild-type HTT therefore functions as a selective autophagy receptor, facilitating clearance of aggregation-prone proteins. Loss of this function with polyQ expansion contributes to the accumulation of misfolded protein aggregates.

- **Endocytosis and membrane trafficking**: HTT associates with clathrin-coated pits and regulates clathrin-mediated endocytosis at synapses. It also interacts with HIP1, which modulates actin dynamics at endocytic sites, influencing receptor internalization and synaptic vesicle recycling.

- **Mitotic spindle orientation**: In neural progenitor cells during embryonic development, HTT interacts with the dynein/dynactin complex at the cell cortex to orient the mitotic spindle, thereby influencing the balance between symmetric and asymmetric cell division. This function is critical for proper cortical layering during brain development.

## Physiological Function

In the developing embryo, HTT is indispensable — its loss is incompatible with life. During nervous system development, HTT promotes the survival and differentiation of neural progenitors by regulating spindle orientation and [[Transcription|transcriptional]] programs. In the mature nervous system, HTT supports:

- **Neuronal survival**: Through facilitation of [[BDNF]] expression and transport, HTT provides trophic support to [[Striatum|striatal]] and cortical neurons. HTT knockout in adult mice leads to progressive [[Neurodegeneration|neurodegeneration]] resembling [[Huntington's Disease]].

- **Axonal transport**: HTT is a critical adaptor for kinesin- and dynein-mediated transport of mitochondria, synaptic vesicle precursors, and [[BDNF]]-containing endosomes along microtubules.

- **Synaptic function**: HTT regulates [[Dopamine|dopaminergic]] and glutamatergic synaptic transmission by modulating vesicle trafficking, receptor recycling, and postsynaptic scaffolding at glutamate receptors.

- **Adult neurogenesis**: HTT supports the proliferation and differentiation of neural stem cells in the subventricular zone and hippocampal dentate gyrus, contributing to ongoing neurogenesis in the adult brain.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

### Huntington's Disease

[[Huntington's Disease]] (HD) is an autosomal dominant [[Neurodegeneration|neurodegenerative]] disorder caused by CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the *HTT* gene. The disease displays a strict genetic threshold:

| CAG Repeat Length | Phenotype |
|---|---|
| 6–26 | Normal; no disease risk |
| 27–35 | Intermediate; no disease but unstable in paternal transmission |
| 36–39 | Reduced penetrance; may or may not develop HD |
| ≥40 | Full penetrance; will develop HD if lifespan is sufficient |
| ≥60 | Juvenile-onset HD (Westphal variant) |

The expanded polyQ tract causes the mutant HTT protein to misfold and aggregate, forming insoluble intranuclear and cytoplasmic inclusion bodies. The most toxic species are N-terminal fragments of mutant HTT (particularly the exon 1 product) generated by aberrant proteolytic cleavage. These fragments readily enter the nucleus, where they disrupt [[Transcription|transcriptional]] regulation by sequestering CBP/p300 and other co-activators, and in the cytoplasm they impair [[Proteasome|proteasomal]] and [[Autophagy|autophagic]] clearance pathways.

The hallmark neuropathology of HD is selective degeneration of GABAergic medium spiny neurons in the [[Striatum]] (caudate nucleus and putamen), with later cortical atrophy. This vulnerability is attributed to several converging factors: high expression of mutant HTT in striatal neurons, dependence on cortically derived [[BDNF]] for survival (impaired by axonal transport deficits), excitotoxic glutamate signaling, and disrupted [[Dopamine|dopaminergic]] input from the substantia nigra.

Clinical manifestations include:

- **Motor**: Progressive chorea (involuntary, irregular movements), followed by rigidity and bradykinesia in later stages.
- **Psychiatric**: Depression, irritability, apathy, psychosis, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms often precede motor onset by years.
- **Cognitive**: Executive dysfunction progressing to subcortical dementia.

Juvenile HD (≥60 CAGs) presents with rigidity, seizures, and rapid cognitive decline rather than chorea. Genetic anticipation is observed, particularly through paternal transmission, as the CAG repeat tends to expand during spermatogenesis.

### Therapeutic Landscape

Current pharmacotherapy for HD is limited to symptomatic management:

- **VMAT2 inhibitors**: [[Tetrabenazine]] and deutetrabenazine deplete presynaptic [[Dopamine]] stores and are approved for chorea management. Deutetrabenazine offers improved pharmacokinetics with reduced dosing frequency and fewer side effects.

- **HTT-lowering strategies**: The most promising disease-modifying approaches aim to reduce mutant HTT protein levels:
  - **Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)**: Tominersen (previously IONIS-HTTRx), an ASO targeting *HTT* mRNA for RNase H-mediated degradation, failed its Phase III GENERATION HD1 trial due to an unfavorable risk–benefit profile, likely related to non-allele-selective HTT reduction. Next-generation ASOs designed for allele-selective silencing (targeting SNPs linked to the expanded allele) are in earlier-stage clinical trials.
  - **RNA interference (RNAi)**: AMT-130 is an AAV5-delivered microRNA-based gene therapy entering Phase I/II trials for HD. It aims for durable HTT lowering with a single intracranial administration.
  - **Small-molecule splicing modulators**: Compounds like branaplam and PTC518 promote aberrant splicing of *HTT* pre-mRNA, reducing functional HTT protein levels. These oral agents are in Phase II trials.

- **Emerging approaches**: CRISPR-based gene editing to excise the expanded CAG repeat, targeted protein degradation (PROTACs/molecular glues), and neuroprotective strategies addressing downstream pathology (mitochondrial dysfunction, excitotoxicity, [[Autophagy|autophagy]] enhancement) are under preclinical and early clinical investigation.
