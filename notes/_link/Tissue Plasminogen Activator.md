# Tissue Plasminogen Activator

**Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)** is a serine protease that initiates fibrinolysis by converting [[Plasminogen]] to [[Plasmin]] at fibrin surfaces. tPA is the primary physiological activator of fibrinolysis and the most widely used thrombolytic agent in clinical medicine (alteplase, tenecteplase). Its fibrin-specific mechanism ensures that clot lysis is spatially controlled, limiting systemic proteolysis.

## Structure

- **Gene:** *PLAT* is located on chromosome 8p12, spanning ~32.4 kb with 14 exons.
- **Protein:** tPA is a 68 kDa single-chain glycoprotein (530 amino acids) composed of:
  - **Finger domain (F):** Mediates binding to fibrin; homologous to fibronectin type I domains
- **EGF-like domain (E):** Epidermal growth factor-like module; involved in receptor binding
- **Kringle 1 (K1):** Contains lysine-binding site; mediates fibrin binding
- **Kringle 2 (K2):** Contains lysine-binding site; mediates fibrin binding and substrate (plasminogen) recognition
- **Serine protease domain (SP):** Catalytic domain; cleaves plasminogen at Arg561-Val562
- **Activation:** Single-chain tPA is converted to the more active two-chain form by plasmin cleavage at Arg275-Ile276.

> [!important] Fibrin-Specific Activation
> tPA activates plasminogen ~500-fold more efficiently when both are bound to fibrin. This fibrin-specific mechanism ensures that plasmin generation is localised to the clot surface, preventing systemic fibrinogenolysis and bleeding complications.

## Mechanism of Action

tPA initiates fibrinolysis through a well-characterised mechanism:

1. **Fibrin binding:** tPA binds to fibrin via its finger and kringle domains, localising the enzyme to the clot surface.
2. **Plasminogen recruitment:** Fibrin also binds plasminogen via its kringle lysine-binding sites, bringing the enzyme and substrate into close proximity.
3. **Complex formation:** A ternary complex (tPA/fibrin/plasminogen) forms on the fibrin surface, dramatically enhancing the rate of plasminogen activation.
4. **Plasmin generation:** tPA cleaves plasminogen at Arg561-Val562, generating active plasmin.
5. **Fibrin degradation:** Plasmin cleaves cross-linked fibrin at multiple sites, generating fibrin degradation products (FDPs) including D-dimer.
6. **Positive feedback:** Plasmin cleaves single-chain tPA to generate the more active two-chain form, amplifying fibrinolysis.

## Regulation

- **PAI-1 (Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor-1):** The primary physiological inhibitor of tPA. PAI-1 rapidly inactivates free tPA in plasma, limiting systemic fibrinolysis. PAI-1 is itself a key [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] factor.
- **PAI-2:** Inhibits tPA less efficiently than PAI-1.
- **Fibrin:** Serves as both substrate and cofactor that accelerates tPA activity, creating a positive feedback loop at the clot surface.
- **α2-Antiplasmin:** Rapidly inactivates free plasmin, preventing systemic proteolysis.
- **Endothelial release:** tPA is stored in Weibel-Palade bodies of endothelial cells and released in response to shear stress, thrombin, and other stimuli.

## Physiological Roles

### Fibrinolysis
tPA's primary physiological role is the dissolution of fibrin clots (fibrinolysis) once tissue repair is complete. This is essential for maintaining vascular patency and preventing pathological thrombus persistence.

### Wound Healing
tPA-derived plasmin degrades ECM components and activates pro-MMPs, facilitating cell migration during tissue repair.

### Ovulation
tPA is expressed in granulosa cells and participates in follicular rupture during ovulation.

### Neuronal Plasticity
tPA is expressed in the brain and plays roles in neuronal migration, axonal growth, and long-term potentiation (LTP). tPA knockout mice have impaired learning and memory.

## Pathological Roles

### Ischaemic Stroke
tPA (alteplase) is the primary thrombolytic agent for acute ischaemic stroke, administered within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. However:
- Only ~30% of patients achieve favourable outcomes
- Risk of haemorrhagic transformation (1–6%) due to BBB disruption
- Narrow therapeutic window limits access to treatment

### Myocardial Infarction
tPA (alteplase, tenecteplase) is used for fibrinolytic therapy in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) when percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is unavailable within 120 minutes.

### Pulmonary Embolism
tPA is used for massive pulmonary embolism with haemodynamic instability.

### Haemorrhagic Transformation
Excessive tPA activity in the setting of ischaemic stroke can exacerbate BBB disruption and cause haemorrhagic transformation. Plasmin generated by tPA degrades BBB components and activates MMPs (MMP-2, MMP-9, MMP-12), contributing to vascular damage.

### Cancer
tPA is expressed by tumour cells and contributes to tumour invasion by activating plasmin that degrades ECM barriers. Elevated tPA in tumour tissue correlates with invasive potential in some cancers.

### Neurological Diseases
tPA participates in BBB remodelling following [[Ischemic Stroke|ischaemic stroke]] and in neuroinflammatory conditions. Excessive tPA activity can exacerbate neuronal damage.

## Clinical Relevance

- **Stroke thrombolysis:** Alteplase (Activase) is the standard-of-care thrombolytic for acute ischaemic stroke; tenecteplase (TNKase) is an engineered variant with longer half-life and greater fibrin specificity.
- **Cardiac thrombolysis:** Alteplase and tenecteplase are used for STEMI when PCI is unavailable.
- **D-dimer:** Fibrin degradation products generated by tPA/plasmin are used clinically as biomarkers for deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and DIC.
- **Engineered variants:** Reteplase (retavase) and pamiteplase are tPA variants with modified pharmacokinetics.
- **Combination therapy:** tPA combined with antiplatelet agents (aspirin, clopidogrel) or anticoagulants (heparin) for acute coronary syndromes.
