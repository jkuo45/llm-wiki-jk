# Dopamine o-quinone

[[Dopamine o-quinone]] (DAQ) is a highly reactive ortho-quinone oxidation product of the neurotransmitter [[Dopamine]], arising through the one-electron or two-electron oxidation of dopamine. It is the central reactive intermediate in the spontaneous non-enzymatic polymerization pathway that builds the pigment [[Neuromelanin]] within catecholaminergic neurons of the [[Substantia Nigra]] and [[Locus Coeruleus]].

## Overview
Under normal conditions dopamine is a relatively stable catecholamine, but its catechol ring is highly susceptible to autoxidation and to oxidation by tyrosinase-related and ferric/iron-dependent mechanisms. Once oxidized, dopamine loses two electrons and two protons to form dopamine o-quinone, a cyclizable electrophile. DAQ is not an end product but a branchpoint molecule: it can either (a) be reduced back to dopamine (redox cycling), (b) react with cysteine residues on proteins via Michael addition, or (c) undergo intramolecular cyclization to the leukoaminochrome (aminochrome), which then rearranges and further oxidizes to the 5,6-indolequinone unit that is incorporated into the growing neuromelanin polymer.

## Chemical Process and Structure
The pathway proceeds as follows: dopamine → dopamine o-quinone → leucoaminochrome (via intramolecular 1,4-Michael addition/cyclization) → aminochrome (5,6-indolequinone, also called [[Aminochrome]]) → further oxidation/polymerization into indolic and benzothiazine oligomers. Dopamine o-quinone and its downstream quinone/indolequinone species are electrophilic and bind covalently to nucleophilic residues (cysteine, lysine, histidine) on proteins, which may explain the protein-adduct burden observed in neuromelanin granules. The redox cycling of DAQ/semiquinone radicals also generates [[Reactive Oxygen Species]], contributing to the local oxidative milieu.

## Physiological Function
In melanized neurons, the continuous formation and sequestration of DAQ into neuromelanin is considered a protective disposal route: by polymerizing the reactive quinone, the neuron traps potentially toxic catechol-quinone species and redox-active metals (iron, copper) within insoluble granules, blunting oxidative damage and buffering cytosolic dopamine turnover. This "sink" hypothesis holds that neuromelanin accumulation is largely a by-product of lifelong dopamine metabolism and a detoxification strategy.

## Pathology and Clinical Relevance
The same chemistry that protects can also injure. DAQ and downstream [[5,6-indolequinone]] are toxic to non-melanized cells, and in vitro studies show DAQ induces apoptosis in dopaminergic neuron models, particularly when glutathione defenses are depleted. The imbalance between dopamine oxidation (driven by [[Tyrosinase]]-like activity and [[Iron]]-mediated Fenton chemistry) and antioxidant buffering is hypothesized to contribute to selective vulnerability in [[Parkinson's Disease]]. Moreover, neuromelanin's propensity to bind and release iron means that its quinone-rich matrix can become a source rather than a sink of [[Oxidative Stress]] as neurons age. DAQ-derived adducts and aminochrome are therefore studied as biomarkers of catecholamine oxidative stress and as contributors to [[Lewy Bodies]] pathology via cross-linking of [[Alpha-synuclein]].
