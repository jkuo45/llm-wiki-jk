# Melanocytes

[[Melanocytes]] are dendritic, neural-crest-derived pigment cells residing in the basal layer of the epidermis, the hair follicle, the uvea of the eye, and the inner ear. They synthesize melanin pigments—[[Eumelanin]] (black/brown) and [[Pheomelanin]] (red/yellow)—within membrane-bound organelles called melanosomes, via the pathway of [[Melanogenesis]] catalyzed by [[Tyrosinase]]. Because melanocytes and neuromelanin-containing neurons share a neural-crest ancestry and the same catechol-oxidation chemistry, studying cutaneous melanocytes illuminates brain pigment biology and the epidemiology linking [[Cutaneous Melanoma]] to [[Parkinson's Disease]].

## Development and Distribution

Melanocyte precursors (melanoblasts) migrate from the neural crest during embryogenesis to populate the skin, where they transfer melanin to keratinocytes for photoprotection. In the eye, uveal melanocytes produce iris and choroidal pigment. Unlike neuromelanin—which accumulates intracellularly in neurons—cutaneous melanin is exported to neighboring cells.

## Melanogenesis and Pigment Types

The melanogenic cascade begins with [[L-Tyrosine]] hydroxylation to [[L-DOPA]] and then dopaquinone by [[Tyrosinase]]. Cysteinyldopas and pheomelanins form under cysteine-rich, MC1R-loss conditions; eumelanin forms under high cAMP/α-MSH signaling through [[MC1R]]. The redox properties of eumelanin (a broad-band radical scavenger and UV absorber) contrast with pheomelanin, which can generate [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] under UV. This duality—protective vs. pro-oxidant—mirrors debates about whether [[Neuromelanin]] protects or stresses dopaminergic neurons.

## Link to Neuromelanin and Parkinson's Disease

The shared Tyrosinase/catecholamine pathway means melanocytes and nigral neurons are both "pigment factories" handling reactive quinones. Epidemiologically, PD is more common in individuals with high skin melanin (and in those with red-hair MC1R variants who have low eumelanin), while cutaneous melanoma and PD show inverse and shared genetic associations (e.g., [[MC1R]]). Some hypotheses posit that systemic immune or pigment-handling mechanisms in melanocytes influence neuronal vulnerability, positioning melanocytes as a peripheral model for neuromelanin pathobiology.

## Clinical Relevance

Melanocyte dysfunction underlies albinism, vitiligo, and melanoma. Insights from melanocyte biology—particularly redox handling of quinones and lysosomal pigment storage—inform understanding of [[Lysosomal Dysfunction]] and [[Oxidative Stress]] in neuromelanin neurons.
