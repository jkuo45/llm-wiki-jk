# Neurotransmitters

[[Neurotransmitters]] are endogenous chemical messengers released by a neuron into the [[Synapse]] to relay signals to a downstream cell. In the context of [[Neuromelanin]] research, the catecholamine neurotransmitters—[[Dopamine]], [[Norepinephrine]], and [[Epinephrine]]—are paramount. Collectively known as [[Catecholamines]], these molecules are not only central to brain signaling but also serve as the direct biochemical precursors of neuromelanin: their oxidative metabolism generates the [[Aminochromes]] that polymerize into the insoluble pigment found in the [[Substantia Nigra Pars Compacta]] and [[Locus Coeruleus]].

## Catecholamine Neurotransmitters

- **Dopamine:** the principal transmitter of the nigrostriatal pathway; loss of dopaminergic transmission causes the motor syndrome of [[Parkinson's Disease]]. Synthesized from [[L-Tyrosine]] via [[L-DOPA]].
- **Norepinephrine:** transmitter of the locus coeruleus; its neurons are the other major neuromelanin-bearing population.
- **Epinephrine:** adrenal-medullary and restricted CNS transmitter; also catecholaminergic.

## From Transmitter to Pigment

Under normal catabolism, catecholamines are either recycled or oxidized. Enzymatic oxidation ([[Monoamine oxidase B]], COMT) and spontaneous auto-oxidation yield dopamine-quinone and related aminochromes. These reactive intermediates covalently cross-link with proteins, lipids, and redox-active metals, polymerizing into [[Neuromelanin]]. Thus the same chemistry underpinning signaling also generates a lifelong, accumulating pigment—a unique intersection of neurotransmission and cellular "aging" material.

## Dual Role in Health and Disease

Catecholamine neurotransmitters are essential for movement, reward, attention, and autonomic control. Yet their redox lability is a double-edged sword: the [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] and quinones produced during signaling contribute to [[Oxidative Stress]] and, ultimately, to pigment deposition and neuronal vulnerability. This explains why neuromelanin-rich neurons—those with the highest catecholamine throughput—are selectively lost in PD and preferentially damaged by toxins such as [[MPP+]] and [[6-hydroxydopamine]].

## Clinical and Research Relevance

Manipulating catecholamine signaling is the basis of PD therapy ([[L-DOPA]] replenishment, MAO-B inhibitors, [[Dopamine Transporter]] imaging). Measuring transmitter metabolites in CSF and imaging presynaptic terminals ([[PET scan]], DaTscan) track disease progression and validate neurotoxin models.
