# Synapse

A [[Synapse]] is the specialized cellular junction through which a neuron (presynaptic) communicates with a target cell (postsynaptic neuron, muscle, or gland). Classical chemical synapses comprise a presynaptic terminal filled with [[Synaptic Vesicles]], a narrow synaptic cleft (~20 nm), and a postsynaptic density bearing receptors. In the brain, the dopaminergic [[Synapse]] of the [[Nigrostriatal System]]—between [[Substantia Nigra Pars Compacta]] terminals and striatal medium spiny neurons—is central to motor control and is progressively lost in [[Parkinson's Disease]] (PD).

## Structure and Transmission

Upon an action potential, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ influx triggers SNARE-mediated fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane, releasing [[Neurotransmitters]] (e.g., [[Dopamine]], [[Norepinephrine]]) into the cleft. Diffusing neurotransmitters bind postsynaptic receptors (ionotropic or metabotropic), evoking excitatory or inhibitory responses. The signal is terminated by reuptake transporters, diffusion, and enzymatic degradation. Vesicle cycling is tightly regulated by [[Alpha-synuclein]], which promotes the clustering and recycling of the readily releasable pool.

## Dopaminergic Synapses and Neuromelanin

The presynaptic terminals of neuromelanin-bearing SNpc neurons are the business end of nigrostriatal transmission. Dopamine released at these synapses acts on D1/D2 receptors to tune the [[Basal Ganglia]] motor circuit. Because dopamine is synthesized and packaged in the neuron whose soma accumulates [[Neuromelanin]], the health of the synapse is coupled to upstream pigment and quinone load. Cytosolic dopamine not stored by [[Vesicular Monoamine Transporter 2]] auto-oxidizes to [[Quinones]], stressing the terminal and contributing to the [[Pathogenic Threshold of Neuromelanin]] burden in the parent cell.

## Synaptic Plasticity and Disease

Activity-dependent changes in synaptic strength—[[Synaptic plasticity]]—underlie learning, habit formation, and motor adaptation, and are impaired when dopaminergic input fails. In PD, loss of nigrostriatal synapses reduces [[Neurotransmission]], precipitating [[Bradykinesia]] and [[Rigidity]]. Presynaptic dysfunction (measurable by [[Positron Emission Tomography]]) precedes cell-body loss, making the synapse a critical early therapeutic target. Aggregation of α-synuclein at the synapse disrupts vesicle trafficking and may spread pathology trans-neuronally.

## Research Relevance

Synaptic markers, vesicle proteins, and dopaminergic terminal imaging are used to stage PD and test neuroprotective drugs. Preserving or restoring nigrostriatal synaptic function—rather than merely replacing dopamine—is a major goal for modifying disease course.
