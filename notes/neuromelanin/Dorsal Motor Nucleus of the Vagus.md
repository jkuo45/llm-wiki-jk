# Dorsal Motor Nucleus of the Vagus

The [[Dorsal Motor Nucleus of the Vagus]] (DMV) is a cranial nerve nucleus for the vagus nerve located in the medulla oblongata, lying on the floor of the fourth ventricle beneath the area postrema. It provides the parasympathetic preganglionic motor output that controls visceral smooth muscle and glands of the thoracic and abdominal organs, particularly the gastrointestinal tract.

## Anatomical and Neurochemical Organization
The DMV contains a dense population of visceromotor neurons that project via the vagus nerve to intramural ganglia of the stomach, intestine, pancreas, and heart. A notable feature is that, like the [[Substantia Nigra]] and [[Locus Coeruleus]], the DMV harbors neurons that contain [[Neuromelanin]]—a pigment more commonly associated with catecholaminergic nuclei, reflecting the nucleus's biosynthetic links to catecholamine handling and its susceptibility to pigment-related pathology.

## Neuromelanin and Vulnerability
Along with the substantia nigra and locus coeruleus, the DMV is among the earliest brainstem structures to show [[Neuromelanin]]-linked neurodegeneration in [[Parkinson's Disease]] (PD). Pathological staging systems (e.g., Braak staging) place the DMV in the prodromal, premotor phases of PD, often before substantial loss in the substantia nigra. This early involvement is thought to underpin the prominent non-motor features of PD.

## Physiological Function
Through vagal efferents, the DMV regulates gastric acid secretion, motility, and pancreatic exocrine output, and modulates heart rate via contributions to cardioinhibitory tone. Its activity is integrated with central autonomic networks and is sensitive to ascending inputs from the nucleus tractus solitarius.

## Pathology and Clinical Relevance
Degeneration of DMV neurons is strongly associated with the autonomic dysfunction seen in PD, especially gastrointestinal dysmotility and constipation—among the most common and earliest non-motor symptoms. Because the vagus nerve also serves as a potential route for pathological alpha-synuclein propagation from the gut to the brainstem (the "gut-first" or ascending Braak hypothesis), the DMV is central to debates about how [[Alpha-synuclein]] pathology enters the central nervous system. Loss of [[Neuromelanin]]-pigmented DMV neurons thus provides both a staging biomarker for early PD and a therapeutic target for managing autonomic symptoms. Vagal nerve stimulation and severed/transected vagal pathways have also been studied in relation to PD risk, underscoring the nucleus's relevance to disease initiation.
