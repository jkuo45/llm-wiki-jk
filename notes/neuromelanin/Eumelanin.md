# Eumelanin

[[Eumelanin]] is the dark brown-to-black form of the melanin pigment family, a heterogeneous nitrogenous biopolymer built from the oxidative polymerization of indolic monomers derived from [[L-Tyrosine]] and [[L-DOPA]]. In the skin and hair, eumelanin is produced by melanocytes and is the predominant photoprotective pigment. Within the central nervous system, eumelanin is believed to constitute the outer shell of [[Neuromelanin]] granules, encasing a more weakly pigmented, sulfur-rich (pheomelanin-like) core.

## Structure and Biosynthesis
Eumelanin is not a single defined molecule but a cross-linked network of oligomeric units, principally 5,6-dihydroxyindole (DHI) and 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA), produced via the Raper-Mason pathway. Tyrosinase hydroxylates [[L-Tyrosine]] to [[L-DOPA]], then oxidizes L-DOPA to dopaquinone; subsequent cyclizations yield the indolic monomers that polymerize into eumelanin. The ratio of DHI to DHICA, and the extent of carboxylation, determines the polymer's stability and metal-binding capacity. In neuromelanin, the eumelanic surface is thought to be rich in catechol-derived quinone moieties, giving the granule its strong affinity for [[Iron]] and other redox-active metals.

## Chemical Properties
Eumelanin is renowned for its broad-band UV-visible absorption, radical-scavenging capacity, and ability to bind cations. These properties make it an effective photoprotectant and antioxidant in peripheral tissues. However, eumelanin is also a semiconductor-like polymer capable of redox cycling, meaning it can both quench and, under certain conditions, generate [[Reactive Oxygen Species]], depending on its redox state and metal load.

## Physiological Function in the Brain
As the surface layer of neuromelanin, eumelanin contributes to the granule's hypothesized role as a metal-sequestration and detoxification depot in catecholaminergic neurons of the [[Substantia Nigra]] and [[Locus Coeruleus]]. By trapping iron and buffering cytosolic catechol-quinones (e.g., [[Dopamine o-quinone]]), eumelanin-rich neuromelanin may protect neurons from [[Oxidative Stress]] during decades of dopamine turnover.

## Pathology and Clinical Relevance
The duality of eumelanin is central to neurodegeneration debates. While its metal-binding and radical-scavenging actions are protective, the high surface area and catechol-quinone content of eumelanic neuromelanin can, when iron-loaded and aged, become a pro-oxidant source that amplifies neuronal injury in [[Parkinson's Disease]]. Differences in eumelanin versus pheomelanin content between individuals have also been explored as modifiers of PD risk. Understanding the eumelanin component of neuromelanin is therefore important for interpreting the pigment's roles in both protection and the selective vulnerability of pigmented neurons, and for designing melanin-targeted imaging probes such as [[Flortaucipir]].
