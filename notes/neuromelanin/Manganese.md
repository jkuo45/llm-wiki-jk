# Manganese

[[Manganese]] (Mn, atomic number 25) is an essential trace transition metal required as a cofactor for enzymes such as manganese superoxide dismutase ([[MnSOD]]), arginase, and pyruvate carboxylase. Despite its nutritional necessity, chronic overexposure—via mining, welding fumes, or contaminated water—is neurotoxic and produces a distinctive Parkinsonian-like disorder called [[Manganism]]. In the neuromelanin field, manganese is notable for its high binding affinity to [[Neuromelanin]], which can concentrate and retain the metal within dopaminergic neurons and modulate pigment redox chemistry.

## Chemical Properties and Bioaccumulation

Mn exists in multiple oxidation states (II, III, IV, VII), with Mn²⁺ and Mn³⁺ predominant biologically. It is absorbed in the gut, transported bound to transferrin and α₂-macroglobulin, and taken up by the brain via the transferrin receptor and the [[Dopamine Transporter]]/DAT-independent routes. Once in the [[Substantia Nigra Pars Compacta]], Mn preferentially accumulates in mitochondria and lysosomes. Neuromelanin's catechol-derived polymeric matrix chelates Mn with high avidity, effectively trapping the metal and creating a localized pro-oxidant depot that can catalyze Fenton-type reactions in the presence of H₂O₂, fostering [[Oxidative Stress]].

## Mechanisms of Neurotoxicity

Unlike [[MPTP]], which kills neurons by inhibiting [[Complex I]], manganese primarily inhibits mitochondrial [[Complex II]] (succinate dehydrogenase) and disrupts Dopa/Glutamate homeostasis. It promotes [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], depolymerizes microtubules, and impairs [[Neurotransmission]] by increasing GABAergic tone while reducing dopaminergic release. The metal also activates [[Microgliosis]] and [[Neuroinflammation]], accelerating degeneration.

## Relation to Neuromelanin and Parkinson's Disease

Epidemiological and imaging studies (notably T1-hyperintense signal on [[Magnetic Resonance Imaging]] in the globus pallidus) link Mn exposure to movement disorders. Whether manganese interacts synergistically with [[Neuromelanin]] to increase PD risk is an active question: pigment-bound Mn may both shield the neuron (sequestering free metal) and endanger it (generating radicals upon redox cycling). The shared vulnerability of pigmented neurons to Mn and to catecholamine toxins underscores a convergent mechanism of selective neurodegeneration.
