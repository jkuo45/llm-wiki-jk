# Basal Ganglia

The [[Basal Ganglia]] are a set of interconnected subcortical nuclei that regulate motor control, procedural learning, and aspects of executive function. Core components include the striatum ([[Caudate Nucleus]] and [[Putamen]]), globus pallidus (internal and external segments), subthalamic nucleus, and [[Substantia Nigra Pars Compacta]] (with its reticulata output). The [[Nigrostriatal System]]—the dopaminergic projection from SNpc to striatum—is the pathway whose degeneration defines [[Parkinson's Disease]].

## Circuit Organization

Information flows through the "direct" (facilitatory) and "indirect" (inhibitory) striatal output pathways that converge on the thalamus to modulate cortical motor commands. Dopamine from SNpc D1 receptors excite the direct pathway and D2 receptors inhibit the indirect pathway; the net effect is facilitated movement. Loss of dopamine produces relative overactivity of the indirect pathway, increasing basal ganglia output inhibition of thalamus and causing hypokinesia.

## Motor, Cognitive, and Limbic Roles

Beyond movement, the basal ganglia contribute to habit learning (dorsal striatum), executive control (cognitive striatum in the caudate), and motivation/reward (ventral striatum/nucleus accumbens). Their dysfunction underlies not only parkinsonism but also Huntington's disease, dystonia, and components of [[Addiction]].

## Relation to Neuromelanin

The SNpc neurons that project to the striatum are among the most heavily [[Neuromelanin]]-pigmented cells in the brain. Their selective loss in PD disconnects the basal ganglia motor circuit, producing the classic triad of resting tremor, rigidity, and [[Bradykinesia]]. Neuromelanin's role as a metal buffer and toxin sink may explain why precisely these pigmented neurons—rather than other dopamine cells—degenerate.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus restores circuit balance in advanced PD. [[L-DOPA]] and dopamine agonists compensate for lost nigral input. Understanding basal ganglia physiology is essential for interpreting both the motor and non-motor features of parkinsonism and for designing circuit-based therapies.
