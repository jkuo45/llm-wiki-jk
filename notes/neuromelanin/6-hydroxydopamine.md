# 6-hydroxydopamine

[[6-hydroxydopamine]] (6-OHDA) is a structurally dopamine-like neurotoxin that is selectively taken up by catecholaminergic neurons and used extensively in research to model [[Parkinson's Disease]]. It destroys these neurons through the generation of reactive oxygen species and the disruption of [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]].

## Overview

6-OHDA is a hydroxylated analogue of dopamine lacking the meta-hydroxyl required for normal catecholamine function. Because it is recognized by the high-affinity catecholamine uptake systems, 6-OHDA is concentrated specifically in dopaminergic and noradrenergic neurons, where it exerts selective toxicity. For this reason it cannot cross the blood–brain barrier and must be injected directly into the brain (e.g., into the [[Substantia Nigra Pars Compacta]] or striatum) to produce a lesion.

## Mechanism of Action

Once inside the neuron, 6-OHDA undergoes rapid auto-oxidation, generating superoxide radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry. It also inhibits mitochondrial respiration at [[Complex I]] and [[Complex IV]], depleting ATP and triggering [[Oxidative Stress]]. The combination of oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, together with energetic failure, culminates in apoptotic and necrotic cell death. Injected unilaterally, 6-OHDA produces the classic rotational model in which rats turn away from the lesioned side when challenged with dopamine agonists such as amphetamine.

## Physiological & Experimental Relevance

The 6-OHDA lesion model has been instrumental in delineating the anatomy of the [[Nigrostriatal System]], validating the efficacy of [[L-DOPA]] and dopamine agonists, and testing neuroprotective and cell-replacement strategies. It faithfully reproduces the loss of striatal dopamine terminals and the motor deficits (akin to [[Bradykinesia]]) seen in PD, although it does not recapitulate the intraneuronal [[Lewy Bodies]] that define human disease.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

As a tool, 6-OHDA has clarified that oxidative stress and mitochondrial impairment are sufficient to kill dopaminergic neurons, supporting the [[Oxidative Stress]] and [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]] hypotheses of PD. It is also a cautionary reminder that environmental or endogenous catechols with redox-cycling potential could contribute to selective neuronal vulnerability. Comparative work with [[MPTP]] and rotenone has refined understanding of which mitochondrial targets are most critical in parkinsonism.
