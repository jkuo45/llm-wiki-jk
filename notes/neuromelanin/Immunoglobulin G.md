# Immunoglobulin G

[[Immunoglobulin G]] (IgG) is the most abundant antibody class in human blood and extracellular fluid, accounting for ~75–80% of serum immunoglobulins. It is a ~150 kDa Y-shaped glycoprotein composed of two heavy and two light chains, with a single antigen-binding fragment (Fab) pair and a crystallizable fragment (Fc) that engages Fc receptors and complement. In the brain, IgG normally enters only in small amounts via a compromised or aged blood-brain barrier, but its accumulation in specific neurons has become a notable finding in [[Parkinson's Disease]].

## Structure and Immune Function
IgG provides long-term humoral immunity through neutralization of pathogens, opsonization, and complement activation. Its four subclasses (IgG1–IgG4) differ in effector functions. Under healthy conditions the intact blood-brain barrier largely excludes IgG from the parenchyma; perivascular and choroid-plexus macrophages handle CNS immune surveillance.

## Mechanism: IgG in the Parkinsonian Brain
Postmortem studies of PD patients have reported IgG deposition within [[Neuromelanin]]-containing neurons of the [[Substantia Nigra]] and [[Locus Coeruleus]], often colocalized with neuronal loss. Several mechanisms may account for this:
- **Barrier breakdown**: Age- and disease-related compromise of the blood-brain barrier permits serum IgG infiltration.
- **Local synthesis**: Intrathecal B-cell/plasma-cell activity could produce CNS-restricted IgG.
- **Receptor-mediated binding**: IgG may bind neuronal Fc receptors or be taken up by damaged neurons.
Crucially, IgG signal in pigmented neurons correlates spatially with the regions of greatest dopaminergic degeneration, implicating adaptive immunity in PD pathogenesis.

## Physiological and Pathological Relevance
The presence of IgG in vulnerable neurons suggests that [[Neuroinflammation]] in PD is not solely microglial (innate) but includes an adaptive, antibody-mediated component. IgG could contribute to injury by activating complement, by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity involving [[Lymphocytes]], or by marking neurons for immune clearance. Conversely, IgG deposition may be a secondary epiphenomenon of blood-brain barrier leakage rather than a primary driver. Distinguishing these possibilities is an active research area, with implications for whether immunosuppressive or antibody-targeted therapies could modify PD progression.

## Clinical and Research Implications
Detecting disease-specific autoantibodies (e.g., against [[Alpha-synuclein]] or neuronal proteins) in serum or cerebrospinal fluid is being explored as a PD biomarker. The IgG–neuromelanin association also supports the broader theme that pigmented neurons exist at the interface of catecholamine metabolism, oxidative stress, and immune attack. Longitudinal studies aim to determine whether IgG infiltration precedes or follows nigral cell death, informing its utility as a prognostic marker.
