## Autophagy: definition and mechanisms

The 2016 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to [[Yoshinori Ohsumi]] for his initial elucidation of the morphological and molecular mechanisms of autophagy in the 1990s,. Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved [[Lysosome|lysosomal]] catabolic process by which cells degrade and recycle intracellular endogenous (damaged organelles, misfolded or [mutant proteins](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/mutant-protein) and macromolecules) and exogenous (viruses and bacteria) components to maintain cellular homeostasis,. The specificity of the cargo and the delivery route to [[Lysosome|lysosomes]] distinguishes the three major types of autophagy. [[Microautophagy]] involves the direct engulfment of cargo in endosomal/lysosomal [membrane invaginations](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/membrane-invagination). [[Chaperone-Mediated Autophagy]] (CMA) recycles [soluble proteins](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/soluble-protein) with an exposed [amino acid motif](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/protein-motif) (KFERQ) that is recognized by the [heat shock protein hsc70](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/heat-shock-cognate-protein-70); these proteins are internalized by binding to lysosomal receptors (LAMP-2A). Autophagy occurs at basal levels under physiological conditions and can also be upregulated in response to stressful stimuli such as [[notes/_link/Hypoxia|hypoxia]], nutritional deprivation, DNA damage, and [cytotoxic agents](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/cytotoxic-agent),. The molecular machinery that mediates the autophagic process is evolutionarily conserved in higher eukaryotes and regulated by specific genes (ATG genes), which were initially characterized in yeast,. Each stage is controlled by different [protein complexes](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/protein-complexes) regulated by the activation or inactivation of several stress-responsive pathways, such as those involving [[notes/_link/mTOR|mammalian target of rapamycin]] (mTOR—nutrient), [[notes/_link/AMPK|AMP-activated protein kinase]] (AMPK—energy) and [[HIF1A|hypoxia-inducible factors]] (HIFs—stress),. Regarding initialization, the activation of the [[ULK1]] complex (ULK1/2, [[Atg13]], [[FIP200]] and [[Atg101]]) signals for autophagosome nucleation under the control of the [[notes/_link/PI3K|PI3K III complex]] (PI3KIII, [[Beclin1|Beclin-1]], Atg14/Barkor, Vps15 and [[Ambra-1]]), whose activation induces PIP3 (phosphatidyl inositol 3 phosphate) production, which in turn recruits other [Atg](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/thymocyte-antibody) proteins to form the phagophore. Subsequently, two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems mediate the recruitment of ATG12–ATG5 and microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 ([[LC3]]) proteins to the phagophore, allowing its expansion and closure to form the mature autophagosome. Throughout the past decade, autophagy has attracted considerable attention as a potential target of pharmacological agents or dietary interventions that inhibit or activate this process for several human disorders, including infections and [inflammatory diseases](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/inflammatory-disorder), [neurodegeneration](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/nerve-degeneration), metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, obesity and [[Cancer|cancer]],.

## Autophagy and cancer

The role of autophagy in [[Cancer|cancer]] is complex, and its function may vary according to several biological factors, including tumor type, progression stage and [genetic](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/genetics) landscape, along with [[RAS|oncogene]] activation and [[notes/_link/p53|tumor suppressor]] inactivation,. Thus, autophagy can be related either to the prevention of tumorigenesis or to the enabling of [cancer cell](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/cancer-cell) adaptation, proliferation, survival and [metastasis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/metastatic-carcinoma),. The initial indication that autophagy could have an important role in tumor suppression came from several studies exploring the essential autophagy gene [[Beclin1|BECN1]], which encodes the Beclin-1 protein, in different [cellular models](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/cellular-model). Liang et al. demonstrated that BECN1 was frequently monoallelically deleted in ovarian, [[notes/_link/Breast Cancer|breast]] and [testicular cancer](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/testis-cancer). Moreover, mice harboring allelic loss of BECN1 had a partial autophagy deficiency and were prone to the development of [hepatocarcinoma](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/liver-cell-carcinoma) and lung tumors at an advanced age,. Once the tumor is established, the main function of autophagy is to provide a means to cope with cellular stressors, including [[notes/_link/Hypoxia|hypoxia]], nutritional and growth factor deprivation and damaging stimuli, thus allowing tumor adaptation, proliferation, survival and dissemination. Autophagy, by degrading [macromolecules](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/macromolecule) and defective organelles, supplies metabolites and upregulates mitochondrial function, supporting tumor [cell viability](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/cell-viability) even in constantly stressful environments,. Studies have demonstrated that autophagy increases in hypoxic regions of [solid tumors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/solid-malignant-neoplasm), favoring cell survival. The inhibition of autophagy leads to an intense induction of cell death in these regions,. Moreover, tumors frequently have mutations or deletions in the [[notes/_link/p53|tumor suppressor protein p53]], which also favors autophagy induction to recycle intracellular components for tumor growth. Although the basal autophagy rate is generally low in normal cells under physiological conditions, some tumors show a high level of basal autophagy, reinforcing the prosurvival role of autophagy in [[Cancer|cancer]],. [[RAS]]-transformed cancer cells undergo autophagy upregulation to supply metabolic needs and maintain functional [[notes/_link/Mitochondria|mitochondria]], which in turn favors tumor establishment,,. Autophagy also has a supportive role in metastasis by interfering with epithelial-mesenchymal transition constituents to favor tumor cell dissemination. ## Autophagy and cancer therapeutics

Because autophagy can inhibit tumor development or favor tumor growth, progression, invasion and treatment resistance, researchers proposed that autophagy modulation could be a new therapeutic strategy in the treatment of some [malignancies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/cancer),,.

Recently, we published a review on autophagy and [[Cancer|cancer]], suggesting that some challenges, such as the incomplete understanding of the relationship between autophagy, tumor resistance, and cell death, as well as the identification of new druggable targets, need to be overcome with the aim of pharmacologically modulating autophagy for cancer treatment. Some of these suggestions are based on the current literature and on previous studies published by our group demonstrating that combining different agents such as [selumetinib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/selumetinib) and [cytarabine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/cytarabine) with autophagy inhibitors (bafilomycin A1, [chloroquine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/chloroquine) or 3-methyladenine) enhanced the activity of [selumetinib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/selumetinib) and [cytarabine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/cytarabine) against [[notes/_link/Colorectal Cancer|colorectal cancer]] cells and [leukemia cells](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/leukemia-cell), respectively. Autophagy was also observed in [[Melanoma|melanoma cells]] under treatment with [palladium complex](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/palladium-complex) drugs, indicating the importance of investigating the relationship between autophagy and [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]] during new [drug development](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/drug-development). ## How does dietary restriction modulate autophagy and cancer therapy?

In [preclinical studies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/preclinical-study), [[Dietary Restriction|dietary restriction]] (DR) has been shown to extend the lifespan and reduce the development of age-related diseases such as diabetes, [[Cancer|cancer]], and neurodegenerative and cardiovascular diseases. DR promotes metabolic and cellular changes in organisms from [prokaryotes](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/prokaryote) to humans that allow adaptation to periods of limited nutrient availability. The main changes include decreased [blood glucose](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/blood-glucose) levels and growth factor signaling and the activation of [stress resistance](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/stress) pathways affecting cell growth, energy metabolism, and protection against [[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species|oxidative stress]], inflammation and cell death,. Nutrient starvation also activates autophagy in most cultured cells and organs, such as the liver and muscle, as an adaptive mechanism to stressful conditions,.

Studies demonstrate that dietary interventions can reduce tumor incidence and potentiate the effectiveness of chemo- and radiotherapy in different tumor models, highlighting dietary manipulation as a possible adjunct to standard cancer therapies,. Among the many diet regimens that have been assessed, [[notes/_link/Caloric Restriction]] (CR) and [[Intermittent Fasting|fasting]] are the methods under intense investigation in oncology,,. CR is defined as a chronic reduction in the daily [caloric intake](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/caloric-intake) by 20-40% without the incurrence of malnutrition and with the maintenance of meal frequency. In contrast, fasting is characterized by the complete deprivation of food but not water, with intervening periods of normal food intake. Based on the duration, fasting can be classified as (i) [[Intermittent Fasting]] (IF—e.g., [alternate day fasting](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/alternate-day-fasting) (≥16 hours) or 48 hours of fasting/week) or (ii) periodic fasting (PF—e.g., a minimum of 3 days of fasting every 2 or more weeks). In this article, we do not review CR studies that have been reviewed elsewhere,,; instead, we focus on studies using IF protocols as an adjuvant to cancer treatment in animals and humans.

Recently, studies in *in vitro* and *in vivo* models have shown that intermittent fasting improved the chemotherapeutic response to [[Cisplatin|cisplatin]], [doxorubicin](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/doxorubicin), [cyclophosphamide](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/cyclophosphamide), [oxaliplatin](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/oxaliplatin), [sorafenib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/sorafenib), [mitoxantrone](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/mitoxantrone), [gemcitabine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/gemcitabine), [etoposide](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/etoposide), [temozolomide](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/temozolomide) and [tyrosine kinase inhibitors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/protein-tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor) in models of [glioma](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/ganglioglioma), [neuroblastoma](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/ganglioneuroblastoma), [[Melanoma|melanoma]], [fibrosarcoma](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/fibrosarcoma) and [[notes/_link/Breast Cancer|breast cancer]], [colon cancer](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/colon-cancer), [pancreatic cancer](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/pancreas-cancer), hepatocellular cancer and lung cancer. Although the results of combining IF with [anticancer drugs](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/anticarcinogen) are encouraging, the molecular mechanisms are not completely clear. Lee et al. demonstrated that IF (48-hour fasting) reduced the glucose and [[IGF1|IGF-1]] levels by 60% and 70%, respectively, in a breast cancer animal model. In a [colon cancer](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/colon-cancer) model, IF inhibited tumor growth without causing permanent weight loss and decreased M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages in mice. *In vitro* data showed autophagy induction and [[CD73]] [downregulation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/downregulation), followed by a decrease in extracellular adenosine and the inhibition of M2 polarization due to the inactivation of JAK1/[[notes/_link/STAT3]].

When IF cycles were combined with chemotherapy, tumor growth was slowed and [overall survival](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/overall-survival) was prolonged in breast cancer, melanoma and [neuroblastoma](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/neuroblastoma) animal models. The *in vitro* data showed that this therapeutic combination resulted in increased [[notes/_link/Akt]] and [S6 kinase](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/s6-kinase) phosphorylation, caspase-3 cleavage and [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]] induction in cancer cells but not in normal cells. Other studies demonstrated that the combination of IF and [oxaliplatin](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/oxaliplatin) also reduced tumor growth and [glucose uptake](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/glucose-transport) *in vivo* and resulted in downregulated [aerobic glycolysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/aerobic-glycolysis) followed by augmented [oxidative phosphorylation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/oxidative-phosphorylation), leading to increased [[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species|oxidative stress]], decreased ATP synthesis and cell death in colon cancer cell models. Furthermore, Our group also demonstrated that nutritional deprivation enhanced the sensitivity of both wild type and BRAF <sup>V600E</sup> human melanoma cells to [[Cisplatin|cisplatin]] treatment followed by [[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] production and mitochondrial perturbation leading to [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]] without autophagy involvement in the cell death process. The combination of IF and [tyrosine kinase inhibitors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor) such as [erlotinib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/erlotinib), [gefitinib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/gefitinib), [lapatinib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/lapatinib), [crizotinib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/crizotinib) and [regorafenib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/regorafenib) promoted the sustained inhibition of the [MAPK](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/mitogen-activated-protein-kinase) pathway, leading to [antiproliferative effects](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/antiproliferative-activity) in breast, colorectal and lung cancer cell models, as well as to the inhibition of tumor growth in an *in vivo* model of lung cancer. The combination of IF and the multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor [sorafenib](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/sorafenib) exhibited an additive effect in inhibiting hepatocarcinoma [cell proliferation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/cell-proliferation) and [glucose uptake](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/glucose-uptake) as well as downregulating the [MAPK](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/mitogen-activated-protein-kinase) pathway and the gene expression of BIRC5, [DKK1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/dkk1), TRIB3 and VEGF, which are commonly altered in hepatocarcinoma cells. A small pilot study comprising 10 patients diagnosed with breast, prostate, esophageal or lung cancer in advanced stages suggested that periods of intermittent fasting before and after chemotherapy reduces the self-reported side effects of therapy, especially those associated with the [gastrointestinal system](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/gastrointestinal-system), as well as [weakness](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/weakness) and fatigue. Additionally, no negative effect on the chemotherapy response or persistent weight loss was observed,. In another clinical trial, the combination of IF and platinum-based chemotherapy promoted pathologic complete or partial radiographic responses in the majority of patients affected by different stages and types of tumors, such as ovarian, uterine, breast and [urothelial cancer](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/urothelial-cancer). A reduction in leukocyte DNA damage, in addition to decreased levels of circulating [[IGF1|IGF-1]], has also been reported. Both studies established the feasibility of IF in humans and suggested that combining IF with cytotoxic agents in the clinical context is safe and may be well-tolerated by patients, although this regimen may be psychologically uncomfortable for some individuals,,,. Another novel pharmacological therapeutic strategy currently being investigated to treat cancer is the combination of [[Caloric Restriction Mimetics|caloric restriction mimetics]] (CRMs) with cytotoxic agents. CRMs are compounds that have different chemical structures and mimic the biochemical and functional effects of CR, such as the activation of [[notes/_link/AMPK]] and inhibition of [[notes/_link/mTOR]] leading to autophagy induction, the depletion of acetyl-CoA and ATP, and the reduced utilization of glucose, without eliciting the discomfort of CR. Several studies demonstrated the tumor-suppressive effects of CRM agents, for example, 2-deoxy-glucose, [[notes/_link/Metformin|metformin]],, [[notes/_link/mTOR]] inhibitors, [resveratrol](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/resveratrol),, [hydroxycitrate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/hydroxycitric-acid), [[notes/_link/Spermidine|spermidine]], and [natural compounds](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/natural-product) such as [curcumin](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/curcumin), in combination with antitumor treatments in different cancer models. In this review, we highlighted the concepts of autophagy, especially in relation to tumorigenesis, as well as the potential of autophagy as a therapeutic target in the treatment of different malignancies. We also pointed out the possibility of using [[Dietary Restriction|dietary manipulation]] as an autophagy modulator as well as a cost-effective intervention to increase therapeutic response in the challenging oncologic arena. Furthermore, [[Intermittent Fasting|fasting]] may protect normal cells from the toxicity of anticancer agents, reducing side effects in patients and increasing the detrimental effects of chemotherapy, radiotherapy and targeted therapy on tumor cells. However, additional studies are required to better understand the molecular mechanisms evoked by fasting, aiming to identify the context in which fasting may be beneficial as an adjunct to cancer treatment. Moreover, further knowledge may also lead to the development of novel pharmacological protocols that replicate effects similar to those of fasting and are more suitable for different oncologic patients.
