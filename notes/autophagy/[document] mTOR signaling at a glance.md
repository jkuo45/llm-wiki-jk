The [[mammalian target of rapamycin]] ([[notes/_link/mTOR]]) signaling pathway integrates both intracellular and extracellular signals and serves as a central regulator of [[cell metabolism]], growth, proliferation and survival. Discoveries that have been made over the last decade show that the mTOR pathway is activated during various cellular processes (e.g. [[tumor formation]] and [[angiogenesis]], [[insulin resistance]], [[adipogenesis]] and [[T-lymphocyte activation]]) and is deregulated in human diseases such as [[Cancer|cancer]] and [[type 2 diabetes]]. These observations have attracted broad scientific and clinical interest in [[notes/_link/mTOR]]. This is highlighted by the growing use of [[notes/_link/mTOR]] inhibitors \[[[rapamycin]] and its analogues ([[Rapalog|rapalogues]])\] in pathological settings, including the treatment of solid tumors, [[organ transplantation]], [[coronary restenosis]] and [[rheumatoid arthritis]]. Here, we highlight and summarize the current understanding of how [[notes/_link/mTOR]] nucleates distinct multi-protein complexes, how intra- and extracellular signals are processed by the [[notes/_link/mTOR]] complexes, and how such signals affect [[cell metabolism]], growth, proliferation and survival.

## mTOR structure and organization into multi-protein complexes

The [[notes/_link/mTOR]] protein is a 289-kDa [[serine-threonine kinase]] that belongs to the [[phospho-inositide 3-kinase]] ([[notes/_link/PI3K]])-related kinase family and is conserved throughout evolution. The poster depicts an overview of [[notes/_link/mTOR]] structural domains. [[notes/_link/mTOR]] nucleates at least two distinct multi-protein complexes, [[mTORC1|mTOR complex 1]] ([[mTORC1]]) and [[mTORC2|mTOR complex 2]] ([[mTORC2]]) (reviewed by [Guertin and Sabatini, 2007](#ref19)).

### mTORC1

[[mTORC1]] has five components: [[notes/_link/mTOR]], which is the catalytic subunit of the complex; [[Raptor|regulatory-associated protein of mTOR]] ([[Raptor]]); [[mLST8|mammalian lethal with Sec13 protein 8]] ([[mLST8]], also known as [[GβL]]); [[PRAS40|proline-rich AKT substrate 40 kDa]] ([[PRAS40]]); and [[Deptor|DEP-domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein]] ([[Deptor]]) ([Peterson et al., 2009](#ref50)). The exact function of most of the [[notes/_link/mTOR]]-interacting proteins in [[mTORC1]] still remains elusive. It has been proposed that [[Raptor]] might affect [[mTORC1]] activity by regulating assembly of the complex and by recruiting substrates for [[notes/_link/mTOR]] ([Hara et al., 2002](#ref22); [Kim et al., 2002](#ref34)). The role of [[mLST8]] in [[mTORC1]] function is also unclear, as deletion of this protein does not affect [[mTORC1]] activity in vivo ([Guertin et al., 2006](#ref20)). [[PRAS40]] and [[Deptor]] have been characterized as distinct negative regulators of [[mTORC1]] ([Peterson et al., 2009](#ref50); [Sancak et al., 2007](#ref56); [Vander Haar et al., 2007](#ref67)). When the activity of [[mTORC1]] is reduced, [[PRAS40]] and [[Deptor]] are recruited to the complex, where they promote the inhibition of [[mTORC1]]. It was proposed that [[PRAS40]] regulates [[mTORC1]] [[kinase]] activity by functioning as a direct inhibitor of substrate binding ([Wang et al., 2007](#ref68)). Upon activation, [[mTORC1]] directly phosphorylates [[PRAS40]] and [[Deptor]], which reduces their physical interaction with [[mTORC1]] and further activates [[mTORC1]] signaling ([Peterson et al., 2009](#ref50); [Wang et al., 2007](#ref68)).

### mTORC2

[[mTORC2]] comprises six different proteins, several of which are common to [[mTORC1]] and [[mTORC2]]: [[notes/_link/mTOR]]; [[Rictor|rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR]] ([[Rictor]]); [[mSIN1|mammalian stress-activated protein kinase interacting protein]] ([[mSIN1]]); [[Protor-1|protein observed with Rictor-1]] ([[Protor-1]]); [[mLST8]]; and [[Deptor]]. There is some evidence that [[Rictor]] and [[mSIN1]] stabilize each other, establishing the structural foundation of [[mTORC2]] ([Frias et al., 2006](#ref15); [Jacinto et al., 2006](#ref31)). [[Rictor]] also interacts with [[Protor-1]], but the physiological function of this interaction is not clear ([Thedieck et al., 2007](#ref64); [Woo et al., 2007](#ref69)). Similar to its role in [[mTORC1]], [[Deptor]] negatively regulates [[mTORC2]] activity ([Peterson et al., 2009](#ref50)); so far, [[Deptor]] is the only characterized endogenous inhibitor of [[mTORC2]]. Finally, [[mLST8]] is essential for [[mTORC2]] function, as knockout of this protein severely reduces the stability and the activity of this complex ([Guertin et al., 2006](#ref20)).

Now that many [[notes/_link/mTOR]]-interacting proteins have been identified, additional biochemical studies will be needed to clarify the functions of these proteins in [[notes/_link/mTOR]] signaling and their potential implications in health and disease. Below, we discuss current understanding of the functions of [[mTORC1]] and [[mTORC2]].

## mTORC1: a master regulator of cell growth and metabolism

[[mTORC1]] positively regulates [[cell growth]] and [[cell proliferation]] by promoting many [[anabolism|anabolic processes]], including [[biosynthesis]] of proteins, lipids and organelles, and by limiting [[catabolism|catabolic processes]] such as [[Autophagy|autophagy]]. Much of the knowledge about [[mTORC1]] function comes from the use of the bacterial [[macrolide]] [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]]. Upon entering the cell, [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] binds to [[FKBP12|FK506-binding protein of 12 kDa]] ([[FKBP12]]) and interacts with the [[FKBP12]]-rapamycin binding domain ([[FRB]]) of [[notes/_link/mTOR]], thus inhibiting [[mTORC1]] functions (reviewed by [Guertin and Sabatini, 2007](#ref19)). In contrast to its effect on [[mTORC1]], [[FKBP12]]-[[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] cannot physically interact with or acutely inhibit [[mTORC2]] ([Jacinto et al., 2004](#ref30); [Sarbassov et al., 2004](#ref58)). On the basis of these observations, [[mTORC1]] and [[mTORC2]] have been respectively characterized as the [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]]-sensitive and [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]]-insensitive complexes. However, this paradigm might not be entirely accurate, as chronic [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] treatment can, in some cases, inhibit [[mTORC2]] activity by blocking its assembly ([Sarbassov et al., 2006](#ref60)). In addition, recent reports suggest that important [[mTORC1]] functions are resistant to inhibition by [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] ([Choo et al., 2008](#ref7); [Feldman et al., 2009](#ref12); [Garcia-Martinez et al., 2009](#ref18); [Thoreen et al., 2009](#ref65)).

### Protein synthesis

[[mTORC1]] positively controls [[protein synthesis]], which is required for [[cell growth]], through various downstream effectors. [[mTORC1]] promotes [[protein synthesis]] by phosphorylating the [[4E-BP1|eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E)-binding protein 1]] ([[4E-BP1]]) and the [[S6K1|p70 ribosomal S6 kinase 1]] ([[S6K1]]). The phosphorylation of [[4E-BP1]] prevents its binding to [[eIF4E]], enabling [[eIF4E]] to promote [[cap-dependent translation]] (reviewed by [Richter and Sonenberg, 2005](#ref54)). The stimulation of [[S6K1]] activity by [[mTORC1]] leads to increases in [[mRNA]] biogenesis, [[cap-dependent translation]] and elongation, and the translation of ribosomal proteins through regulation of the activity of many proteins, such as [[SKAR|S6K1 aly/REF-like target]] ([[SKAR]]), [[PDCD4|programmed cell death 4]] ([[PDCD4]]), [[eEF2K|eukaryotic elongation factor 2 kinase]] ([[eEF2K]]) and [[ribosomal protein S6]] ([[S6]]) (reviewed by [Ma and Blenis, 2009](#ref44)). The activation of [[mTORC1]] has also been shown to promote [[ribosome biogenesis]] by stimulating the transcription of [[ribosomal RNA]] through a process involving the [[PP2A|protein phosphatase 2A]] ([[PP2A]]) and the [[TIF-IA|transcription initiation factor IA]] ([[TIF-IA]]) ([Mayer et al., 2004](#ref47)).

### Autophagy

[[Autophagy]] – that is, the sequestration of intra - cellular components within [[autophagosomes]] and their degradation by [[lysosomes]] – is a [[catabolic process]] that is important in organelle degradation and protein turnover. When [[nutrient availability]] is limited, the degradation of organelles and protein complexes through [[Autophagy|autophagy]] provides biological material to sustain [[anabolism|anabolic processes]] such as [[protein synthesis]] and [[energy production]]. Studies have shown that [[mTORC1]] inhibition increases [[Autophagy|autophagy]], whereas stimulation of [[mTORC1]] reduces this process (reviewed by [Codogno and Meier, 2005](#ref8)). We have observed that [[mTORC1]] controls [[Autophagy|autophagy]] through an unknown mechanism that is essentially insensitive to inhibition by [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] ([Thoreen et al., 2009](#ref65)). It was recently shown by three independent groups that [[mTORC1]] controls [[Autophagy|autophagy]] through the regulation of a protein complex composed of [[ULK1|unc-51-like kinase 1]] ([[ULK1]]), [[Atg13|autophagy-related gene 13]] ([[Atg13]]) and [[FIP200|focal adhesion kinase family-interacting protein of 200 kDa]] ([[FIP200]]) ([Ganley et al., 2009](#ref16); [Hosokawa et al., 2009](#ref25); [Jung et al., 2009](#ref33)). These studies have revealed that [[mTORC1]] represses [[Autophagy|autophagy]] by phosphorylating and thereby repressing [[ULK1]] and [[Atg13]].

### Lipid synthesis

The role of [[mTORC1]] in regulating [[lipid synthesis]], which is required for [[cell growth]] and [[cell proliferation]], is beginning to be appreciated. It has been demonstrated that [[mTORC1]] positively regulates the activity of [[SREBP1|sterol regulatory element binding protein 1]] ([[SREBP1]]) ([Porstmann et al., 2008](#ref51)) and of [[PPARγ|peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ]] ([[PPARγ]]) ([Kim and Chen, 2004](#ref36)), two [[transcription factors]] that control the expression of genes encoding proteins involved in lipid and [[cholesterol homeostasis]]. Blocking [[notes/_link/mTOR]] with [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] reduces the expression and the transactivation activity of [[PPARγ]] ([Kim and Chen, 2004](#ref36)). The molecular mechanism of [[SREBP1]] activation by [[mTORC1]] is unknown. Additionally, [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] reduces the phosphorylation of [[lipin-1]] ([Huffman et al., 2002](#ref26)), a [[phosphatidic acid]] ([[PA]]) phosphatase that is involved in glycerolipid synthesis and in the coactivation of many [[transcription factors]] linked to lipid metabolism, including [[PPARγ]], [[PPARα]] and [[PGC1-α]]. The precise impact of [[lipin-1]] phosphorylation on lipid synthesis remains to be established.

### Mitochondrial metabolism and biogenesis

[[Mitochondrial metabolism]] and [[mitochondrial biogenesis]] are both regulated by [[mTORC1]]. Inhibition of [[mTORC1]] by [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] lowers [[mitochondrial membrane potential]], [[oxygen consumption]] and cellular [[ATP]] levels, and profoundly alters the mitochondrial phosphoproteome ([Schieke et al., 2006](#ref61)). Recently, it has been observed that [[mitochondrial DNA]] copy number, as well as the expression of many genes encoding proteins involved in [[oxidative metabolism]], are reduced by [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]] and increased by mutations that activate [[mTORC1]] signaling ([Chen et al., 2008](#ref6); [Cunningham et al., 2007](#ref10)). Additionally, conditional deletion of [[Raptor]] in mouse [[skeletal muscle]] reduces the expression of genes involved in [[mitochondrial biogenesis]] ([Bentzinger et al., 2008](#ref2)). Cunningham and colleagues have discovered that [[mTORC1]] controls the transcriptional activity of [[PGC1-α|PPARγ coactivator 1]] ([[PGC1-α]]), a nuclear [[cofactor]] that plays a key role in [[mitochondrial biogenesis]] and [[oxidative metabolism]], by directly altering its physical interaction with another [[transcription factor]], namely [[YY1|yin-yang 1]] ([[YY1]]) ([Cunningham et al., 2007](#ref10)).

## Many roads lead to mTORC1: overview of a complex signaling network

[[mTORC1]] integrates four major signals – [[growth factors]], [[energy status]], [[oxygen]] and [[amino acids]] – to regulate many processes that are involved in the promotion of [[cell growth]]. One of the most important sensors involved in the regulation of [[mTORC1]] activity is the [[TSC|tuberous sclerosis complex]] ([[TSC]]), which is a heterodimer that comprises [[TSC1]] (also known as [[hamartin]]) and [[TSC2]] (also known as [[tuberin]]). [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] functions as a [[GTPase-activating protein]] ([[GAP]]) for the small [[RAS|Ras]]-related [[GTPase]] [[Rheb]] ([[Ras homolog enriched in brain]]). The active, [[GTP]]-bound form of [[Rheb]] directly interacts with [[mTORC1]] to stimulate its activity ([Long et al., 2005](#ref42); [Sancak et al., 2007](#ref56)). The exact mechanism by which [[Rheb]] activates [[mTORC1]] remains to be determined. As a [[Rheb]]-specific [[GAP]], [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] negatively regulates [[mTORC1]] signaling by converting [[Rheb]] into its inactive [[GDP]]-bound state ([Inoki et al., 2003](#ref28); [Tee et al., 2003](#ref63)). Consistent with a role of [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] in the negative regulation of [[mTORC1]], inactivating mutations or loss of heterozygosity of [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] give rise to [[tuberous sclerosis]], a disease associated with the presence of numerous [[benign tumors]] that are composed of enlarged and disorganized cells (reviewed by [Crino et al., 2006](#ref9)).

### Growth factors

[[Growth factors]] stimulate [[mTORC1]] through the activation of the canonical [[insulin]] and [[RAS|Ras]] signaling pathways. The stimulation of these pathways increases the phosphorylation of [[TSC2]] by [[notes/_link/Akt|protein kinase B]] ([[PKB]], also known as [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]]) ([Inoki et al., 2002](#ref27); [Potter et al., 2002](#ref52)), by [[ERK1_2|extracellular-signal-regulated kinase 1/2]] ([[ERK1_2]]) ([Ma et al., 2005](#ref43)), and by [[RSK1|p90 ribosomal S6 kinase 1]] ([[RSK1]]) ([Roux et al., 2004](#ref55)), and leads to the inactivation of [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] and thus to the activation of [[mTORC1]]. Additionally, [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] activation by [[growth factors]] can activate [[mTORC1]] in a [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]]-independent manner by promoting the phosphorylation and dissociation of [[PRAS40]] from [[mTORC1]] ([Sancak et al., 2007](#ref56); [Vander Haar et al., 2007](#ref67); [Wang et al., 2007](#ref68)).

The binding of [[insulin]] to its cell-surface receptor promotes the [[tyrosine kinase]] activity of the [[insulin receptor]], the recruitment of [[IRS1|insulin receptor substrate 1]] ([[IRS1]]), the production of [[PtdIns(3,4,5)P3|phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-triphosphate]] \[[[PtdIns(3,4,5)P3]]\] through the activation of [[notes/_link/PI3K]], and the recruitment and activation of [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] at the [[plasma membrane]]. In many cell types, activation of [[mTORC1]] strongly represses the [[notes/_link/PI3K]]-[[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] axis upstream of [[notes/_link/PI3K]]. Activation of [[S6K1]] by [[mTORC1]] promotes the phosphorylation of [[IRS1]] and reduces its stability (reviewed by [Harrington et al., 2005](#ref24)). This auto-regulatory pathway, characterized as the [[S6K1-dependent negative feedback loop]], has been shown to have profound implications for both metabolic diseases and [[tumorigenesis]] (reviewed by [Manning, 2004](#ref45)). Other pathways that are independent of [[IRS1]] are also likely to contribute to the retro-inhibition of [[mTORC1]]. For example, loss of [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] suppresses [[PDGFR|platelet-derived growth factor receptor]] ([[PDGFR]]) expression in a [[Rapamycin|rapamycin]]-sensitive manner ([Zhang et al., 2007](#ref71)). How [[notes/_link/mTOR]] signaling controls [[PDGFR]] expression remains to be determined.

### Energy status

The [[energy status]] of the cell is signaled to [[mTORC1]] through [[notes/_link/AMPK|AMP-activated protein kinase]] ([[notes/_link/AMPK]]), a master sensor of intracellular [[energy status]] (reviewed by [Hardie, 2007](#ref23)). In response to [[energy depletion]] (low [[ATP]]:[[ADP]] ratio), [[notes/_link/AMPK]] is activated and phosphorylates [[TSC2]], which increases the [[GAP]] activity of [[TSC2]] towards [[Rheb]] and reduces [[mTORC1]] activation ([Inoki et al., 2003](#ref28)). Additionally, [[notes/_link/AMPK]] can reduce [[mTORC1]] activity in response to [[energy depletion]] by directly phosphorylating [[Raptor]] ([Gwinn et al., 2008](#ref21)).

### Oxygen levels

[[Oxygen]] levels affect [[mTORC1]] activity through multiple pathways (reviewed by [Wouters and Koritzinsky, 2008](#ref70)). Under conditions of mild [[notes/_link/Hypoxia|hypoxia]], the reduction in [[ATP]] levels activates [[notes/_link/AMPK]], which promotes [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] activation and inhibits [[mTORC1]] signaling as described in the previous section ([Arsham et al., 2003](#ref1); [Liu et al., 2006](#ref41)). [[notes/_link/Hypoxia]] can also activate [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] through transcriptional regulation of [[REDD1|DNA damage response 1]] ([[REDD1]]) ([Brugarolas et al., 2004](#ref4); [Reiling and Hafen, 2004](#ref53)). [[REDD1]] blocks [[mTORC1]] signaling by releasing [[TSC2]] from its growth-factor-induced association with [[14-3-3]] proteins ([DeYoung et al., 2008](#ref11)). This ability of [[REDD1]] to reduce [[mTORC1]] signaling by disrupting the interaction of [[TSC2]] and [[14-3-3]] has probably evolved to limit energy-consuming processes when [[oxygen]], but not [[growth factors]], is scarce. Additionally, [[PML|promyelocytic leukemia]] tumor suppressor and [[BNIP3|BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa protein-interacting protein 3]] ([[BNIP3]]) reduce [[mTORC1]] signaling during [[notes/_link/Hypoxia|hypoxia]] by disrupting the interaction between [[notes/_link/mTOR]] and its positive regulator [[Rheb]] ([Bernardi et al., 2006](#ref3); [Li et al., 2007](#ref40)).

### Amino acids

[[Amino acids]] represent a strong signal that positively regulates [[mTORC1]] (reviewed by [Guertin and Sabatini, 2007](#ref19)). It was recently shown that [[leucine]], an essential [[amino acid]] required for [[mTORC1]] activation, is transported into cells in a [[glutamine]]-dependent fashion ([Nicklin et al., 2009](#ref48)). [[Glutamine]], which is imported into cells through [[SLC1A5]] \[solute carrier family 1 (neutral amino acid transporter) member 5\], is exchanged to import [[leucine]] via a heterodimeric system composed of [[SLC7A5]] \[antiport solute carrier family 7 (cationic amino acid transporter, y+ system, member 5\] and [[SLC3A2]] \[solute carrier family 3 (activators of dibasic and neutral amino acid transport) member 2\]. The mechanism by which intracellular [[amino acids]] then signal to [[mTORC1]] remained obscure for many years. The activation of [[mTORC1]] by [[amino acids]] is known to be independent of [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]], because the [[mTORC1]] pathway remains sensitive to [[amino acid]] deprivation in cells that lack [[TSC1]] or [[TSC2]] ([Nobukuni et al., 2005](#ref49)). Some studies have implicated human [[Vps34|vacuolar protein-sorting-associated protein 34]] ([[Vps34]]) in nutrient sensing ([Nobukuni et al., 2005](#ref49)); however, the precise role of human [[Vps34]] in this process still remains to be established ([Juhasz et al., 2008](#ref32)).

Recently, two independent teams, including ours, have shown that the [[Rag proteins]], a family of four related small [[GTPases]], interact with [[mTORC1]] in an amino acid-sensitive manner and are necessary for the activation of the [[mTORC1]] pathway by [[amino acids]] ([Kim et al., 2008](#ref35); [Sancak et al., 2008](#ref57)). In the presence of [[amino acids]], [[Rag proteins]] bind to [[Raptor]] and promote the relocalization of [[mTORC1]] from discrete locations throughout the cytoplasm to a perinuclear region that contains its activator [[Rheb]] ([Sancak et al., 2008](#ref57)). The physical dissociation of [[mTORC1]] and [[Rheb]] with [[amino acid]] deprivation might explain why activators of [[Rheb]], such as [[growth factors]], cannot stimulate [[mTORC1]] signaling in the absence of [[amino acids]].

### Other cellular conditions and signals

In addition to the key signals described above, other cellular conditions and signals, such as [[genotoxic stress]], [[notes/_link/Inflammation|inflammation]], [[Wnt]] ligand and [[PA]], have all been shown to regulate [[mTORC1]] signaling. [[Genotoxic stress]] reduces [[mTORC1]] activity through many mechanisms. For instance, the activation of [[notes/_link/p53]] in response to [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]] rapidly activates [[notes/_link/AMPK]] through an unknown process, which in turn phosphorylates and thereby activates [[TSC2]] ([Feng et al., 2005](#ref13)). Additionally, [[notes/_link/p53]] negatively controls [[mTORC1]] signaling by increasing the transcription of [[notes/_link/PTEN|phosphatase and tensin homolog]] ([[notes/_link/PTEN]]) and [[TSC2]], two negative regulators of the pathway ([Feng et al., 2005](#ref13); [Stambolic et al., 2001](#ref62)). [[notes/_link/Inflammation|Inflammatory]] mediators also signal to [[mTORC1]] via the [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] complex. Pro-inflammatory [[cytokines]], such as [[TNFα]], activate [[IKKβ|IκB kinase-β]] ([[IKKβ]]), which physically interacts with and inactivates [[TSC1]], leading to [[mTORC1]] activation ([Lee et al., 2007](#ref38)). This positive relationship between [[notes/_link/Inflammation|inflammation]] and [[mTORC1]] activation is thought to be important in [[tumor angiogenesis]] ([Lee et al., 2007](#ref38)) and in the development of [[insulin resistance]] ([Lee et al., 2008](#ref39)). [[Wnt]] signaling also increases [[mTORC1]] activity through the inactivation of [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]]. Stimulation of the [[Wnt]] pathway inhibits [[GSK3|glycogen synthase kinase 3]] ([[GSK3]]), a [[kinase]] that promotes [[TSC1]]/[[TSC2]] activity by directly phosphorylating [[TSC2]] ([Inoki et al., 2006](#ref29)). Finally, [[PA]] has been identified as another activator of [[mTORC1]]. Many groups have shown that exogenous [[PA]] or overexpression of [[PA]]-producing enzymes such as [[PLD1|phospholipase D1]] ([[PLD1]]) and [[PLD2|phospholipase D2]] ([[PLD2]]) significantly increases [[mTORC1]] signaling (reviewed by [Foster, 2007](#ref14)). A recent study suggests that [[PA]] affects [[notes/_link/mTOR]] signaling by facilitating the assembly of [[notes/_link/mTOR]] complexes, or stabilizing the complexes ([Toschi et al., 2009](#ref66)).

## mTORC2 still has many secrets to reveal

In contrast to [[mTORC1]], for which many upstream signals and cellular functions have been defined (see above), relatively little is known about [[mTORC2]] biology. The early lethality caused by the deletion of [[mTORC2]] components in mice, as well as the absence of [[mTORC2]] inhibitors, have complicated the study of this protein complex. Nonetheless, many important discoveries have been made over the last few years. Using various genetic approaches, it has been demonstrated that [[mTORC2]] plays key roles in various biological processes, including [[cell survival]], [[cell metabolism]], [[cell proliferation]] and [[cytoskeletal organization]]. The role of [[mTORC2]] in these processes is discussed in more detail below.

### Cell survival, metabolism and proliferation

[[Cell survival]], [[cell metabolism]] and [[cell proliferation]] are all highly dependent on the activation status of [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]], which positively regulates these processes through the phosphorylation of various effectors (reviewed by [Manning and Cantley, 2007](#ref46)). Full activation of [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] requires its phosphorylation at two sites: Ser308, by [[PDK1|phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1]] ([[PDK1]]), and Ser473, by a [[kinase]] that remained unidentified for many years, but was demonstrated to be [[mTORC2]] by our group in 2005 ([Sarbassov et al., 2005](#ref59)). Other studies have subsequently observed that ablation of various [[mTORC2]] components specifically blocks [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] phosphorylation at Ser473 and the downstream phosphorylation of some, but not all, [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] substrates ([Guertin et al., 2006](#ref20); [Jacinto et al., 2006](#ref31)). Inhibition of [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] following [[mTORC2]] depletion reduces the phosphorylation of, and therefore activates, the [[FOXO1|forkhead box protein O1]] ([[FOXO1]]) and [[FOXO3A]] transcription factors, which control the expression of genes involved in [[stress resistance]], metabolism, [[cell-cycle arrest]] and [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]] (reviewed by [Calnan and Brunet, 2008](#ref5)). By contrast, the phosphorylation state of [[TSC2]] and [[GSK3]] is not affected by [[mTORC2]] inactivation. Recently, [[SGK1|serum- and glucocorticoid-induced protein kinase 1]] ([[SGK1]]), which shares homology with [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]], was also shown to be regulated by [[mTORC2]] ([Garcia-Martinez and Alessi, 2008](#ref17)). In contrast to [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]], which retains a basal activity when [[mTORC2]] is inhibited, [[SGK1]] activity is totally abrogated under these conditions. Because [[SGK1]] and [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] phosphorylate [[FOXO1]] and [[FOXO3a]] on common sites, it is possible that the lack of [[SGK1]] activity in [[mTORC2-deficient]] cells is responsible for the inhibition of phosphorylation of [[FOXO1]] and [[FOXO3a]].

### Cytoskeletal organization

[[mTORC2]] regulates [[cytoskeletal organization]]. Many independent groups have observed that knocking down [[mTORC2]] components affects [[actin polymerization]] and perturbs cell morphology ([Jacinto et al., 2004](#ref30); [Sarbassov et al., 2004](#ref58)). These studies have suggested that [[mTORC2]] controls the [[actin cytoskeleton]] by promoting [[PKCα|protein kinase Cα]] ([[PKCα]]) phosphorylation, phosphorylation of [[paxillin]] and its relocalization to [[focal adhesions]], and the [[GTP]] loading of [[RhoA]] and [[Rac1]]. The molecular mechanism by which [[mTORC2]] regulates these processes has not been determined.

### Signaling to mTORC2: the black box

The signaling pathways that lead to [[mTORC2]] activation are not well characterized. Because [[growth factors]] increase [[mTORC2]] [[kinase]] activity and [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] phosphorylation at Ser473, they are considered to be a plausible signal for regulating this pathway (reviewed by [Guertin and Sabatini, 2007](#ref19)). With [[growth-factor]] stimulation, [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] is phosphorylated at the [[cell membrane]] through the binding of [[PtdIns(3,4,5)P3]] to its [[pleckstrin homology]] ([[PH]]) domain. Under these conditions, [[PDK1]] is also recruited to the membrane through its [[PH]] domain and phosphorylates [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] at Ser308 (reviewed by [Lawlor and Alessi, 2001](#ref37)). Interestingly, the [[mTORC2]] component [[mSIN1]] possesses a [[PH]] domain at its C-terminus, suggesting that [[mSIN1]] can promote the translocation of [[mTORC2]] to the membrane and the phosphorylation of [[notes/_link/Akt|AKT]] at Ser473. Additional work is needed to support this model and to identify other cellular signals that play a role in the regulation of [[mTORC2]].

## Perspectives

Over the last decade, knowledge of the [[notes/_link/mTOR]] signaling pathway has greatly progressed, enabling researchers to better understand the mechanism of diseases such as [[Cancer|cancer]] and [[type 2 diabetes]]. Despite these advances, our understanding of this signaling network is far from complete and many important questions remain to be answered. For example, how is [[mTORC2]] regulated and which biological processes does it control? How are the [[mTORC1]] and [[mTORC2]] signaling pathways integrated with each other? What are the functions of these complexes in adult tissues and organs and what are the implications of their dysfunction or dysregulation in health and disease? Are there additional [[notes/_link/mTOR]] complexes that regulate other biological processes? Finding answers to these important questions will advance our understanding of cellular biology, and will also help the development of therapeutic avenues to treat many human diseases.
