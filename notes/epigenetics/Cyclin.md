# Cyclin

[[Cyclin]]s are a family of regulatory proteins that control the progression of the [[Cell Cycle]]. They function by binding to and activating [[Cyclin-Dependent Kinase]] complexes. The concentration of different cyclins fluctuates throughout the cell cycle, acting as a molecular clock that drives cells through the various phases: G1, S, G2, and M.

## Overview

Cyclins are essential for the orderly progression of the cell cycle. Without the precise temporal and spatial regulation provided by cyclins, cells would be unable to coordinate the complex events required for division, such as DNA replication and chromosome segregation. The interaction between cyclins and their partner CDKs ensures that each phase of the cell cycle is completed before the next one begins, maintaining genomic stability and preventing premature or inappropriate division.

## Mechanism/Function

Cyclins do not have enzymatic activity themselves; instead, they act as regulatory subunits for [[Cyclin-Dependent Kinase]]. When a cyclin binds to its partner CDK, it induces a conformational change that allows the CDK to become active and phosphorylate specific target proteins. This phosphorylation triggers downstream events such as DNA replication, spindle assembly, or chromosome segregation.

Different cyclins are responsible for driving different stages of the cell cycle:
- **Cyclin D**: Regulates the G1 phase and the transition to the S phase.
- **Cyclin E**: Drives the G1/S transition.
- **Cyclin A**: Essential for the S phase and G2 phase.
- **Cyclin B**: Drives the entry into and progression through [[Mitosis]] (M phase).

The levels of these cyclins are controlled by a combination of transcriptional regulation, protein stability, and degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome system.

## Clinical Relevance

The dysregulation of cyclin expression and activity is a hallmark of many [[Cancer]] types. Overexpression of certain cyclins, such as Cyclin D1, can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation and is a common driver of oncogenesis. Conversely, the loss of cyclin-dependent regulation can also contribute to genomic instability. 

Targeting cyclin-CDK interactions or the activity of specific cyclins is a major area of research in cancer pharmacology, with several CDK inhibitors currently in clinical use or under development.
