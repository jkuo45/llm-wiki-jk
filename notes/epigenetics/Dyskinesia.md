# Dyskinesia

Dyskinesia describes a group of movement disorders marked by involuntary, extraneous, and often purposeless movements. These may affect the face, trunk, limbs, or respiratory muscles and arise from disordered basal ganglia output. Dyskinesias are frequently drug-induced or accompany [[Neurodegeneration]], and they represent a significant [[Adverse Effect]] of long-term dopaminergic therapy.

## Overview

Movement is normally tuned by the basal ganglia–thalamocortical loop, which balances direct (facilitatory) and indirect (inhibitory) pathways via dopamine signaling. Dyskinesia emerges when this balance is perturbed, producing excessive, poorly controlled movement. The phenomenology ranges from chorea-like writhing to dystonic posturing and rhythmic stereotypies.

## Mechanism and Types

- **Drug-induced dyskinesia**: Chronic dopamine-replacement therapy (e.g., levodopa in Parkinson's disease) sensitizes striatal pathways, producing peak-dose or diphasic dyskinesia.
- **[[Tardive Dyskinesia]]**: A delayed, often persistent orofacial dyskinesia caused by long-term antipsychotic (dopamine receptor blocker) exposure, reflecting receptor supersensitivity.
- **Chorea and dystonia**: Involuntary flowing movements (chorea) or sustained muscle contractions (dystonia) that may be genetic (e.g., Huntington's) or acquired.
- **Neurodegenerative basis**: Progressive loss of dopaminergic or striatal neurons disrupts movement gating, as in Parkinson's and Huntington's disease.

## Physiological and Pathological Relevance

Dyskinesia signals underlying dysfunction of dopamine-dependent motor circuits and can indicate toxicity (e.g., from psychotropics or antiemetics) or advancing neurodegeneration. It impairs quality of life, speech, and swallowing, and in severe forms raises aspiration and injury risk. Recognizing the subtype guides attribution to medication, disease, or metabolic disturbance.

## Clinical and Research Relevance

Management includes dose adjustment of offending drugs, switching to atypical agents, and targeted therapies such as VMAT2 inhibitors (e.g., deutetrabenazine) for tardive and Huntington's dyskinesia. Deep brain stimulation is used in Parkinson's-associated cases. Research focuses on the molecular signatures of dopamine receptor plasticity and on biomarkers to predict and prevent drug-induced dyskinesia.
