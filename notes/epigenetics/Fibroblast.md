# Fibroblast

A [[Fibroblast]] is a mesenchymal, spindle-shaped cell that synthesizes the [[Extracellular Matrix]] and [[Collagen]], producing the structural framework (stroma) for animal tissues. Fibroblasts are the most abundant connective-tissue cells and are indispensable for tissue architecture, repair, and homeostasis. Quiescent fibroblasts remain metabolically active, continuously maintaining and remodeling the [[ECM]].

## Origin and Heterogeneity

Fibroblasts arise from embryonic mesoderm and, in many organs, from resident mesenchymal progenitor pools. They are highly heterogeneous and tissue-specific; for example, [[HFF1]] (human foreskin fibroblast) is a widely used cell-culture model. Organ-specific fibroblast subsets express distinctive marker repertoires and are imprinted by local microenvironmental cues. During injury, resident fibroblasts activate into myofibroblasts—contractile, α-smooth-muscle-actin-positive cells that close wounds but, when persistently activated, drive [[Fibrosis]].

## Functions

The principal role of fibroblasts is synthesis and turnover of ECM components (collagens, fibronectin, proteoglycans) and the [[Basal Lamina]] support structures. They secrete matrix metalloproteinases and their inhibitors to remodel the matrix, and release cytokines and growth factors (TGF-β, PDGF, FGF) that instruct neighboring cells. Through [[Integrin]]-mediated contacts and [[FAK Signaling]], fibroblasts sense matrix stiffness and adjust deposition accordingly.

## Physiological Role

In development and adult life, fibroblasts provide mechanical support, maintain stem-cell niches that enforce [[Quiescence]], and orchestrate [[Tissue Repair]]. Their contractile activity is essential for wound closure, and their provisional matrix scaffolds re-epithelialization.

## Pathological Relevance

Dysregulated fibroblast activation underlies organ fibrosis (liver, lung, kidney, heart, skin), where excessive ECM replaces parenchyma and elevates tissue stiffness, perpetuating a pro-fibrotic loop. Fibroblasts also influence [[Cancer]] by building the tumor stroma, secreting factors that promote invasion and [[Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition]], and suppressing or evading immunity. Fibroblast-targeted therapies (e.g., TGF-β or LOX inhibition) aim to limit pathological scarring.
