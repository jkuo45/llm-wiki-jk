# CRISPR

CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a genome-editing technology adapted from the adaptive immune system of *Streptococcus pyogenes* and related bacteria. The system uses a CRISPR-associated (Cas) nuclease, most commonly Cas9, guided by a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) to introduce site-specific double-strand breaks (DSBs) in genomic DNA. These breaks are resolved by endogenous repair pathways — non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which produces small insertions or deletions (indels) that disrupt gene function, or homology-directed repair (HDR), which enables precise sequence replacement when a donor template is provided.

## Mechanism & Molecular Variants

The standard CRISPR-Cas9 system has been extensively engineered to expand its functional repertoire. Catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) retains DNA-binding capacity without nuclease activity, forming the basis for programmable transcription modulation. Fusion of dCas9 with the Krüppel-associated box (KRAB) repressor domain creates CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), which silences gene expression at the transcriptional level without altering the DNA sequence. Conversely, fusion with transcriptional activators such as VP64 or the tripartite VPR domain enables CRISPR activation (CRISPRa), driving endogenous gene upregulation.

Base editors, developed by fusing dCas9 or nickase Cas9 (nCas9) with cytidine or adenosine deaminases, enable single-nucleotide conversions (C→T or A→G) without requiring double-strand breaks or donor templates. Prime editing, a further innovation, uses a nCas9-reverse transcriptase fusion paired with a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) to directly write new genetic information into the genome, enabling insertions, deletions, and all 12 base-to-base conversions with minimal byproducts.

## Applications

CRISPR has become the dominant platform for gene knockout and knockin in cell lines and model organisms. In disease modeling, CRISPR-engineered organoids carrying patient-specific mutations recapitulate disease phenotypes for mechanistic study and drug screening. High-throughput CRISPR screens, conducted in pooled or arrayed formats, systematically interrogate gene function across the genome, identifying essential genes, drug targets, and synthetic lethal interactions.

Epigenetic editing — using dCas9 fused to epigenetic modifiers such as DNA methyltransferases (DNMT3A), histone acetyltransferases (p300), or demethylases (LSD1, TET1) — enables targeted, heritable modulation of chromatin states without altering the underlying DNA sequence. This approach has been applied to study enhancer function, imprinting, and non-coding RNA regulation.

## Clinical Relevance

The first CRISPR-based therapy to receive regulatory approval is exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy, exa-cel), a CRISPR-Cas9-edited autologous CD34+ hematopoietic stem cell product for sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia. Casgevy functions by disrupting the *BCL11A* erythroid enhancer, thereby reactivating fetal hemoglobin (HbF) expression. Additional clinical trials are evaluating CRISPR therapies for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, transthyretin amyloidosis, Leber congenital amaurosis, and HIV-1 elimination.

## Ethical Considerations

The application of CRISPR to human germline editing has generated substantial ethical debate following the 2018 announcement of CRISPR-edited twins (HE Jiankui affair). International consensus bodies, including the WHO and the National Academies of Sciences, have called for a moratorium on heritable human genome editing while safety, efficacy, and governance frameworks are established.
