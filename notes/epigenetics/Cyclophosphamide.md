# Cyclophosphamide

Cyclophosphamide is a nitrogen mustard alkylating agent and prodrug that requires hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP450)-mediated bioactivation, primarily by CYP2B6 and CYP3A4, to generate its active metabolites. The initial oxidation produces 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide, which exists in equilibrium with its tautomer aldophosphamide. Aldophosphamide undergoes spontaneous β-elimination to yield phosphoramide mustard — the primary DNA-crosslinking species — and the toxic byproduct acrolein. Phosphoramide mustard forms interstrand and intrastrand DNA cross-links at the N7 position of guanine residues, resulting in replication arrest, double-strand breaks, and apoptotic cell death.

## Clinical Indications

Cyclophosphamide is a component of numerous combination chemotherapy regimens. In non-Hodgkin and Hodgkin lymphoma, it is used in the CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) and BEACOPP regimens, respectively. In breast cancer, it is a core agent in AC (doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide) and TAC (docetaxel, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide) protocols. Other indications include ovarian cancer, sarcoma, neuroblastoma, and multiple myeloma. At lower doses, cyclophosphamide functions as an immunosuppressant in autoimmune diseases such as lupus nephritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's), and myasthenia gravis, as well as in conditioning regimens for bone marrow and stem cell transplantation.

## Side Effects and Management

The dose-limiting toxicity of cyclophosphamide is myelosuppression, particularly leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, which typically nadirs at 8–14 days after administration. Hemorrhagic cystitis is a well-characterized adverse effect caused by acrolein accumulation in the urinary bladder; prophylactic administration of mesna (2-mercaptoethanesulfonate) binds and detoxifies acrolein in the urine, substantially reducing the incidence of this complication. Additional toxicities include alopecia, nausea and vomiting, infertility (both ovarian failure and azoospermia), and a dose-dependent risk of cardiotoxicity, including myopericarditis and arrhythmias at high doses (>1.5 g/m²).

## Research Applications

In addition to its clinical utility, cyclophosphamide is used experimentally to induce cellular senescence in vitro. Treatment of primary fibroblasts and cancer cell lines with genotoxic doses of cyclophosphamide metabolites leads to persistent p53/p21-dependent cell cycle arrest, SA-β-gal positivity, and SASP acquisition. The senescence-inducing property of cyclophosphamide provides a model for studying chemotherapy-induced senescence and its role in tumor recurrence and aging-related pathologies.
