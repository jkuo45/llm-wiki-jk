# DNA Replication

[[DNA Replication]] is the fundamental biological process by which a cell makes an identical copy of its genome before cell division. This process occurs during the [[S Phase]] of the [[Cell Cycle]] and is essential for the faithful inheritance of genetic information from one generation of cells to the next.

## Overview

DNA replication is a highly coordinated and remarkably accurate process that ensures every daughter cell receives a complete and correct set of genetic instructions. It is a semi-conservative process, meaning that each new DNA molecule consists of one original parental strand and one newly synthesized daughter strand. The precision of this process is critical for maintaining genomic integrity and preventing the accumulation of mutations that could lead to diseases like cancer.

## Mechanism/Function

DNA replication is carried out by a complex molecular machine known as the replisome, which includes a variety of specialized proteins:

- **Origin of Replication**: The process begins at specific sequences in the genome where the replication machinery is assembled.
- **Helicase (e.g., MCM complex)**: This enzyme unwinds the DNA double helix at the replication fork, separating the two strands.
- **Primase**: Because DNA polymerases cannot start synthesis *de novo*, primase synthesizes short RNA primers that provide the necessary 3'-OH group for elongation.
- **DNA Polymerases ($\alpha$, $\delta$, $\epsilon$)**: These are the primary enzymes responsible for synthesizing the new DNA strands. In eukaryotes, different polymerases specialize in different roles: [[DNA Polymerase alpha]] is involved in priming, while [[DNA Polymerase delta]] and [[DNA Polymerase epsilon]] are the main replicative polymerases for the lagging and leading strands, respectively.
- **Topoisomerases**: As the DNA is unwound, torsional strain (supercoiling) builds up ahead of the replication fork. Topoisomerases relieve this strain by making transient breaks in the DNA.
- **DNA Ligase**: On the lagging strand, DNA is synthesized discontinuously in small fragments called Okazaki fragments. DNA ligase is required to join these fragments together into a continuous strand.

The replication fork moves along the DNA, with the leading strand being synthesized continuously in the 5' to 3' direction, while the lagging strand is synthesized discontinuously in the opposite direction.

## Clinical Relevance

Errors in DNA replication can lead to mutations, which are a major source of genetic variation and can also drive oncogenesis. Replication stress, caused by factors such as insufficient nucleotide pools, DNA damage, or physical obstacles on the DNA template, can lead to stalled replication forks, DNA breaks, and widespread genomic instability. 

Many anticancer drugs target the replication machinery or exploit the vulnerabilities caused by replication stress. For example, some drugs inhibit topoisomerases or deplete nucleotide pools, effectively stalling DNA replication in rapidly dividing cancer cells and inducing cell death.
