# Contact Inhibition

[[Contact Inhibition]] is the cessation of cell proliferation and locomotion that occurs when cells establish direct contact with neighboring cells. It is a fundamental homeostatic mechanism that constrains tissue growth to appropriate boundaries and is a hallmark that normal cells possess but cancer cells frequently lose. Two related but distinct phenomena are described: **contact inhibition of proliferation** (density-dependent arrest of the [[Cell Cycle]]) and **contact inhibition of locomotion** (the turning away of migrating cells upon collision). Together they maintain tissue architecture and suppress uncontrolled expansion.

## Mechanism

Contact inhibition of proliferation is enforced through cell–cell junctions and downstream signaling. **[[Adherens Junction|Adherens junctions]]** and tight junctions recruit adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin, which cluster and sequester signaling proteins at the membrane. Mechanical tension transmitted through these junctions activates the **[[Hippo Signaling Pathway]]**, leading to phosphorylation and cytoplasmic sequestration of the transcriptional co-activator YAP/TAZ, thereby repressing growth-promoting genes. Concurrently, cadherin engagement elevates the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21^Cip1^ and p27^Kip1^, facilitating exit into [[G0 Phase]] and [[Quiescence]]. Loss of E-cadherin, as seen during [[Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition]], abolishes contact inhibition and contributes to invasion.

Contact inhibition of locomotion depends on the polarization of migrating cells at collision interfaces. Filopodial contacts trigger Rac1 inactivation and RhoA activation at the contact site, causing actomyosin contraction and a change in migration direction (the "cell repolarization" response). The cadherin–actin network at the collision front is essential; blocking cadherin function causes cells to crawl over one another instead of stopping.

## Physiological Function

In developing and adult tissues, contact inhibition ensures that epithelia and other monolayers form a cohesive barrier of defined thickness. It cooperates with the surrounding [[Extracellular Matrix]] and soluble mitogens to set the balance between proliferation and arrest, allowing tissues to reach a stable size. Stem and progenitor compartments exploit contact inhibition to remain quiescent until injury demands re-entry into the cycle.

## Pathological Relevance

The breakdown of contact inhibition is a defining feature of cancer. Transformed cells continue to divide beyond confluence ("focus formation" in culture) and migrate invasively over one another. Mutations that disable [[Hippo Signaling Pathway]] components, downregulate E-cadherin, or constitutively activate YAP/TAZ or growth-factor receptors all relieve contact inhibition. Restoring contact-inhibitory signaling is therefore an attractive therapeutic strategy, including cadherin restoration, Hippo pathway agonism, and YAP/TAZ inhibition.
