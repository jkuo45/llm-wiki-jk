# Sister Chromatids

[[Sister Chromatids]] are the two identical DNA copies of a single chromosome generated by semiconservative [[DNA Replication]] during S phase. They remain physically joined at the [[Centromere]] by the ring-shaped [[Cohesin]] complex from replication until their synchronized separation at anaphase of [[Mitosis]] (or anaphase II of meiosis), ensuring each daughter cell receives a complete genome.

## Formation and Cohesion

After replication, the replication machinery couples the nascent sister duplexes, and cohesin is loaded onto chromatin to embrace both sisters along their length. At the centromere, the protector shugoshin and sororin maintain cohesion despite the removal of arm cohesin at prophase. Proper bipolar attachment of kinetochores to spindle microtubules is monitored by the spindle assembly checkpoint before segregation.

## Separation

At the [[Anaphase Promoting Complex-Cyclosome]] (APC/C)–mediated anaphase onset, separase cleaves cohesin's Scc1/Rad21 subunit. Centromeric cohesion releases, and poleward forces pull the formerly joined sisters to opposite poles. This single-event segregation is the mechanical essence of chromosome inheritance.

## Biological Significance

Accurate sister-chromatid cohesion and segregation prevent [[Aneuploidy]] and chromosomal instability, both hallmarks of [[Cancer]] and developmental disorders. Cohesin also organizes higher-order chromatin loops (via CTCF–cohesin) and participates in DNA repair by sister-chromatid recombination. Defective cohesion causes Cornelia de Lange syndrome and predisposes to tumorigenesis.

## Meiotic Distinction

In meiosis I, homologs (not sisters) segregate because cohesin is protected at centromeres; sisters finally separate in meiosis II. Errors here produce [[Trisomy]] and other aneuploidies, notably [[Down Syndrome]].
