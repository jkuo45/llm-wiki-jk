# Trisomy

[[Trisomy]] is a form of [[Aneuploidy]] in which a particular [[Chromosome]] is present in three copies rather than the usual two. Most trisomies arise from nondisjunction—failure of homologous chromosomes or [[Sister Chromatids]] to separate properly during [[Meiosis]]—and are typically sporadic, with maternal-age-associated risk.

## Origins

Trisomy can originate in meiosis I (homolog nondisjunction), meiosis II (sister-chromatid nondisjunction), or post-zygotically (mitotic nondisjunction), the latter producing mosaicism. The extra chromosome may be free (full trisomy), translocated onto another chromosome (e.g., Robertsonian), or present in only a subset of cells (mosaic trisomy). Errors in recombination (reduced or absent chiasmata) strongly predispose to meiotic nondisjunction.

## Clinical Spectrum

*Full trisomy 21* causes [[Down Syndrome]], the most common viable autosomal trisomy, with characteristic facies, intellectual disability, and cardiac defects. Trisomy 18 (Edwards) and trisomy 13 (Patau) are severe and often lethal in infancy. Trisomy of the sex chromosomes (XXX, XXY, XYY) produces milder phenotypes. Monosomy and most autosomal trisomies are embryonic lethal, underscoring dosage sensitivity.

## Mechanisms of Pathology

Gene-dosage imbalance from the extra chromosome disrupts stoichiometry of transcription, splicing, and proteostasis, with downstream effects on proliferation, [[Senescence]], and development. The "dosage-sensitive" model explains both global and tissue-specific defects; the extra chromosome also perturbs the cell cycle and [[Quiescence]] programs.

## Diagnostic and Research Relevance

Prenatal screening (NIPT, ultrasound, karyotype, microarray) detects trisomy; advanced maternal age is the principal risk factor. Model systems (e.g., trisomy cell lines, Ts65Dn mice) elucidate mechanisms and test therapies. Understanding nondisjunction informs infertility, [[Cancer]] cytogenetics, and aneuploidy-driven aging.
