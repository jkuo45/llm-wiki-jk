# Progenitor Cell

A [[Progenitor Cell]] is a tissue-resident, lineage-committed cell type intermediate between a [[Stem Cell]] and a fully mature effector cell. Unlike pluripotent stem cells, progenitors are typically multipotent or unipotent and have begun to restrict their developmental potential, yet they retain the capacity to proliferate and give rise to the differentiated cell types of their tissue. The transition from [[Pluripotency]] toward a progenitor state is called [[Differentiation]], culminating in [[Terminal Differentiation]] and loss of division capacity.

## Hierarchy & Types

Progenitors sit within cellular hierarchies: a tissue stem cell divides asymmetrically to self-renew and to produce a transiently amplifying progenitor that expands the pool before maturing. Examples include hematopoietic progenitors (common myeloid/lymphoid progenitors), neuronal [[Neural Stem Cell]]-derived progenitors (neuroblasts), muscle satellite cell progeny, and intestinal crypt base-columnar progenitors. Progenitors are often marked by specific surface antigens and transcription-factor signatures distinct from both stem and terminal cells.

## Regulation of State

Progenitor identity is enforced by lineage-specifying transcription factors and by chromatin states — including repressive marks such as [[H3K27me3]] at alternative-fate genes — that lock in commitment while keeping tissue programs poised. Progenitor proliferation is gated by the [[Cell Cycle]] (notably the [[G1 Phase]]) and by signaling from the niche (Wnt, Notch, Hedgehog, FGF). Quiescence versus activation of progenitors is a key determinant of tissue homeostasis and repair.

## Physiological Function

Progenitors constitute the engine of normal tissue maintenance and regeneration, rapidly expanding to replace cells lost to turnover or injury. They balance self-renewal against differentiation to preserve tissue architecture over a lifetime. Their restricted potency limits aberrancy compared with pluripotent cells, lowering the risk of ectopic differentiation.

## Pathology & Research Relevance

Dysregulated progenitors contribute to developmental defects, failed repair, and [[Cancer]], where cancer stem-like cells share features with normal progenitors. Progenitor pools are also targets of oncogenic signaling (e.g., [[MET gene]]/HGF-driven invasive growth). In regenerative medicine, expanding and directing progenitors offers a safer alternative to pluripotent cells by reducing [[Tumorigenicity]]. Progenitors derived from [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] are used to model disease and to screen therapeutics via [[High-Throughput Screening]].
