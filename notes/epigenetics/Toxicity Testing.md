# Toxicity Testing

[[Toxicity Testing]] is the set of methods used to determine the degree to which a substance can damage a living organism, tissue, or cell. It quantifies adverse effects — cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, organ-specific injury, developmental or reproductive toxicity — across dose and time, providing the safety evidence base for pharmaceuticals, chemicals, and consumer products.

## Traditional vs. Human-Relevant Models

Conventional testing relied heavily on animal models and immortalized cancer cell lines, which often poorly predict human responses due to species differences and non-physiological genetics. Modern approaches increasingly use human [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] differentiated into hepatocytes, cardiomyocytes, neurons, and kidney cells, offering patient-relevant, genetically tractable systems. These models improve prediction of drug-induced liver injury, cardiotoxicity (e.g., QT prolongation), and neurotoxicity.

## Methods & Platforms

Toxicity assessment spans:

- **Cytotoxicity assays** (viability, ATP, LDH release) in 2D and 3D formats.
- **High-Throughput Screening]]** — automated microplate platforms enabling rapid dose–response across thousands of compounds and multiple cell types.
- **Genotoxicity and organ-on-chip** systems that model tissue-level physiology.
- **Senolytic and targeted-toxicity screens** — e.g., assessing [[Senolytic Drugs]] for selective elimination of senescent cells with minimal off-target harm.

## Role in Drug Discovery

In [[Drug Discovery]], early toxicity testing de-risks candidate compounds, reduces late-stage attrition, and supports mechanism-of-toxicity studies. iPSC-derived isogenic panels allow genotype-stratified safety profiling, while organoid and microphysiological systems capture tissue architecture absent from monolayers.

## Regulatory & Ethical Shift

The move toward human stem-cell and organ-on-chip models aligns with reduction of animal use and the demand for more translational safety data. Standardization of differentiation, endpoints, and reporting remains an active area to enable regulatory acceptance.
