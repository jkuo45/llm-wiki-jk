# T Cell Receptor

The [[T Cell Receptor]] ([[TCR]]) is a membrane protein complex on [[T Lymphocyte|T cells]] that recognizes antigenic peptides presented by [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] (MHC) molecules, initiating [[Adaptive Immunity]]. It is the cardinal specificity determinant of cellular immunity.

## Structure and Complex

The TCR is a disulfide-linked αβ (or γδ) heterodimer that forms the antigen-binding site. It is non-covalently associated with the CD3 complex (CD3γ, δ, ε, and ζ chains), whose immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) transduce signals. Co-receptors CD4 or CD8 bind MHC class II or I, respectively, stabilizing the interaction. The αβ dimer is generated by V(D)J recombination, creating enormous diversity; thymic selection ensures self-MHC restriction and central tolerance.

## Antigen Recognition and Signaling

The TCR recognizes peptide–MHC complexes via its complementarity-determining regions. Engagement triggers Lck phosphorylation of CD3 ITAMs, recruitment/activation of ZAP-70, and downstream cascades (NF-AT, AP-1, NF-κB) that drive proliferation, cytokine production, and effector differentiation. Recognition of self-peptide–MHC in the thymus eliminates or tolerizes autoreactive clones, underpinning self-[[Immunity]] without autoimmunity.

## Biological and Clinical Relevance

TCR specificity defines helper, cytotoxic, regulatory, and memory T-cell functions and is essential for clearing intracellular pathogens and tumors. TCR-engineered T cells (TCR-T therapy) target intracellular tumor antigens; soluble TCR mimics and TCR sequencing are used in diagnostics and immunomonitoring. Defective TCR signaling causes immunodeficiency, while aberrant selection contributes to autoimmunity and to escaping [[Cancer]] immune surveillance.
