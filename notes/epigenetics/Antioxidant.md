# Antioxidant

An **[[Antioxidant]]** is any substance that inhibits **[[Oxidation]]** or the activity of **[[Oxidative Stress]]** by neutralizing or scavenging highly reactive molecules, most notably **[[Reactive Oxygen Species]]** (ROS). In biological systems, antioxidants are essential for maintaining **[[Redox Balance]]**, protecting cells, tissues, and organs from the damaging effects of oxidative processes.

## Overview

Oxidation is a fundamental chemical process involving the loss of electrons from a molecule. While necessary for many biological functions, excessive oxidation—driven by the overproduction of ROS such as the **[[Hydroxyl radical]]**, superoxide, and hydrogen peroxide—can lead to significant cellular damage. This damage can manifest as lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, collectively contributing to aging and various diseases.

Antioxidants work by donating electrons to these reactive species, thereby neutralizing them without becoming highly reactive themselves. They are broadly categorized into two groups: **endogenous antioxidants**, which are synthesized by the body, and **exogenous antioxidants**, which must be obtained through diet or supplementation.

## Mechanism and Function

The defense against oxidative stress is a multi-layered system involving various biochemical mechanisms:

### 1. Endogenous Antioxidant Systems

The body employs a sophisticated network of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants to manage ROS levels:

- **Enzymatic Antioxidants**:
    - **[[Superoxide Dismutase]] (SOD)**: Catalyzes the dismutation of the superoxide radical into oxygen and hydrogen peroxide, serving as the first line of defense against superoxide.
    - **[[Catalase]]**: An enzyme primarily located in peroxisomes that rapidly converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.
    - **Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx)**: Uses reduced glutathione to reduce hydrogen peroxide and organic hydroperoxides to water and alcohols, respectively.
- **Non-Enzymatic Antioxidants**:
    - **[[Glutathione]] (GSH)**: A tripeptide that serves as a major intracellular antioxidant. It can directly scavenge ROS or act as a substrate for enzymes like GPx. Its redox state (GSH/GSSG ratio) is a critical indicator of cellular oxidative stress.
    - Other molecules like uric acid and certain vitamins also play roles in maintaining the redox environment.

### 2. Exogenous Antioxidants

These are substances acquired from external sources and can significantly augment the body's endogenous defenses:

- **Vitamins**:
    - **Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)**: A highly effective water-soluble antioxidant that can scavenge various ROS and help regenerate other antioxidants like Vitamin E.
    - **Vitamin E (Tocopherol)**: A crucial lipid-soluble antioxidant that protects cell membranes from lipid peroxidation by scavenging radicals within the lipid bilayer.
- **Dietary Polyphenols**: Many plant-derived compounds, such as flavonoids, exhibit potent antioxidant properties and are known to modulate cellular redox signaling.

## Clinical Relevance

The balance between pro-oxidants and antioxidants is central to several pathological states:

- **Aging and Longevity**: The "Free Radical Theory of Aging" posits that the accumulation of oxidative damage over time is a primary driver of the aging process. Maintaining robust antioxidant defenses is a key area of research in longevity science.
- **Neurodegenerative Diseases**: The brain is particularly susceptible to oxidative stress due to its high oxygen consumption and lipid content. Deficiencies in antioxidant capacity are strongly linked to the pathogenesis of diseases like **[[Alzheimer's Disease]]** and Parkinson's disease.
- **Cancer**: The role of antioxidants in cancer is complex. While they can protect healthy cells from DNA damage, they may also protect cancer cells from the oxidative stress-induced apoptosis that the immune system or chemotherapy attempts to trigger.
- **Cardiovascular Disease**: Oxidative stress plays a critical role in the development of atherosclerosis, where the oxidation of LDL cholesterol is a key step in plaque formation.
