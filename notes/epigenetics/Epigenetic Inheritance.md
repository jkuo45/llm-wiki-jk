# Epigenetic Inheritance

**Epigenetic inheritance** refers to the transmission of epigenetic information across cell divisions (mitotic inheritance) or, in some cases, across generations (meiotic/transgenerational inheritance) without any change to the underlying DNA sequence. It is the cellular memory system that allows a daughter cell or an offspring to retain a parental gene-expression state despite sharing an identical genome with all other cells in the organism.

## Overview

Whereas genetic inheritance relies on the fidelity of the DNA template itself, epigenetic inheritance relies on the faithful copying and resetting of chromatin marks — DNA methylation, histone modifications, nucleosome positioning, and regulatory non-coding RNAs. The concept is central to developmental biology (how one genome yields hundreds of cell types) and to the broader question of whether environmental exposures in one generation can shape the phenotype of descendants.

## Mechanisms

- **[[DNA Methylation]]** — The most robustly documented carrier of heritable epigenetic information. Maintenance methylation by [[DNMT1]] copies patterns from the parental to the daughter strand during replication, enabling mitotic stability across hundreds of divisions.
- **[[Histone Modification]]** — Marks such as H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and H3K4me3 are propagated through the cell cycle, partly by "reader-writer" complexes that recognize a mark and recruit the enzyme that deposits the same mark on neighboring nucleosomes.
- **[[Non-Coding RNA]]** — Small RNAs (piRNAs, siRNAs, certain miRNAs) guide silencing complexes to homologous sequences, establishing and maintaining silent chromatin; in some organisms they can mediate transgenerational transmission.
- **[[Genomic Imprinting]]** — A specialized form of epigenetic inheritance in which a subset of genes are expressed from only one parental allele according to parent-of-origin methylation marks established in the germline.

## Mitotic vs. Transgenerational Inheritance

- **Mitotic (somatic) inheritance** is well established: differentiated cells maintain their identity across divisions because chromatin states are copied. This underlies tissue homeostasis and lineage commitment.
- **Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance** is more contested. It requires epigenetic information to survive the extensive reprogramming that occurs during gametogenesis and early embryogenesis (global demethylation at fertilization, re-establishment in the blastocyst). Documented examples exist in plants, *C. elegans*, and *Drosophila*, and a growing body of mammalian evidence (e.g., paternal diet effects transmitted via sperm small RNAs) supports the phenomenon, though the mechanisms and durability remain actively debated.

## Physiological and Pathological Relevance

Epigenetic inheritance stabilizes cellular identity and is essential for normal development. Its failure contributes to disease: loss of imprinting causes disorders such as Beckwith-Wiedemann and Prader-Willi/Angelman syndromes; aberrant inheritance of silencing marks drives [[Cancer]] progression and [[Senescence]]. Age-related "epigenetic drift" — stochastic gains and losses of methylation transmitted through divisions — is increasingly viewed as a primary [[Aging]] hallmark. Understanding these processes informs reprogramming, [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]], and [[Rejuvenation]] strategies.
