# Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition

[[Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition]] (EMT) is a biological process where polarized epithelial cells lose their cell-cell adhesion and apical-basal polarity, gaining migratory and invasive properties characteristic of mesenchymal cells.

## Role in Cellular Reprogramming and Pluripotency

In the field of [[Cellular Reprogramming]], the suppression of [[Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition]] is essential for the generation of [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] (iPSCs) from mesenchymal somatic cells like [[Fibroblasts]]. Somatic cells must instead undergo the reverse process, [[Mesenchymal-to-Epithelial Transition]] (MET), to reach a pluripotent state.

The [[TGF-beta Signaling Pathway]] is a potent inducer of EMT and acts as a major roadblock to reprogramming. By inhibiting TGF-beta signaling or its downstream effectors (such as Snail, Slug, and Zeb1/2), researchers can significantly enhance the efficiency of MET and the transition to [[Pluripotency]]. The [[Yamanaka Factors]] (OSKM) facilitate this suppression; for example, [[Oct4]] and [[Sox2]] repress the expression of Snail.

## Connections

- [[Mesenchymal-to-Epithelial Transition]]: The reverse process of EMT; required for reprogramming mesenchymal cells into iPSCs.
- [[TGF-beta Signaling Pathway]]: A primary signaling pathway that promotes EMT; its inhibition is a common strategy to improve reprogramming efficiency.
- [[Fibroblasts]]: Mesenchymal cells that must have their EMT programs suppressed to be reprogrammed.
- [[Cellular Reprogramming]]: Suppression of EMT is a key event in the early phases of reprogramming toward pluripotency.
- [[Cancer]]: EMT is frequently co-opted by cancer cells to promote metastasis and invasion.
