# Cyclopentenyl cytosine

Cyclopentenyl cytosine (CPEC) is a synthetic cytidine analog in which the ribose sugar is replaced by a cyclopentenyl ring. It functions as a potent inhibitor of CTP synthetase (CTPS), the rate-limiting enzyme in the de novo biosynthesis of cytidine triphosphate (CTP). By competitively binding to the CTP-binding site of CTPS, CPEC depletes intracellular CTP pools, thereby impairing both DNA and RNA synthesis in proliferating cells.

## Mechanism of Action

CPEC is phosphorylated intracellularly to its active triphosphate form (CPEC-TP) by cellular kinases, including uridine-cytidine kinase 2 (UCK2). CPEC-TP then inhibits CTPS with an IC50 in the low micromolar range. The resulting depletion of CTP selectively affects rapidly dividing cells, which have a high demand for pyrimidine nucleotides to support nucleic acid synthesis. Unlike chain-terminating nucleoside analogs, CPEC does not require incorporation into DNA to exert its antiproliferative effects — its primary mechanism is metabolic perturbation of the pyrimidine biosynthetic pathway.

## Cellular Effects and Senescence

Exposure of cultured cancer cells and primary fibroblasts to CPEC results in sustained CTP depletion, leading to replication stress, S-phase arrest, and activation of the DNA damage checkpoint. Notably, CPEC has been shown to induce a stable senescent phenotype in multiple cell types, characterized by enlarged flattened morphology, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, upregulation of p53 and p21^WAF1/CIP1, and secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors. The p53-p21 axis is considered a primary effector of CPEC-induced senescence, as p53-null cells exhibit resistance to both cell cycle arrest and the senescent phenotype following CPEC treatment.

## Preclinical Anticancer Activity

CPEC has demonstrated antiproliferative activity against a range of human cancer cell lines, including breast, lung, colon, and leukemia models, with IC50 values in the nanomolar to low micromolar range. In murine xenograft models, CPEC treatment suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival, although clinical development has been limited by dose-dependent toxicities, including myelosuppression and gastrointestinal epithelial damage.
