# Connective Tissue

[[Connective Tissue]] is one of the four basic types of animal tissue, alongside epithelial, muscle, and nervous tissue. It is distinguished by a relatively sparse population of resident cells dispersed within an abundant [[Extracellular Matrix]] (ECM) of fibrous proteins and hydrated ground substance. This architecture allows connective tissue to provide structural support, mechanical integrity, metabolic storage, and a conduit for nerves and vessels throughout the body.

## Structure & Composition

Connective tissue is conventionally divided into connective tissue proper (loose and dense), specialized connective tissue (cartilage, bone, adipose, blood), and supporting structures. The ECM is synthesized and remodeled primarily by [[Fibroblast|fibroblasts]] and comprises:

- **Fibers** — [[Collagen]] (types I–III dominant in skin, tendon, bone), elastic fibers (elastin, fibrillin), and reticular fibers (type III collagen networks).
- **Ground substance** — a hydrated gel of proteoglycans and hyaluronan that resists compressive forces and regulates diffusional transport.
- **Resident and transient cells** — fibroblasts, adipocytes, macrophages, mast cells, and plasma cells.

The physical properties of a given connective tissue reflect the relative proportions of these components: tendon is collagen-dense and tensile, whereas areolar tissue is loose and permissive of cell trafficking.

## Physiological Function

Connective tissue fulfills several systemic roles: mechanical support and organ encapsulation; transmission of tensile and compressive loads; energy storage (adipose); and host defense (immune cell trafficking through loose connective tissue). It also serves as a scaffold for angiogenesis and wound healing, with fibroblasts depositing provisional matrix during repair that is later remodeled into scar.

## Pathological Relevance

Dysregulation of connective tissue underlies numerous diseases. Excessive deposition produces [[Fibrosis]] in organs such as liver, lung, and kidney, often driven by transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling and persistent fibroblast activation. Heritable defects in collagen (e.g., Ehlers–Danlos, osteogenesis imperfecta) produce connective-tissue fragility. In aging, cross-linking of ECM proteins (advanced glycation end-products) stiffens tissue and alters mechanosignaling, with consequences for stem-cell niche function and regenerative capacity.
