## Summary

To date, dozens of stress‐induced [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] phenotypes have been reported. These cellular senescence states may differ substantially from each other, as well as from [[Replicative Senescence|replicative senescence]] through the presence of specific senescence features. Here, we attempted to catalog virtually all of the cellular senescence‐like states that can be induced by low molecular weight compounds. We summarized biological markers, molecular pathways involved in senescence establishment, and specific traits of cellular senescence states induced by more than fifty small molecule compounds.

**Keywords:** [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]], [[Cell Stress|cell stress]], [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]], [[DNA Replication Stress|DNA replication stress]], [[Epigenetic Modifiers|epigenetic modifiers]], [[notes/_link/Aging|aging]]

---

[[notes/_link/Senescence|Cellular senescence]] is a stable arrest of the [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] and is characterized by complex phenotypic changes. It was first described in studies of human [[Fibroblast|fibroblasts]] that ceased proliferation following an extended cultivation (Hayflick & Moorhead, [^52]; Hayflick, [^51]). Discovered by Hayflick and Moorhead, senescence in normal human cells was shown to depend on telomere dysfunction originating mainly from replication‐associated [[Telomere Shortening|telomere shortening]] (Harley *et al*., [^50]; Allsopp, [^2]; Bodnar *et al*., [^13]). This type of senescence is also known as [[Replicative Senescence]] and is the prototypical cellular senescence state. Other forms of senescence (i.e., not linked to proliferation‐dependent telomere shortening) include a variety of prematurely developed cellular senescence phenotypes, similar but not identical to replicative senescence. Many proliferative cell types can undergo so‐called [[Stress-Induced Premature Senescence]] ([[Stress-Induced Premature Senescence|SIPS]]) upon exposure to subcytotoxic stresses (UV, [[γ‐irradiation]], [[Hydrogen Peroxide|H2O2]], hyperoxia, etc.) (Toussaint *et al*., [^139], [^140]). [[Oncogene-Induced Senescence]] ([[Oncogene-Induced Senescence|OIS]]) represents another complex senescence phenotype that depends on activation and/or overexpression of oncogenes (Serrano *et al*., [^127]; Bianchi‐Smiraglia & Nikiforov, [^12]). The mechanism of OIS involves [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]] that may be a result of DNA hyper‐replication (Di Micco *et al*., [^36]), replication fork reversal (Neelsen *et al*., [^99]), depletion of nucleotide pools (Mannava *et al*., [^81]), and/or increased levels of [[notes/_link/ROS|reactive oxygen species]] ([[notes/_link/ROS|ROS]]) (Lee *et al*., [^71]). Conceptually and mechanistically, OIS is closely related to [[Tumor-Suppressor Loss-Induced Senescence]] (Chen *et al*., [^23]; Di Mitri & Alimonti, [^37]). [[Cell-to-Cell Fusion-Induced Senescence]] can also be considered a premature senescence subtype (Chuprin *et al*., [^27]; Burton & Faragher, [^16]). The distinctive phenotypic changes typical of various types of cellular senescence are cell enlargement and flattening, [[SA-beta-gal|senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase activity]] ([[SA-beta-gal]]), formation of [[SAHF|senescence‐associated heterochromatin foci]] ([[SAHF]]), persistent [[DNA Damage Response|DNA damage response]] ([[DNA Damage Response|DDR]]), and [[SASP|senescence‐associated secretory phenotype]] ([[SASP]]). However, these and several other facultative features of cellular senescence that manifest in each particular case of cell cycle arrest greatly depend on the senescence‐inducing stimulus and the cell type (Campisi, [^19]; Salama *et al*., [^125]).

The contribution of [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] to organismal aging is a question of ongoing research (van Deursen, [^34]). However, strong evidence for this connection has been reported recently. Specifically, it was shown that clearance of age‐accumulated [[p16INK4A|p16 <sup>INK4A</sup>]] ‐positive senescent cells in mice could extend their healthy lifespan (Baker *et al*., [^7], [^8]). Several chemical compounds that specifically target senescent cells have been identified in the last 2 years (so‐called [[Senolytic Drugs|senolytic drugs]]) (Xu *et al*., [2015b](#acel12518-bib-0152); Zhu *et al*., [2015a](#acel12518-bib-0168),[b](#acel12518-bib-0169)). It was shown that clearance of senescent cells by such drugs may alleviate age‐related vasomotor dysfunction and frailty, enhance adipogenesis, rejuvenate haematopoietic stem cells after total‐body irradiation, and, generally, extend lifespan (Xu *et al*., [2015a](#acel12518-bib-0151); Zhu *et al*., [2015b](#acel12518-bib-0169); Roos *et al*., [^124]). Furthermore, these studies confirm the known pathological impact of [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]], exemplified by cellular dysfunction, impairment of tissue regeneration, detrimental effects on tissue microenvironment, etc. (Burton & Krizhanovsky, [^17]). It is evident that along with its detrimental effects, [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] has clearly defined beneficial physiological functions. For instance, it has been shown recently that [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] plays a role in the differentiation of megakaryocytes (Besancenot *et al*., [^11]), the maturation of the placenta (Chuprin *et al*., [^27]), the restriction of [[Fibrosis|fibrosis]] (Krizhanovsky *et al*., [^68]; Jun & Lau, [^65]; Zhu *et al*., [^163]), [[Tissue Repair|tissue repair]] (Demaria *et al*., [^32]), and [[Embryonic Development|embryonic development]] (Nacher *et al*., [^97]; Munoz‐Espin *et al*., [^96]; Storer *et al*., [^132]). The role of [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] in [[Cancer|cancer prevention]] is well documented (Burton & Krizhanovsky, [^17]; Munoz‐Espin & Serrano, [^95]).

It is generally agreed in the field that the most important features of cellular senescence are SASP and resistance to apoptosis (Munoz‐Espin & Serrano, [^95]; Burton & Faragher, [^16]). SASP stimulates immune system‐dependent elimination of unwanted precancerous cells or specific embryonic cells that undergo senescence. Notably, cellular senescence may serve as an alternative to apoptosis in embryonic development as well as in cancer prevention (Childs *et al*., [^25]). It has been shown that failure to undergo senescence triggers apoptosis in a compensatory manner to eliminate transient structures during development (Munoz‐Espin *et al*., [^96]; Storer *et al*., [^132]). Therefore, it may be reasonable to consider some of the cellular senescence states (e.g., SIPS), along with apoptosis, autophagy, necrosis, etc., in terms of the cell stress response rather than aging. However, it is unclear whether or not the cellular senescence that is widely implicated in normal aging, chronic diseases, tumor suppression, tumorigenesis, cell differentiation, and embryogenesis represents a single physiological cellular state.

To date, dozens of stress‐induced cellular senescence phenotypes have been reported. These senescence states may differ substantially from each other, as well as from replicative senescence, through the presence of specific senescence features. Additionally, it has been reported that some stress‐induced senescence states can be overcome, thus challenging the dogma that cellular senescence is an irreversible form of growth arrest (Romanov *et al*., [^123]; Beausejour *et al*., [^9]). Such caveats can lead to confusion regarding the terminology of stress‐induced cellular senescence states; it is not clear whether senescence‐like growth arrest (and variations thereof) resembles ‘true’ cellular senescence. The indispensable characteristics of this ‘true’ cellular senescence are also elusive. It can be argued that SASP (arising along with morphological changes and SA‐β‐gal) may be the most important physiologically relevant feature of cellular senescence; however, SASP has not been studied in most cases of stress‐induced senescence. Here, we attempt to catalog virtually all of the cellular senescence‐like states that can be induced by low molecular weight compounds (Table [1](#acel12518-tbl-0001)). We summarize the biological markers, molecular pathways involved in senescence establishment, and specific traits of cellular senescence states induced by small compounds, as well as the treatment conditions used. In total, we analyzed more than 50 chemical inducers of [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] and senescence‐like states. These chemical compounds can be functionally classified into eight groups: (1) [[DNA Replication Stress|DNA replication stress]] inducers ([[Aphidicolin|aphidicolin]], [[Hydroxyurea|hydroxyurea]], [[Thymidine|thymidine]], [[Bromodeoxyuridine|bromodeoxyuridine]], [[Gemcitabine|difluorodeoxycytidine]], [[Cyclopentenyl cytosine|cyclopentenyl cytosine]]); (2) DNA‐damaging agents, including (2a) [[DNA Topoisomerase Inhibitors|DNA topoisomerase inhibitors]] ([[Doxorubicin|doxorubicin]], [[Etoposide|etoposide]], [[Daunorubicin|daunorubicin]], [[Mitoxantrone|mitoxantrone]], [[Camptothecin|camptothecin]]), (2b) [[DNA Cross-linkers|DNA cross‐linkers]] ([[Cisplatin|cisplatin]], [[Mitomycin C|mitomycin C]], [[Busulfan|busulfan]], [[Cyclophosphamide|cyclophosphamide]], [[Diaziquone|diaziquone]]), and (2c) drugs with complex effects ([[Actinomycin D|actinomycin D]], [[Bleomycin|bleomycin]], [[Temozolomide|temozolomide]]); (3) [[Epigenetic Modifiers|epigenetic modifiers]] that inhibit [[DNA Methyltransferase|DNA methyltransferases]] ([[Decitabine|5‐aza‐2′‐deoxycytidine]]), [[Histone Deacetylase|histone deacetylases]] ([[Sodium Butyrate|sodium butyrate]], [[Trichostatin A|trichostatin A]], [[Entinostat|MS‐275]], [[Vorinostat|SAHA]], [[Panobinostat|LBH589]], [[Phenylbutyrate|phenylbutyric acid]], [[Sodium Valproate|valproic acid]]), [[Histone Acetyltransferase|histone acetyltransferases]] ([[Curcumin|curcumin]], [[C646]]), and [[Histone Methyltransferase|histone methyltransferases]] ([[BRD4770]]); (4) [[Inhibitors of Telomerase Activity|inhibitors of telomerase activity]] (SYUIQ‐5, [[BMVC4]], [[Pyridostatin|pyridostatin]], compound 115405, perylene and indole derivatives, [[Harmine|harmine]], [[BIBR1532]], [[Azidothymidine|azidothymidine]]); (5) [[CDK Inhibitor|inhibitors of cyclin‐dependent kinases]] ([[Palbociclib|palbociclib]], [[Roscovitine|roscovitine]], [[Ribociclib|ribociclib]]); (6) activators of [[notes/_link/p53|p53]] ([[Nutlin-3a|nutlin 3a]], [[FL118]]); (7) activators of [[Protein Kinase C]] (TPA/PMA, PEP005, PEP008); and (8) [[notes/_link/ROS|reactive oxygen species]] ([[notes/_link/ROS|ROS]]) inducers ([[Hydrogen Peroxide|hydrogen peroxide]], [[tert-Butyl hydroperoxide|tert‐butyl hydroperoxide]], phenyl‐2‐pyridyl ketoxime, phenylaminonaphthoquinones, [[Paraquat|paraquat]]).

The table highlights the fact that cancer cells can undergo [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] *in vitro* just as well as their normal nontransformed counterparts. It is apparent that there is no senescence marker or pathway unique to normal or cancer cells. In most cases, increased [[SA-beta-gal]], morphological changes, and persistent [[DNA Damage Response|DDR]] foci were recorded. [[SAHF]] were found in only a few cases ([[Aphidicolin|aphidicolin]], [[Etoposide|etoposide]], [[Palbociclib|palbociclib]], and [[Epigenetic Modifiers|epigenetic modifiers]]). [[SASP]] was also noted only in some cases; however, this is likely because [[SASP]] is not commonly analyzed as a senescence biomarker. Apparently, an implicit consensus was established that the demonstration of [[SA-beta-gal]], morphological changes, and persistent [[DNA Damage Response|DDR]] is sufficient to document a [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]]‐like state. It is notable that authors designated these phenotypes as a state of [[Stress-Induced Premature Senescence|premature senescence]] or senescence‐like [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] arrest, regardless of the set of biomarkers observed in each case.

Extremely prolonged drug exposure (from hours to days) was typically required to induce [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]], as is evidenced by the table. In marginal situations, as in the case of [[Aphidicolin|aphidicolin]]‐induced [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] arrest, the full set of senescence biomarkers ([[SA-beta-gal]], cell enlargement, [[SAHF]], and [[DNA Damage Response|DDR]] foci) was maintained, while the drug was present in the culture medium and lost upon drug removal (Maya‐Mendoza *et al*., [^87]). The requirement for prolonged incubation time was found for all groups of chemical compounds analyzed; however, the mechanism of senescence development appeared to differ among these groups. Whereas [[DNA Replication Stress|replication stress]] inducers, different DNA‐damaging agents, and telomerase inhibitors likely generate a persistent [[DNA Damage Response|DDR]] following prolonged introduction of a small number of [[DNA Lesions|DNA lesions]] or [[Telomere Uncapping|telomere uncapping]], long‐term incubation with [[Epigenetic Modifiers|epigenetic modifiers]] likely causes [[Gene Expression|transcriptional activation]] of [[Chromatin|repressed loci]] (particularly [[p16INK4A|INK4A]], which encodes [[p16INK4A|p16]] [[CDK Inhibitor|CDK inhibitor]]). This hypothesis is supported by the fact that, in contrast to [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]]‐induced [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]], which depends on [[CDKN1A|p21]] [[CDK Inhibitor|CDK inhibitor]], epigenetically induced senescence is mostly dependent on [[p16INK4A|p16]]. This characterizes epigenetically induced senescence as ‘causeless’—[[Epigenetic Modifiers|epigenetic modifiers]] directly activate molecular pathways maintaining the [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] state without generating any [[Cell Stress|cell stress]]. In this regard, senescence induced by [[Epigenetic Modifiers|epigenetic modifiers]] can resemble developmentally programmed or organismal [[notes/_link/Aging|aging]]‐associated [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]], while [[DNA Replication Stress|replication stress]]‐ and [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]]‐induced senescence are examples of [[Stress-Induced Premature Senescence]] states.

It follows from the table that [[DNA Replication Stress|replication stress]]‐ and [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]]‐induced [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] mostly depend on the [[notes/_link/p53|p53]]‐[[CDKN1A|p21]] pathway. The same is basically true for [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] induced by [[Cell Stress|physical stressors]] such as [[Ionizing Radiation]] ([[IR]]) and [[UV Radiation|ultraviolet]] ([[UV Radiation|UV]]) (Latonen *et al*., [^70]; Suzuki *et al*., [^135]). It is well known that [[IR]] as well as [[UV Radiation|UV]] can stimulate senescence in a variety of normal and cancer cell lines (Chainiaux *et al*., [^20]; Meng *et al*., [^89]; Jones *et al*., [^64]; Jee *et al*., [^61]). Mechanistically, this type of [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]] mostly depends on [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]] induced by these stressors; this links [[IR]] and [[UV Radiation|UV]] to chemical DNA‐damaging agents. Moreover, [[IR]] and [[UV Radiation|UV]], along with most of the DNA‐damaging agents presented in the table, induce [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]] rather than senescence when used at higher doses. These observations further emphasize the relationship between [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]] and senescence. Accordingly, these [[Cell Stress|cell stress]] response pathways may operate either as alternatives or as supplement to each other. While prominent (but short term) [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]] induces [[notes/_link/Apoptosis|apoptosis]], prolonged mild [[notes/_link/DNA Damage|DNA damage]] activates [[notes/_link/Senescence|cellular senescence]]. The [[notes/_link/p53|p53]] [[Transcription Factor|transcription factor]] emerges as a [[Scientific Concept|master regulator]] controlling these cell fate decisions (Purvis *et al*., [^117]).
