# Bromodeoxyuridine

**Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU)** is a synthetic nucleoside analog of [[Thymidine]] in which the 5-methyl group is replaced by bromine. It is incorporated into newly synthesized [[DNA]] during [[S phase|S-phase]] of the [[Cell cycle]] in place of thymidine. BrdU-labeled DNA is detected using specific anti-BrdU monoclonal antibodies via immunohistochemistry (IHC), immunofluorescence (IF), or flow cytometry, making it one of the most widely used tools for studying cell proliferation.

## Mechanism of Action

BrdU is phosphorylated by thymidine kinase to BrdU-monophosphate, then converted to BrdU-triphosphate and incorporated by [[DNA polymerase]] during replication. Because the bromine atom is similar in size to the methyl group of thymine, BrdU pairs with adenine without significant helix distortion, though at high concentrations it can induce base-pairing errors and DNA damage. BrdU-substituted DNA is also more sensitive to UV-induced strand breaks, a property exploited in BrdU comet assays for [[DNA Repair]] analysis.

## Research Applications

- **Cell proliferation assay**: BrdU incorporation followed by immunodetection (flow cytometry, ELISA, IF) is the gold standard for quantifying DNA synthesis and mitotic index. It is used extensively in cancer research to measure tumor growth and drug response.
- **Cell cycle analysis**: Combined with DNA dyes (e.g., propidium iodide), BrdU labeling reveals S-phase fraction, cell cycle phase distribution, and cell cycle kinetics.
- **Neurogenesis and birth dating**: Systemic BrdU administration labels dividing neural progenitors; post-hoc detection identifies newborn neurons (e.g., in the hippocampal dentate gyrus). This technique has been pivotal in demonstrating adult [[Neurogenesis]] in mammals.
- **DNA repair**: BrdU photolysis combined with alkaline comet assay (comet-FISH) detects repair of UV-induced damage and base excision repair activity.

## Limitations and Alternatives

BrdU has notable limitations: high doses cause toxicity, immunogenicity, and potential mutagenesis. The halogenated pyrimidine can trigger DNA damage responses and alter cell cycle progression at elevated concentrations. The detection protocol requires DNA denaturation (acid or heat), which can compromise cell morphology and epitope integrity. **[[EdU]] (5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine)** has emerged as a preferred alternative: EdU detection uses copper-catalyzed click chemistry (CuAAC) with fluorescent azides, requires no denaturation, and allows superior preservation of cellular architecture and multiplexing.
