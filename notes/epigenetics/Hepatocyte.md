# Hepatocyte

A [[Hepatocyte]] is the chief parenchymal cell of the liver, constituting 70–85% of its mass. These large, polygonal, binucleate cells are organized into hepatic plates radiating from central veins and are separated by sinusoidal blood spaces. Hepatocytes are metabolically versatile, integrating signals from portal blood and performing the bulk of the liver's synthetic, detoxifying, and storage functions.

## Structure & Polarity

Hepatocytes display pronounced functional polarity with three distinct membrane domains: a sinusoidal (basolateral) face for blood exchange, a canalicular (apical) face forming bile ducts, and a lateral face for intercellular adhesion. Organelles are zonally distributed — peroxisomes and smooth endoplasmic reticulum (rich in [[Cytochrome P450]]) concentrate near the sinusoidal face to support detoxification.

## Metabolic & Detoxification Function

Hepatocytes execute central [[Metabolism]]: gluconeogenesis, glycogen storage, fatty-acid β-oxidation, lipoprotein assembly, and urea synthesis. They metabolize xenobiotics via phase I (oxidation by CYP enzymes) and phase II (conjugation, sulfation, glucuronidation) reactions, exporting products into bile or blood. They synthesize albumin, clotting factors (fibrinogen, prothrombin), and transport proteins.

## Regenerative Capacity

Unlike most somatic cells, hepatocytes are normally quiescent but retain the ability to re-enter the [[Cell Cycle]] after injury. Following partial hepatectomy up to two-thirds of the liver can regenerate within days through synchronized entry of mature hepatocytes into proliferation, guided by growth factors (HGF, EGF) and cytokine priming (TNF-α, IL-6). This capacity links to [[Liver Regeneration]] and makes hepatocytes a key target for [[Disease Modeling]] and transplantation.

## Developmental & Research Relevance

Hepatocytes are [[Somatic Cells]] derived from endoderm during [[Embryonic Development]] and can be generated from [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] for disease modeling and drug screening. Their specification depends on transcription factors (HNF4α, Foxa) and epigenetic remodeling. Hepatocyte dysfunction underlies steatosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, making them central to toxicity and regenerative-medicine research.
