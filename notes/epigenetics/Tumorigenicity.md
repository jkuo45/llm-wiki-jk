# Tumorigenicity

[[Tumorigenicity]] is the capacity of a cell, population of cells, or agent to form a tumor when introduced into a suitable host (typically an immunodeficient mouse). It is a quantitative attribute reflecting the combined effects of uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of senescence/apoptosis, invasion, and failure of differentiation control. In stem-cell research, tumorigenicity is the principal safety risk: residual undifferentiated [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] or embryonic stem cells can generate [[Teratoma]]s or, more rarely, malignant tumors, so rigorous purification and release testing are required before clinical use.

## Determinants

Tumorigenic potential arises from the acquisition of cancer hallmarks. Driver events include activating [[Oncogene]] mutations (e.g., RAS, MYC) and loss of [[Tumor Suppressor Gene]] function (e.g., [[TP53]], [[RB1]]), which together overcome [[Tumor-Suppressor Loss-Induced Senescence]] and [[Oncogene-Induced Senescence]]. Additional contributors are telomerase reactivation, angiogenic signaling, evasion of immune surveillance, and epigenetic destabilization (e.g., aberrant [[H3K27me3]] domains). The degree of dedifferentiation correlates with tumorigenicity: more primitive, less committed cells generally form tumors more readily.

## Assays & Measurement

The classic in vivo assay inoculates test cells subcutaneously or under the kidney capsule of immunocompromised mice and monitors for tumor formation over weeks; teratoma formation in such assays is the standard proof of pluripotency for human stem cells. In vitro surrogates include soft-agar colony formation, sphere formation, and proliferation under anchorage-independent conditions. Quantifying tumorigenicity guides risk assessment of cell therapies and the malignant potential of postulated [[Cancer]] models.

## Physiological vs Pathological Context

Physiologically, the capacity to proliferate and form organized tissue is beneficial (regeneration, wound healing); tumorigenicity represents its pathological uncoupling from normal controls. Cellular [[Senescence]] and intact tumor-suppressor checkpoints normally suppress tumorigenic outgrowth, explaining why lesions that disable these barriers are tumorigenic.

## Research & Clinical Relevance

Minimizing tumorigenicity is central to regenerative medicine: strategies include differentiating stem cells to remove undifferentiated contaminants, suicide-gene "safety switches," and using committed [[Progenitor Cell]] populations with lower tumor-forming capacity. In oncology, tumorigenicity assays validate candidate drivers and test [[Senolytic Drugs]] or differentiation therapies. Understanding tumorigenicity also informs the design of [[HLA Haplobank]] master lines and GMP release criteria.
