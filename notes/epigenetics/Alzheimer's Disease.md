# Alzheimer's Disease

[[Epigenetics]] plays a critical role in the development and progression of **Alzheimer’s Disease (AD)**, acting as a bridge between genetic predisposition and environmental risk factors. Unlike genetic mutations, epigenetic changes do not alter the DNA sequence itself but instead modify how genes are expressed.

### 1. Primary Epigenetic Mechanisms in AD
Research has identified three main types of epigenetic modifications that are dysregulated in the brains of Alzheimer's patients:

*   **[[DNA Methylation]]:** This involves the addition of a methyl group to DNA, which usually "silences" or reduces the expression of a gene.
    *   **Global Hypomethylation:** A general decrease in methylation across the genome has been observed in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex of AD patients, potentially leading to the unwanted activation of genes.
    *   **Gene-Specific Hypermethylation:** Conversely, certain genes related to memory and synaptic plasticity often show increased methylation, which may contribute to cognitive decline.
*   **[[Histone Modification]]:** Histones are proteins that package DNA into structural units called [[Nucleosome]]s. Chemical changes to these proteins can "open" or "close" the [[Chromatin]] structure.
    *   **Deacetylation:** In AD, a general state of histone deacetylation is often linked to a "closed" chromatin state, which prevents the expression of genes necessary for learning and memory.
*   **Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs):** These are RNA molecules that do not code for proteins but regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level.
    *   **[[MicroRNA]] (miRNAs):** Dysregulation of specific miRNAs can lead to the overproduction of amyloid-beta (Aβ) or the hyperphosphorylation of tau proteins.

### 2. The Role of [[Aging]] and Environment
Aging is the most significant non-genetic risk factor for AD, and it is characterized by a progressive "drift" in the [[Epigenome]].
*   **[[Epigenetic Clock]]:** AD patients often show "epigenetic age acceleration," where their brain tissue appears biologically older than their chronological age.
*   **Environmental Triggers:** Factors such as diet, exercise, stress, and exposure to toxins can trigger epigenetic changes that either increase or decrease AD risk.

### 3. Pathological Consequences
Epigenetic dysregulation contributes to the core hallmarks of Alzheimer's:
*   **Amyloid & Tau Pathology:** Epigenetic changes can upregulate genes involved in the production of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles.
*   **[[Neuroinflammation]]:** Aberrant methylation of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes can keep the brain's immune cells (microglia) in a chronic state of inflammation.
*   **Synaptic Loss:** The silencing of genes responsible for synaptic plasticity directly impairs the ability of neurons to communicate, leading to memory loss.

### 4. Therapeutic Potential
Because epigenetic modifications are chemically reversible, they represent a promising target for new treatments:
*   **[[HDAC]] Inhibitors:** Drugs that inhibit histone deacetylases (HDACs) are being studied for their ability to "re-open" chromatin and restore the expression of memory-related genes.
*   **[[DNA Methyltransferase]] (DNMT) Modulators:** Tools that target DNMTs aim to correct abnormal methylation patterns.
*   **[[Biomarkers]]:** Epigenetic signatures in the blood or cerebrospinal fluid are being investigated as potential early-detection biomarkers for AD.
