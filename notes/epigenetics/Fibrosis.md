# Fibrosis

Fibrosis is a pathological biological process defined by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components — principally [[Collagen]], fibronectin, and proteoglycans — within tissues, resulting in scarring and progressive architectural distortion. It is the common终点 of chronic injury across virtually every organ system, including the liver (cirrhosis), lung (pulmonary fibrosis), kidney (nephrosis), heart (myocardial fibrosis), and skin (keloids/scleroderma). Rather than being a single disease, fibrosis represents a conserved wound-healing response that has failed to resolve, shifting from physiological repair toward maladaptive tissue remodeling.

## Mechanism and Cellular Basis

The central effector cell is the activated [[Fibroblast]], and its transdifferentiated derivative, the myofibroblast, which expresses α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and is the principal source of [[Extracellular Matrix]]. Myofibroblasts arise not only from resident fibroblasts but also through [[Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition]] (EMT) of epithelial cells, endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), and recruitment of fibrotic progenitor cells. The master profibrotic cytokine is [[TGF-beta Signaling Pathway|TGF-β]], which drives collagen synthesis, inhibits ECM degradation by suppressing matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and stimulates tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). Additional mediators include PDGF, CTGF, IL-13, and angiotensin II.

## Physiological vs. Pathological Function

In acute wound healing, transient fibroblast activation and matrix deposition restore barrier integrity — a beneficial [[Biological Process]]. Fibrosis becomes pathological when the inciting stimulus (persistent [[Inflammation]], toxins, mechanical stress, or metabolic injury) is not removed and the myofibroblast niche is not dismantled via apoptosis. Chronic [[Inflammation]] sustains a feed-forward loop: immune cells secrete TGF-β and ROS, perpetuating fibroblast activation. Epigenetic reprogramming — DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNAs — locks fibroblasts into a persistent activated state, explaining why established fibrosis is often irreversible.

## Clinical and Research Relevance

Fibrosis contributes to an estimated 45% of all deaths in the developed world, yet antifibrotic therapies remain limited. Approved agents include pirfenidone and nintedanib for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which slow decline without reversing scarring. Research frontiers target myofibroblast senescence, the TGF-β pathway, and epigenetic "reversal" of the activated fibroblast state. The interplay between [[Inflammation]] and fibrogenesis makes dual anti-inflammatory/antifibrotic strategies an active area, as is the role of mechanical tension and the matrisome in sustaining the fibrotic niche.
