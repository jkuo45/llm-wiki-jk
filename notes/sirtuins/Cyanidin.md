# Cyanidin

**Cyanidin** is a natural **anthocyanidin** (a class of flavonoid pigments) widely found in red, blue, and purple berries and fruits. Its glycoside form, **cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G)**, is one of the most prevalent and bioavailable anthocyanins in the human diet. Cyanidin has emerged as a potent natural activator of **[[SIRT6]]**, with reported activity increases of over **50-fold** in certain in vitro assays.

## Mechanism of Action — SIRT6 Activation

[[SIRT6]] features a unique elongated **hydrophobic pocket** designed to accommodate long-chain fatty acyl groups (myristoyl, palmitoyl). Cyanidin interacts with SIRT6 to stabilize its catalytically active conformation:

- **Allosteric activation**: Cyanidin (and related anthocyanidins) bind to the SIRT6 allosteric pocket, stabilizing the NAD⁺ co-substrate and the acylated/acetylated histone substrate.
- **Enhanced deacetylase/deacylase activity**: Leads to increased deacetylation of H3K9ac and H3K56ac, compacting chromatin and facilitating DNA repair.
- **Potency**: Reported 50-fold+ activation in vitro — among the highest for natural SIRT6 activators.

## Physiological Impacts

- **Genomic stability**: SIRT6 activation deacetylates H3K9 and H3K56, improving DNA double-strand break repair recruitment.
- **Tumor suppression**: SIRT6 suppresses glycolysis (Warburg effect) and promotes autophagy in tumor cells — cyanidin-mediated SIRT6 activation may contribute to anti-cancer effects.
- **Anti-inflammatory**: Broadly anti-inflammatory via NF-κB suppression and antioxidant activity.
- **Cardiometabolic**: Anthocyanins improve endothelial function, reduce LDL oxidation, lower blood pressure, and improve insulin sensitivity.

## Dietary Sources and Dose Estimates

Cyanidin/C3G is one of the most realistically achievable SIRT6 activators through diet:

| Source | C3G Content | Quantity for ~300 mg C3G |
|---|---|---|
| Black elderberries (*Sambucus nigra*) | ~300–450 mg/100g | **~70–100 g** (~½ cup raw) |
| Blackberries | ~80–95 mg/100g | ~315–375 g (~2–2.5 cups) |
| Black currants | ~150–300 mg/100g total anthocyanins | ~100–200 g |
| Blueberries | ~25–50 mg/100g | Large quantities required |

**Note**: Raw elderberries require cooking/processing (toxic saponins in raw form); elderberry syrup or extract is safer.
