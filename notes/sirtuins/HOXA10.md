# HOXA10

**HOXA10** (Homeobox A10) is a homeodomain-containing transcription factor belonging to the **HOX gene family**, which encodes master regulators of **cell-type determination and positional identity during embryogenesis**. HOXA10 is critical for hematopoiesis, fertility, and reproductive tract development.

## Structure & Domains

Like all HOX proteins, HOXA10 contains a highly conserved 60-amino-acid homeodomain that binds DNA in a sequence-specific manner, typically recognizing TAAT-core motifs. Its activity is fine-tuned by cofactors of the TALE (three-amino-acid loop extension) family such as PBX and MEIS, which increase DNA-binding specificity and affinity. Post-translational modifications, including acetylation, modulate its transcriptional output.

## Physiological Roles

HOXA10 is essential for:
- **Hematopoiesis** — regulates self-renewal and differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells, with HOXA10 overexpression linked to leukemic transformation.
- **Fertility** — required for uterine receptivity and implantation during the menstrual cycle; its expression peaks in the window of implantation.
- **Embryogenesis** — establishes body patterning along the anterior-posterior axis, particularly in limb and genitourinary development.

## SIRT2 Interaction

[[SIRT2]] interacts with HOXA10, linking sirtuin-mediated deacetylation to homeobox transcription factor regulation. SIRT2 can deacetylate HOXA10, potentially altering its DNA-binding affinity, cofactor recruitment, or protein stability. This interaction may modulate HOXA10's transcriptional activity during development and in adult tissue homeostasis, though the specific functional consequences of SIRT2-mediated regulation of HOXA10 remain an active area of investigation. Given SIRT2's role in differentiation and cell-cycle control, the SIRT2–HOXA10 axis could integrate metabolic and positional cues in stem and progenitor cells.

## Clinical & Research Relevance

*HOXA10* dysregulation is implicated in endometrial receptivity disorders, congenital uterine anomalies, and hematologic malignancies. Understanding how sirtuins such as SIRT2 post-translationally gate HOX activity may illuminate mechanisms of [[Aging]]-related stem cell exhaustion and tissue regeneration.
