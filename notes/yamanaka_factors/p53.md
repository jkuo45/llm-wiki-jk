# p53

[[p53]] (encoded by the TP53 gene) is a crucial tumor suppressor protein often referred to as the "guardian of the genome." It coordinates the cellular response to DNA damage by inducing cell cycle arrest, [[Apoptosis]], or [[Senescence]].

## Role in Cellular Reprogramming and Pluripotency

In [[Cellular Reprogramming]], [[p53]] acts as a major roadblock to the generation of [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] (iPSCs). The process of viral transduction commonly used to deliver the [[Yamanaka Factors]] (OSKM) triggers an innate immune response, which generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and causes oxidative DNA damage. This damage activates [[p53]], which in turn initiates [[Apoptosis]] or [[Senescence]] in the transduced cells, significantly reducing the efficiency of iPSC induction.

While inhibiting [[p53]] can dramatically increase the efficiency of reprogramming, it also poses significant risks. iPSCs generated in the absence of [[p53]] are more susceptible to chromosomal aberrations and genomic instability, which increases the risk of tumorigenesis in therapeutic applications. Therefore, finding ways to transiently modulate the innate immune response to avoid [[p53]] activation without permanently disabling it is a key area of research in optimizing iPSC technology.

## Connections

- [[Apoptosis]]: [[p53]] is a primary inducer of programmed cell death in response to cellular stress during reprogramming.
- [[Senescence]]: One of the major outcomes of [[p53]] activation that prevents somatic cells from reaching a pluripotent state.
- [[Cancer]]: As a tumor suppressor, the loss or mutation of [[p53]] is a hallmark of many cancers, highlighting the safety risks of disabling it during iPSC generation.
- [[Cellular Reprogramming]]: [[p53]] is the most significant biological barrier to the efficient induction of pluripotency.
- [[OSKM]]: The delivery of the four Yamanaka factors via viral vectors often results in the activation of [[p53]].
