# Neurodegeneration

## Definition
Neurodegeneration is the progressive loss of structure or function of neurons, including the death of neurons. 

## Role of Oxidative Stress
The brain is particularly vulnerable to [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]] due to its high oxygen consumption, high lipid content (prone to [[Lipid Peroxidation]]), and relatively low antioxidant defenses. Oxidative damage to [[Proteins]] (leading to aggregation) and [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]] (leading to energy failure) are central drivers of neurodegenerative processes.

## Related Conditions
- [[notes/_link/Alzheimer's Disease]]: Characterized by [[Amyloid-beta]] plaques and [[Tau protein]] tangles, both linked to oxidative damage.
- [[notes/_link/Parkinson's Disease]]: Involves the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the [[notes/_link/Substantia Nigra]], often driven by mitochondrial dysfunction.
- [[notes/_link/Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis]] (ALS): Linked to mutations in [[notes/_link/Superoxide Dismutase]] (SOD1) and oxidative damage to motor neurons.

## Connections
- [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]]: A primary cause and consequence of neuronal damage.
- [[Lipid Peroxidation]]: Brain tissue is rich in polyunsaturated fats, making it a major target.
- [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]]: Dysfunction in these organelles is a common hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases.

## Oxidative Stress in Alzheimer's Disease: Metal- and Aβ-Catalyzed ROS

In [[notes/_link/Alzheimer's Disease]], oxidative stress precedes and drives amyloid plaque formation. [[Amyloid-beta]] (Aβ) peptides (particularly the Aβ1–42 isoform) bind redox-active metals (Cu^2+ and Fe^3+) through histidine residues at positions 6, 13, and 14. The Aβ–metal complex reduces Cu^2+ to Cu^+ and Fe^3+ to Fe^2+, which then reduce molecular oxygen to [[Superoxide Radicals|superoxide]] and [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide]] via [[Fenton reaction|Fenton chemistry]]. This process, termed "Aβ-associated redox cycling," generates stoichiometric amounts of H2O2 in the presence of reducing agents such as [[Ascorbic Acid|vitamin C]] and [[notes/_link/Dopamine|dopamine]]. Elevated H2O2 then oxidizes nearby [[Lipids]] (producing [[Malondialdehyde]] and [[4-Hydroxynonenal|4-HNE]]), [[Proteins]] (carbonyl formation), and [[Nucleic Acids]] (8-oxo-dG). [[Tau protein]] hyperphosphorylation is both a consequence and amplifier of this oxidative environment: [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide|H2O2]] inhibits [[notes/_link/PP2A]] (the main tau phosphatase) through oxidation of its catalytic cysteine, promoting tau aggregation into neurofibrillary tangles. Oxidized tau seeds propagate trans-synaptically in a prion-like manner, spreading oxidative pathology across neural networks.

## Parkinson's Disease: Dopamine Oxidation and Complex I Deficiency

[[notes/_link/Parkinson's Disease]] is characterized by the selective loss of [[notes/_link/Dopaminergic Neurons]] in the [[notes/_link/Substantia Nigra]] pars compacta, where three intersecting oxidative mechanisms converge. First, [[notes/_link/Dopamine]] itself is inherently unstable: its catechol ring undergoes [[Autoxidation]] in the presence of iron or [[notes/_link/Monoamine oxidase|MAO]] to form dopamine quinones, superoxide, H2O2, and the neurotoxic metabolite [[6-Hydroxydopamine|6-OHDA]]. Dopamine quinones covalently adduct to [[cysteine]] residues on [[Alpha-synuclein]] (αSyn), stabilizing its toxic oligomeric forms and impairing [[Proteasome|proteasomal]] degradation. Second, [[notes/_link/Mitochondrial]] Complex I activity is reduced by ~30–40% in the [[notes/_link/Substantia Nigra]] of PD patients, causing [[notes/_link/Mitochondrial Dysfunction|energetic failure]] and increased electron leakage to oxygen, amplifying [[Superoxide Radicals|superoxide]] production. This Complex I defect can be reproduced by environmental toxins like [[MPTP]] (through its metabolite MPP+) and [[Rotenone]], both of which selectively inhibit Complex I and induce parkinsonism in humans and animal models. Third, [[Neuroinflammation]] driven by activated [[notes/_link/Macrophage|microglia]] releases [[notes/_link/Nitric Oxide]] and [[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] that further damage adjacent dopaminergic neurons. [[Peroxynitrite]], formed from the reaction of NO with superoxide, nitrates [[Alpha-synuclein]] at tyrosine residues (particularly Tyr-39, Tyr-125, and Tyr-133), promoting its aggregation into [[Lewy Bodies]], the pathological hallmark of PD.

## Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: SOD1 Mutations and Excitotoxicity

Mutations in [[notes/_link/Superoxide Dismutase]] (SOD1) account for ~20% of familial ALS cases. While the initial hypothesis implicated loss of SOD1 enzymatic activity (i.e., impaired superoxide scavenging), subsequent research established that mutant SOD1 gains a toxic function: aberrant [[Fenton reaction|copper-mediated chemistry]] that generates hydroxyl radicals and peroxynitrite, and the formation of insoluble intracellular aggregates that impair the [[Unfolded Protein Response|proteasome]] and [[Autophagy|autophagic]] pathways. More than 180 distinct SOD1 mutations have been identified, with the A4V and G93A variants being the most studied. Mutant SOD1 also triggers [[Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress|ER stress]] by binding to the ER chaperone [[Derlin-1]] and activating the [[Unfolded Protein Response]] ([[notes/_link/Unfolded Protein Response|UPR]]), leading to motor neuron apoptosis. Concurrently, loss of the astrocytic [[Glutamate Transporter|glutamate transporter EAAT2]] in ALS leads to [[Excitotoxicity|excitotoxic]] Ca^2+ overload in motor neurons, which activates [[NADPH Oxidase]] (NOX2) and [[Calpain]], unleashing further ROS production and proteolytic damage. The combination of SOD1-mediated oxidative stress, proteasomal dysfunction, and excitotoxicity creates a self-amplifying degenerative cascade specific to [[Motor Neurons|motor neurons]].

## Glial Contributions: Microglial ROS and Astrocytic Dysfunction

[[notes/_link/Neuroinflammation|Neuroinflammation]] mediated by microglia and astrocytes amplifies and propagates oxidative damage in all neurodegenerative diseases. Activated microglia undergo a [[notes/_link/Respiratory Burst]] via [[NADPH Oxidase]] (NOX2), producing large quantities of [[Superoxide Radicals|superoxide]] that dismutate to [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide]], which diffuses into neighboring neurons and causes oxidative damage. Microglia also release [[notes/_link/Nitric Oxide]] via iNOS (NOS2), which reacts with superoxide to form [[Peroxynitrite]], causing [[Nitrative Stress|nitrative damage]] to nearby cells. In [[notes/_link/Alzheimer's Disease]], Aβ-activated microglia produce [[TNF-alpha]], [[IL-1b]], and [[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] that impair neuronal synaptic function and promote tau pathology. In PD, α-synuclein released from degenerating neurons activates microglial [[notes/_link/Toll-like Receptor|TLR2]] and [[TLR4]], perpetuating a cycle of neuroinflammation and oxidative neurodegeneration. Astrocytes normally protect neurons through [[notes/_link/Glutathione]] export, [[notes/_link/Metabolism|glutamate]] uptake, and [[iron]] sequestration, but in neurodegenerative states, astrocytic [[notes/_link/Nrf2]] signaling declines, reducing antioxidant capacity. Reactive astrocytes (A1 phenotype) lose their neuroprotective functions and instead release toxic factors, including [[Complement C3]] and [[chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans]] that inhibit neuronal repair.

## Therapeutic Strategies Targeting Oxidative Stress

Current therapeutic approaches aim to break the cycle of oxidative damage and neurodegeneration. [[notes/_link/MitoQ|MitoQ]] (mitoquinone mesylate) is a mitochondria-targeted derivative of [[Coenzyme Q10]] that concentrates ~1000-fold in the mitochondrial matrix, reducing [[Mitochondrial ROS|mitochondrial ROS]]. The phase II PROOF trial in PD demonstrated reduced decline in off-medication UPDRS scores over 12 months. [[notes/_link/N-Acetylcysteine]] (NAC) replenishes [[notes/_link/Glutathione]] and has shown benefit in [[notes/_link/Parkinson's Disease|PD]] and [[notes/_link/Alzheimer's Disease|AD]] models by reducing oxidative protein damage and improving [[notes/_link/Mitochondria|mitochondrial function]]. [[notes/_link/Vitamin E]] supplementation trials in AD (e.g., the TEAM-AD trial) showed modest slowing of functional decline at high doses (2000 IU/day). [[notes/_link/Curcumin]] and [[notes/_link/Resveratrol]] have pleiotropic effects including [[notes/_link/Nrf2]] activation, [[notes/_link/NF-kappa B]] inhibition, and direct ROS scavenging, though bioavailability remains a challenge. [[notes/_link/Exercise]] and [[notes/_link/Caloric Restriction]] are among the most effective interventions, increasing [[notes/_link/PGC-1α]] expression, promoting [[notes/_link/Mitophagy]], and inducing neuronal [[notes/_link/Antioxidants|antioxidant]] enzymes through [[notes/_link/Nrf2]] and [[notes/_link/FOXO]] activation.
