# Ionizing Radiation
Ionizing radiation is radiation, traveling as particles or electromagnetic waves, that carries sufficient energy to detach electrons from atoms or molecules, thereby ionizing them. This process leads to the immediate generation of [[Reactive Oxygen Species]], resulting in extensive [[DNA Damage]] and systemic [[Oxidative Stress]].

## Molecular Mechanisms of Radiation-Induced Oxidative Damage

### Water Radiolysis and Primary ROS Generation
The interaction of ionizing photons (X-rays, γ-rays) or charged particles with cellular water generates a cascade of reactive species within femtoseconds. The primary event — water radiolysis — produces the [[Hydroxyl Radicals|hydroxyl radical]] (•OH; yield ~0.28 µmol/J), hydrated electrons (e⁻ₐq), and hydrogen atoms (H•). The •OH radical is the most damaging species, reacting with [[DNA]], [[Proteins|proteins]], and [[Lipids|lipids]] at near diffusion-limited rates. Molecular oxygen reacts with organic radicals (R•) to form peroxyl radicals (ROO•), propagating oxidative chain reactions that persist long after the initial exposure.

### Direct vs. Indirect DNA Damage
Approximately two-thirds of radiation-induced [[DNA Damage]] is attributable to indirect effects from •OH radicals, while one-third results from direct ionization of DNA itself. Direct effects generate [[DNA radical cations]] (•+dG) and [[base lesions]]; indirect effects produce single-strand breaks (SSBs) and double-strand breaks (DSBs), the latter being the most lethal lesion. Clustered DNA damage — two or more lesions within 1–2 helical turns — is a hallmark of ionizing radiation and is particularly challenging for [[DNA Repair]] machinery. The efficiency of DSB repair by [[non-homologous end joining]] (NHEJ) and [[Homologous Recombination]] (HR) determines cellular radiosensitivity.

### Mitochondrial Amplification and Bystander Effects
Post-irradiation [[Mitochondria]] generate a sustained burst of ROS — termed "mitochondrial oxidative amplification" — that can last for hours to days. This delayed ROS production arises from dysfunction of [[Respiratory Chain Complex I]] and [[Respiratory Chain Complex III|III]], leading to electron leakage and [[Superoxide Radicals]] formation. [[Radiation-induced bystander effect]] (RIBE) refers to the propagation of damage signals to non-irradiated neighboring cells via gap junctions and paracrine factors (e.g., [[TNF-alpha]], [[TGF-beta]], and [[Nitric Oxide]]), amplifying the initial insult.

### Signaling Consequences: ATM/ATR, p53, and Senescence
DNA DSBs activate the [[ATM]] (ataxia telangiectasia mutated) kinase, which phosphorylates downstream targets including [[CHK2]], [[p53]], and [[BRCA1]]. ATM also activates [[AMPK]], linking DNA damage to metabolic checkpoints. [[p53]] coordinates cell cycle arrest (G₁/S through [[p21]]), [[Apoptosis]] (via [[Bax]]/[[Puma]]), and [[Senescence]]. Cells that survive radiation but retain unresolved damage enter a state of premature senescence characterized by [[SASP]] (senescence-associated secretory phenotype), which drives chronic [[Inflammation]] through secretion of [[IL-6]], [[IL-1b]], and [[MMP]]s.

### Clinical Implications: Radiotherapy and Radioprotection
Fractionated radiotherapy exploits differential DNA repair capacity between tumor and normal tissues, while generating a "radiation flux" of ROS that contributes to tumor cell killing. However, normal tissue toxicity — including [[radiation pneumonitis]], [[radiation fibrosis]], [[mucositis]], and [[myelosuppression]] — is driven by excessive [[Oxidative Stress]]. Radioprotective agents such as [[Amifostine]] (WR-2721) scavenge •OH radicals and donate hydrogen atoms to repair carbon-centered radicals. [[N-Acetylcysteine]], [[Ascorbic Acid|Vitamin C]], and [[Vitamin E]] have been investigated as mitigators of radiation-induced oxidative injury.
