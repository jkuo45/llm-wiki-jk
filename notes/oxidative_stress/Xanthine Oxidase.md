# Xanthine Oxidase

## Definition
Xanthine oxidase (XO) is a form of the enzyme xanthine oxidoreductase that catalyzes the oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. It is a significant biological source of [[Reactive Oxygen Species]].

## Biological Role
- **Purine Metabolism:** The rate-limiting enzyme in the final stages of purine breakdown.
- **ROS Production:** Unlike its precursor form (xanthine dehydrogenase), XO uses molecular oxygen as an electron acceptor, producing [[Superoxide Radicals]] and [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] as byproducts.

## Impact on Health
- **Hyperuricemia:** Excessive XO activity leads to elevated uric acid, which can cause gout and renal damage.
- **Oxidative Stress:** Contributes to endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, particularly in ischemia-reperfusion injury and [[Cardiovascular Disease]].

## Connections
- [[Uric Acid]]: The final product of the reaction.
- [[Superoxide Radicals]]: A major byproduct.
- [[Hydrogen Peroxide]]: Another significant byproduct.
- [[Renal Failure]]: Linked to uric acid deposition and ROS-induced damage.

## The Xanthine Dehydrogenase-to-Oxidase Conversion

Xanthine oxidoreductase exists in two interconvertible forms: xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) and xanthine oxidase (XO). Both forms are products of the same gene (XDH in humans) and share identical subunit composition — each ~150 kDa homodimer containing one [[molybdenum]] cofactor (Moco), two [[Fe2S2|2Fe–2S]] clusters, and one [[FAD]] cofactor per subunit. The critical difference lies in the electron acceptor used at the FAD site: XDH preferentially reduces NAD+ to NADH, whereas XO exclusively reduces molecular oxygen to [[Superoxide Radicals]] and [[Hydrogen Peroxide]]. The conversion from XDH to XO occurs via two mechanisms. **Reversible conversion** involves oxidation of critical cysteine residues (Cys-535 and Cys-992 in bovine enzyme; Cys-992 in human) to form intramolecular disulfide bonds, which alters the FAD microenvironment to favor oxygen as an electron acceptor. This can be reversed by [[dithiothreitol]] (DTT) or [[Thioredoxin|thioredoxin]]/[[Glutathione|glutaredoxin]] systems. **Irreversible conversion** involves proteolytic cleavage (by trypsin-like proteases such as [[Calpain]] and [[trypsin]]) that removes a ~20 kDa N-terminal domain, permanently locking the enzyme in the oxidase conformation. In vivo, the XDH/XO ratio shifts dramatically during [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischemia]]: within minutes of ischemic onset, cellular ATP depletion causes Ca^2+-dependent [[Calpain]] activation, which proteolytically converts XDH to XO. Simultaneously, the decline in NAD+ levels and accumulation of reducing equivalents (due to failed electron transport) favor XO activity. Upon reperfusion with oxygenated blood, the accumulated XO converts newly available oxygen to a burst of superoxide and H2O2, initiating the oxidative cascade that characterizes reperfusion injury.

## Catalytic Mechanism and Kinetics

The catalytic cycle of XO proceeds through two sequential hydroxylation reactions. At the [[Molybdenum Cofactor|molybdenum center]], a water molecule is activated to a Mo=O group, and the carbon–hydrogen bond of the substrate (hypoxanthine or xanthine) is cleaved, with the oxygen atom transferred to the C-2 (hypoxanthine → xanthine) or C-8 (xanthine → uric acid) position. The reducing equivalents (two electrons per reaction) are shuttled through the enzyme's internal electron transport chain: the Mo center → 2Fe–2S cluster I → 2Fe–2S cluster II → FAD. At the FAD site, the electron acceptor determines product identity: with NAD+ (XDH form), the two electrons reduce NAD+ to NADH; with O2 (XO form), the electrons reduce O2 to [[Superoxide Radicals]] (single-electron reduction, generating O2•−) or [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] (two-electron reduction, generating H2O2). The ratio of superoxide to H2O2 production by XO depends on pH, oxygen tension, and substrate concentration: at low pH (<6.5) and low O2, superoxide predominates; at high pH (>7.5) and high O2, H2O2 is favored. In physiological conditions (pH 7.4, pO2 ~40 mmHg in tissues), XO produces approximately 80% superoxide and 20% H2O2. The enzyme also catalyzes the one-electron reduction of various drugs and xenobiotics (e.g., [[Mitomycin C]], [[Doxorubicin]], [[Paraquat]]), acting as a [[Drug Metabolism|drug-metabolizing]] enzyme that can bioactivate prodrugs or generate toxic metabolites.

## Tissue Distribution and Systemic Effects

Xanthine oxidoreductase is expressed at the highest levels in the [[Liver|liver]] and [[Small Intestine|small intestine]], with significant expression in the kidney, [[Brain|brain]], and heart. In the [[Cardiovascular Disease|vascular system]], XO is present both intracellularly (in endothelial cells) and circulating in the plasma. Endothelial XO is released into the bloodstream by heparin-displaceable binding to [[glycosaminoglycans]] (heparan sulfate) on the endothelial surface. Circulating XO retains catalytic activity and binds to vascular glycosaminoglycans in distal tissues, functioning as a mobile source of ROS that can amplify [[Endothelial Dysfunction]] throughout the vasculature. Plasma XO activity is elevated in [[Chronic Heart Failure]], [[Coronary Heart Disease]], [[Diabetes Mellitus]], and [[Chronic Kidney Disease]], making it a systemic contributor to oxidative stress. [[Lactate]], elevated in ischemic tissues and during exercise, inhibits the re-conversion of XO to XDH, favoring the oxidase form and contributing to exercise-induced oxidative stress. Conversely, [[Allopurinol]] and [[oxypurinol]] (the active metabolite) suppress XO activity, which is the basis for the observed improvement in [[Flow-Mediated Dilation|endothelial function]] and reduction in [[Reactive Oxygen Species|oxidative stress biomarkers]] in patients with [[Cardiovascular Disease|cardiovascular disease]].

## Pathophysiology in Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

The XO model of [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury]] is one of the best-established paradigms in oxidative stress biology. During ischemia, [[ATP]] is degraded to [[hypoxanthine]] and [[xanthine]], which accumulate in tissues because the downstream [[Xanthine Dehydrogenase|XDH/XO]] cannot function without O2 as an electron acceptor. Simultaneously, XDH is converted to XO (by [[Calpain]]-mediated proteolysis). Upon reperfusion, the sudden availability of O2 allows XO to rapidly oxidize the accumulated hypoxanthine/xanthine to [[Uric Acid]], generating a burst of [[Superoxide Radicals]] and [[Hydrogen Peroxide]]. This initial ROS burst triggers a cascade that includes [[Iron]] release from [[Ferritin|ferritin]] (by superoxide reducing Fe^3+ to Fe^2+), [[Fenton Reaction|Fenton chemistry]] generating [[Hydroxyl Radicals]], activation of [[NADPH Oxidase]] (NOX2) in infiltrating [[Neutrophils]], and [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] permeability transition. The relative contribution of XO to IR injury is tissue-dependent: XO is the dominant source of ROS in [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|intestinal IR]] (where XO activity is highest), contributes substantially in [[Myocardial infarction|cardiac IR]], and plays a lesser but still significant role in [[Cerebral Ischemia|stroke]] and [[Renal Failure|renal IR]]. In animal models, allopurinol pretreatment reduces infarct size by 30–50% in myocardial IR, and febuxostat (a non-purine XO inhibitor) shows similar or superior cardioprotection. Human trials of allopurinol in acute myocardial infarction (the ALL-MI and ALL-HEART trials) have shown mixed results, with benefits most evident in patients with pre-existing [[Cardiovascular Disease|cardiovascular risk factors]].

## Xanthine Oxidase Inhibitors as Therapeutics

XO inhibitors are classified into three generations. **First-generation** ([[Allopurinol]] and its active metabolite [[oxypurinol]]) are purine analogs that competitively inhibit XO and also reduce purine synthesis via feedback inhibition of [[Hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase|HGPRT]]. Allopurinol has been the standard of care for [[Gout]] for over 50 years and also reduces cardiovascular events in patients with [[Gout|hyperuricemia]] through both urate-lowering and antioxidant effects. **Second-generation** [[febuxostat]] is a non-purine selective XO inhibitor that inhibits both oxidized and reduced forms of XO (unlike allopurinol, which only inhibits the reduced form). The CONFIRMS trial showed febuxostat was superior to allopurinol in achieving serum urate targets, but the [[CARES]] trial raised safety concerns about cardiovascular mortality with febuxostat compared to allopurinol. **Third-generation** inhibitors include [[topiroxostat]] (approved in Japan) and [[tisoxostat]], which have higher selectivity and longer half-lives. Beyond gout, XO inhibitors are being investigated for [[Chronic Kidney Disease]] (the CKD-FIX and PERL trials), [[Heart Failure]] (the EXACT-HF trial), and [[Hypertension|hypertension]] (particularly in adolescents with [[Hypertension|early hypertension]]). The clinical benefit likely derives from the dual effect of reducing both [[Uric Acid]] (a [[pro-inflammatory]] crystal-forming metabolite that also activates [[NLRP3 Inflammasome|NLRP3 inflammasome]]) and [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] (reducing [[Endothelial Dysfunction]] and [[Inflammation|inflammation]]).

## Emerging Roles in Metabolism and Signaling

Recent evidence indicates that XO plays roles beyond purine catabolism. Under [[Hypoxia|hypoxic]] conditions, XO can function as a [[Nitrate Reductase|nitrate reductase]], reducing [[nitrite]] (NO2−) to [[Nitric Oxide]] (NO), providing an alternative source of NO when NOS enzymes are inactive (due to low O2 or BH4 deficiency). This nitrite-reductase activity of XO is enhanced at acidic pH and under [[Hypoxia|hypoxia]], conditions typical of [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischemic tissues]]. XO also modulates [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]] through ROS-mediated [[PPARγ]] regulation, and its expression is upregulated in [[Adipose Tissue|adipose tissue]] of obese individuals, linking [[Metabolic Syndromes|obesity]] to systemic oxidative stress. In the gut, XO activity contributes to the mucosal barrier through [[Hydrogen Peroxide|H2O2]]-mediated antimicrobial activity, and its expression is regulated by gut microbial [[Metabolites]] including [[Butyrate|butyrate]]. This places XO at a critical intersection between [[Gut Microbiome|microbiome]] composition, purine metabolism, and host redox state — a rapidly evolving area of research.
