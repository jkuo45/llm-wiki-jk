# A20

**A20 (TNF alpha-induced protein 3, TNFAIP3)** is a dual-function ubiquitin-editing enzyme that functions as a master negative regulator of [[NF-kappa B]] signaling and [[Inflammation]]. It terminates pro-inflammatory signaling downstream of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL-1β, Toll-like receptor (TLR), and NOD-like receptor pathways, and is a critical molecular brake preventing chronic inflammation and autoimmunity.

## Structure & Mechanism

A20 is a 790-amino-acid protein containing two functionally distinct domains. The N-terminal ovarian tumor (OTU) domain possesses deubiquitinase (DUB) activity that removes K63-linked polyubiquitin chains from key signaling intermediates, including [[RIPK1]], [[TRAF6]], and the IKK regulatory subunit [[NEMO]]/IKKγ. The C-terminal zinc-finger domain (ZnF4–ZnF7) confers E3 ubiquitin ligase activity that conjugates K48-linked polyubiquitin chains onto those same substrates, marking them for proteasomal degradation. This coordinated "ubiquitin-editing" removes activating signals and targets the signaling complex for destruction, thereby terminating [[NF-kappa B]] activation.

A20 is itself an immediate-early transcriptional target of NF-κB, establishing a rapid, self-limiting negative feedback loop. Within minutes of TNF stimulation, A20 expression is induced, halting further NF-κB nuclear translocation and cytokine production. This feedback is essential: cells lacking A20 show prolonged NF-κB activity and sustained production of [[TNF-alpha]] and IL-6.

## Physiological Function

By constraining NF-κB, A20 maintains immune homeostasis and tolerance. It protects endothelial cells from TNF-induced apoptosis, limits leukocyte adhesion and vascular inflammation, and restrains inflammasome activation. In hematopoietic cells, A20 prevents spontaneous activation and controls the magnitude of both innate and adaptive immune responses. Loss of a single A20 allele in mice causes multi-organ inflammation, cachexia, and premature death, underscoring its non-redundant protective role.

## Pathological & Clinical Relevance

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms in TNFAIP3 are among the strongest genetic risk loci for [[Rheumatoid Arthritis]], [[Inflammatory Bowel Disease]], systemic lupus erythematosus, [[Psoriasis]], and [[Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease]]. These variants—often in enhancer regions—reduce A20 expression, weakening the brake on NF-κB and amplifying inflammatory signaling. Heterozygous loss-of-function mutations cause A20 haploinsufficiency syndrome (HA20), a rare autosomal-dominant autoinflammatory disorder featuring early-onset recurrent fevers, arthritis, and colitis, treated with IL-1/IL-6 blockade or TNF inhibition.

Under [[Oxidative Stress]], A20 activity is impaired by oxidation of its catalytic cysteine residues, blunting DUB function and permitting sustained NF-κB activation—a mechanism that links redox imbalance to chronic inflammation in diseases ranging from COPD to atherosclerosis. Conversely, A20 induction by antioxidants such as [[Sulforaphane]] (via [[NRF2]]) is being explored as an anti-inflammatory strategy.
