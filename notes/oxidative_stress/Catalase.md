# Catalase

## Definition
Catalase is a common enzyme found in nearly all living organisms exposed to oxygen. It catalyzes the decomposition of [[notes/_link/Hydrogen Peroxide]] to water and oxygen.

## Function
It is a very important enzyme in protecting the cell from oxidative damage by [[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species]] (ROS).

## Structure and Catalytic Mechanism
Catalase is a tetrameric heme-containing enzyme (MW ~240 kDa) with each subunit harboring a ferriprotoporphyrin IX (heme b) active site. The enzyme exhibits one of the highest turnover numbers known in biochemistry ($k_{cat} \approx 10^7$ s⁻¹), enabling it to decompose millions of $H_2O_2$ molecules per second. The catalytic cycle proceeds in two steps. In the first step, $H_2O_2$ oxidizes the ferric heme iron (Fe³⁺) to an oxyferryl intermediate (Compound I, Fe⁴⁺=O porphyrin π-cation radical), releasing water. In the second step, a second $H_2O_2$ molecule acts as a reducing substrate, donating two electrons to regenerate the resting ferric enzyme while producing O₂ and H₂O: $2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2$. This "catalatic" reaction distinguishes catalase from other peroxidases in that it uses $H_2O_2$ both as oxidant and reductant, avoiding the need for exogenous reducing cofactors such as [[notes/_link/Glutathione]] or NADPH.

The enzyme also exhibits peroxidase activity (using organic substrates such as ethanol, methanol, or phenols as electron donors) in the presence of low steady-state $H_2O_2$ concentrations. NADPH binding to catalase (four molecules per tetramer) protects the enzyme from inactivation by its own Compound I intermediate, particularly under conditions of oxidative stress. The mammalian catalase gene (CAT) is located on chromosome 11p13 and encodes a 527-amino-acid protein that is targeted primarily to [[Peroxisomes]] via a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting signal 1 (PTS1; Ser-Lys-Leu).

## Cellular Localization and Compartmentalization
In mammalian cells, catalase is predominantly localized within [[Peroxisomes]], organelles that also house $H_2O_2$-generating oxidases (e.g., acyl-CoA oxidase in [[notes/_link/Fatty Acid Oxidation]], D-amino acid oxidase, and urate oxidase). This spatial arrangement allows catalase to capture $H_2O_2$ at its source, preventing its diffusion to the [[notes/_link/Cytosol]] and [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]] where it could participate in [[Fenton reaction]] chemistry. Lower catalase activity is present in the cytosol and [[notes/_link/Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] matrix, where [[Glutathione Peroxidase]] (GPx1) serves as the primary $H_2O_2$ scavenger. Erythrocytes contain high catalase activity, reflecting their need to detoxify the large flux of $H_2O_2$$ generated by the spontaneous dismutation of [[Superoxide Radicals]] produced during hemoglobin autoxidation.

## Clinical Significance
**Acatalasemia** ([[Takahara's Disease]]) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the CAT gene, resulting in absent or severely deficient catalase activity. Most patients are asymptomatic, but up to 50% develop progressive oral gangrene (Takahara's disease) due to the inability of oral [[Streptococci]] and accumulated $H_2O_2$ to be detoxified, leading to tissue necrosis. This condition is most prevalent in Japanese and Korean populations (carrier frequency ~1.5%). In contrast to the mild phenotype of acatalasemia, catalase overexpression in transgenic models confers protection against [[notes/_link/Ischemia-reperfusion Injury]], [[notes/_link/Diabetes Mellitus]]-induced nephropathy, and [[notes/_link/Atherosclerosis]], suggesting that $H_2O_2$ detoxification capacity is rate-limiting for oxidative stress resistance in many tissues.

In [[Cancer]], catalase expression is frequently downregulated in tumor cells compared to their normal counterparts, particularly in [[notes/_link/Breast Cancer]], [[notes/_link/Prostate Cancer]], and [[notes/_link/Hepatocellular Carcinoma]]. This reduction in catalase activity elevates steady-state $H_2O_2$ levels, which paradoxically may promote proliferation by activating redox-sensitive transcription factors ([[notes/_link/NFKB]], [[notes/_link/AP-1]]) while simultaneously increasing genomic instability. The "catalase paradox" in aging is notable: [[notes/_link/Caloric Restriction]] upregulates catalase expression in several tissues, and mice overexpressing human catalase targeted to [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]] (MCAT mice) show a ~20% increase in median lifespan and delayed cardiac and cataract pathology, directly implicating mitochondrial $H_2O_2$ in the aging process.

## Interactions with Other Antioxidant Systems
Catalase operates in parallel with the [[notes/_link/Glutathione Peroxidase]] (GPx) and [[notes/_link/Thioredoxin Peroxidase]] ([[notes/_link/Peroxiredoxin]]) systems. While GPx has a higher affinity for $H_2O_2$ ($K_m$ ~1–10 μM) than catalase ($K_m$ ~25–100 mM), catalase has a vastly higher $V_{max}$ and does not consume NADPH or glutathione. Thus, at low $H_2O_2$ fluxes, GPx predominates; at high fluxes (e.g., during the [[notes/_link/Respiratory Burst]] or in [[Peroxisomes]]), catalase becomes the dominant scavenger. The interplay between these systems is essential for maintaining the low steady-state $H_2O_2$ (~1–10 nM) typical of most cell types.
