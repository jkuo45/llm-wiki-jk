# Free Radicals

## Definition
Free radicals are atoms, molecules, or ions that have at least one unpaired valence electron. This unpaired electron makes them highly chemically reactive toward other substances, or even toward themselves.

## Role in Oxidative Stress
In biological systems, free radicals are frequently derived from oxygen ([[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species]]) or nitrogen ([[Reactive Nitrogen Species]]). They can initiate chain reactions, such as [[Lipid Peroxidation]], where a single radical can cause the oxidation of many lipid molecules. 

## Key Examples
- **[[Superoxide Radicals]]** (O2•−)
- **[[Hydroxyl Radicals]]** (•OH)
- **[[notes/_link/Nitric Oxide]]** (NO•)

## Reactivity and Classification
Free radicals vary dramatically in their half-lives and target specificity. Oxygen-centered radicals ([[notes/_link/Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]]) include [[Superoxide Radicals]] (t½ ~1–10 μs in biological milieus) and the exceedingly reactive [[Hydroxyl Radicals]] (t½ ~10⁻⁹ s), which reacts at diffusion-limited rates with virtually any molecule within its immediate vicinity. Nitrogen-centered radicals, primarily [[notes/_link/Nitric Oxide]] (t½ ~1–5 s), are more selective and serve dual signaling and cytotoxic roles. Carbon-centered radicals (e.g., lipid alkyl radicals, L•) arise during [[Lipid Peroxidation]] as intermediates that propagate chain reactions through polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in [[Cell membranes]]. Thiyl radicals (RS•) are generated from thiol oxidation and participate in redox cycling with [[notes/_link/Glutathione]] and protein thiols.

## Chain Reaction Mechanisms
A defining feature of free radical chemistry is the propagation of chain reactions. In the initiation step, a primary radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from a target molecule (e.g., a PUFA), creating a secondary radical. During propagation, this radical reacts with O₂ to form a peroxyl radical (ROO•), which in turn abstracts hydrogen from adjacent molecules, perpetuating the cycle. Thousands of oxidation events can arise from a single initiating radical before termination occurs via radical–radical recombination or interception by chain-breaking [[notes/_link/Antioxidants]] such as [[notes/_link/Vitamin E]] (α-tocopherol) in lipid phases or [[notes/_link/Ascorbic Acid]] (vitamin C) in aqueous compartments. The branching of radical chains — particularly through iron-catalyzed decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides via the [[Fenton reaction]] — amplifies oxidative damage and is central to the pathology of [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]].

## Pathological Implications
Uncontrolled free radical production damages all major biomolecule classes. In [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]], electron leakage from Complex I and III of the respiratory chain generates [[Superoxide Radicals]] that overwhelm matrix-localized [[notes/_link/Superoxide Dismutase]] (MnSOD/SOD2), contributing to [[notes/_link/Mitochondrial Dysfunction]]. Free radical-mediated [[notes/_link/DNA Damage]] — especially [[8-oxo-2'-deoxyguanosine]] formation and single-strand breaks — drives [[notes/_link/Genomic Instability]] and is implicated in [[Cancer]] initiation and progression. In [[notes/_link/Cardiovascular Disease]], the oxidation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) by free radicals within the arterial wall is a rate-limiting step in [[notes/_link/Atherosclerosis]] plaque formation. In the central nervous system, the high oxygen consumption and modest antioxidant capacity of neural tissue render it especially vulnerable; free radical damage to [[notes/_link/Dopaminergic Neurons]] is a hallmark of [[notes/_link/Parkinson's Disease]], and [[notes/_link/Beta-amyloid]] plaques in [[notes/_link/Alzheimer's Disease]] promote local free radical generation through metal ion chelation.

## Therapeutic Targeting
Strategies to mitigate free radical damage include direct small-molecule radical scavengers ([[notes/_link/N-Acetylcysteine]] as a glutathione precursor, [[notes/_link/Melatonin]], [[notes/_link/EGCG]] from green tea), enhancement of endogenous enzymatic defenses (activation of the [[notes/_link/NRF2]]/ARE pathway via [[notes/_link/Sulforaphane]]), and prevention of radical formation through metal chelation ([[notes/_link/EDTA]], deferoxamine) or [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]]-targeted antioxidants (MitoQ, [[notes/_link/Spermidine]]-based compounds). The hormetic response induced by low-level free radical exposure — a concept termed [[notes/_link/Mitohormesis]] — paradoxically strengthens cellular resilience by upregulating [[notes/_link/Antioxidants]] and [[notes/_link/DNA Repair]] machinery, informing lifestyle interventions such as [[notes/_link/Caloric Restriction]] and [[notes/_link/Exercise]].

## Connections
- [[notes/oxidative_stress/Oxidative Stress]]: The pathological state resulting from an excess of free radicals.
- [[notes/_link/Antioxidants]]: Molecules that neutralize free radicals by donating an electron.
- [[notes/_link/Mitochondria]]: The primary site of endogenous free radical production.
- [[notes/_link/Inflammation]]: Inflammatory cells ([[notes/_link/Neutrophils]], [[notes/_link/Macrophage]]) deliberately produce free radicals via [[NADPH Oxidase]] during the [[notes/_link/Respiratory Burst]].
- [[notes/_link/Aging]]: The free radical theory of aging (Harman, 1956) posits that accumulated oxidative damage drives senescence.
