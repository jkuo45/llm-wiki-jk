# Myocardial infarction

## Overview

**Myocardial infarction** (MI), commonly known as a heart attack, is the irreversible death of [[Cardiomyocytes]] caused by prolonged [[Ischemia]] — an acute supply–demand mismatch of oxygenated blood to the [[Myocardium]]. MI is a leading manifestation of [[Heart disease|heart disease]], with approximately 800,000 cases annually in the United States (one every 40 seconds). It is classified clinically into two main subtypes:

- **STEMI (ST-elevation myocardial infarction)** — transmural ischemia with full-thickness myocardial necrosis, identified by ST-segment elevation on [[ECG]]. STEMI typically results from total occlusion of a major coronary artery and requires immediate reperfusion.
- **NSTEMI (non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction)** — subendocardial ischemia with partial-thickness necrosis, presenting as ST depression, T-wave inversion, or non-specific ECG changes. NSTEMI usually involves subtotal occlusion or microembolization.

The distinction is prognostically important: STEMI carries higher acute mortality but a clearer revascularization target; NSTEMI patients are older with more comorbidities and worse long-term outcomes. Unstable angina, NSTEMI, and STEMI together constitute **acute coronary syndrome** (ACS), a continuum of ischemic [[Heart disease]].

## Pathophysiology

The proximate cause of most MIs is **atherosclerotic plaque rupture** or **plaque erosion**. Rupture of a vulnerable plaque — characterized by a thin fibrous cap, large lipid-rich necrotic core, and dense macrophage infiltration — exposes highly thrombogenic subendothelial collagen and tissue factor to the bloodstream.

**Sequence of events:**

1. **Plaque disruption** — mechanical or inflammatory weakening of the fibrous cap, driven by [[Matrix Metalloproteinases]] secreted by activated macrophages and suppressed collagen synthesis.
2. **Thrombus formation** — platelet adhesion, activation, and aggregation at the exposed site, with fibrin deposition and thrombin generation, forming an occlusive or subocclusive thrombus.
3. **Coronary occlusion** — complete (STEMI) or near-complete (NSTEMI) obstruction of coronary blood flow, causing a sharp drop in oxygen delivery.
4. **Myocardial ischemia** — within seconds of occlusion, oxidative phosphorylation ceases, [[ATP]] reserves are depleted, and anaerobic glycolysis becomes the sole energy source. Lactic acidosis, ion pump failure, and cytosolic Ca²⁺ overload ensue.
5. **Cardiomyocyte necrosis** — irreversible cell death begins after 20–40 minutes of severe ischemia, progressing as a "wavefront" from subendocardium to subepicardium. Necrosis is complete in 3–6 hours without reperfusion.
6. **Inflammatory response** — damaged cells release DAMPs (ATP, HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA) that activate the [[NLRP3 Inflammasome]] and recruit [[Neutrophils]] and macrophages via chemokine gradients. Neutrophils clear necrotic debris but also amplify injury via [[Myeloperoxidase Pathway|myeloperoxidase]]-generated ROS and proteolytic enzymes.
7. **Scar formation** — within 1–2 weeks, macrophages and [[Transforming Growth Factor Beta|TGF-β]] signaling drive fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and formation of a collagen-rich scar. The infarcted region becomes non-contractile, leading to ventricular remodeling, wall thinning, and compensatory hypertrophy of viable myocardium — a process that can progressively impair cardiac function and culminate in [[Heart Failure|heart failure]].

## Diagnostic Criteria

MI is defined by the **Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction** (Thygesen et al., *Circulation* 2018), requiring:

**Cardiac troponin rise and/or fall** with at least one value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit, plus at least one of:

- Symptoms of acute myocardial ischemia (chest pain, dyspnea, arm/jaw pain, diaphoresis, nausea)
- New ischemic ECG changes (ST-segment elevation, ST depression, T-wave inversion, new left bundle branch block)
- Development of pathological Q waves
- Imaging evidence of new loss of viable [[Myocardium]] or regional wall motion abnormality
- Identification of intracoronary thrombus by angiography or autopsy

**ECG classification:**

| Feature | STEMI | NSTEMI |
|---|---|---|
| ST segment | Elevation ≥1 mm in contiguous leads | Depression or transient elevation |
| T waves | Hyperacute tall T → inverted | Inversion or flattening |
| Q waves | Develop in hours–days | Rare |
| Occlusion | Transmural, complete | Subendocardial, subtotal |

**Biomarkers:**

- **High-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and I (hs-cTnI)** — the gold-standard biomarkers. hs-cTn assays detect very low concentrations, enabling earlier diagnosis (1–3 hours post-symptom onset). A rise/fall pattern distinguishes acute MI from chronic troponin elevation in [[Chronic Kidney Disease|renal failure]] or heart failure.
- **CK-MB** (creatine kinase myocardial band) — historically used but less sensitive and specific; remains useful for detecting early reinfarction due to its shorter half-life (~12 hours vs. ~5–7 days for troponin).

**Imaging:**
- **Coronary angiography** — the gold standard for identifying culprit lesions and guiding revascularization.
- **[[Echocardiography]]** — demonstrates regional wall motion abnormalities and assesses ejection fraction.
- **[[Cardiac MRI]]** — late gadolinium enhancement delineates infarct size and distinguishes ischemic from non-ischemic scarring.

## Treatment

Management follows a time-critical chain, with the mantra: **"time is muscle."**

**Reperfusion:**

- **Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)** — balloon angioplasty and stent placement at the culprit lesion. Guideline door-to-balloon time: ≤90 minutes for STEMI (≤60 minutes for patients presenting directly to PCI-capable hospitals). PCI is preferred over fibrinolysis when timely available, achieving higher patency rates and lower mortality.
- **[[Fibrinolysis]]** — tissue plasminogen activator (alteplase, tenecteplase) administered when PCI cannot be performed within 120 minutes of first medical contact. Success is defined by >50% ST-segment resolution at 60–90 minutes. Rescue PCI is indicated if fibrinolysis fails.

**Antithrombotic therapy:**

- **Antiplatelet:** Aspirin 162–325 mg chewed immediately, followed by lifelong low-dose aspirin (81 mg/day). P2Y12 inhibitors (ticagrelor 180 mg loading dose, prasugrel 60 mg, or clopidogrel 600 mg) are added for dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for 6–12 months post-PCI.
- **Anticoagulant:** Unfractionated heparin, enoxaparin, or bivalirudin during PCI to prevent thrombus propagation.

**Adjunctive pharmacotherapy:**

- **[[Beta blockers]]** (metoprolol, carvedilol) — reduce myocardial oxygen demand, decrease arrhythmia risk, and improve long-term survival.
- **[[ACE inhibitors]]/[[ARBs]]** — attenuate adverse ventricular remodeling, particularly in anterior MI or reduced ejection fraction (<40%).
- **[[Statins]]** (atorvastatin 80 mg, rosuvastatin 40 mg) — high-intensity statin initiated immediately, irrespective of baseline LDL, to stabilize plaques and reduce recurrent events.
- **[[Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists]]** (eplerenone, spironolactone) — indicated in post-MI patients with ejection fraction ≤40% and heart failure or diabetes.
- **Nitrates** and **[[Opioids]]** (morphine) — for symptom relief during acute ischemia.

**Long-term secondary prevention:**
- DAPT for 6–12 months, then lifelong aspirin
- High-intensity statin (LDL target <55 mg/dL or ≥50% reduction)
- Beta blocker (≥1 year; indefinitely if reduced EF)
- ACEi/ARB (titrated to maximum tolerated dose)
- Lifestyle: Mediterranean diet, smoking cessation, ≥150 min/week aerobic exercise, weight management, and blood pressure (<130/80 mmHg) and glycemic (HbA1c <7%) control

## Adrenochrome Relevance

[[Adrenochrome]], the oxidation product of [[Epinephrine]], is relevant to MI through several intersecting mechanisms:

**Coronary vasoconstriction:** Adrenochrome causes dose-dependent [[Coronary artery constriction]] in animal and ex vivo models — an effect resistant to α-adrenergic blockade but attenuated by [[Cyclooxygenase]] inhibitors (indomethacin) and [[Calcium channel blockers]]. This suggests a non-adrenergic, prostanoid-mediated mechanism that may exacerbate ischemia in the peri-infarct setting.

**Direct myocardial injury:** Adrenochrome undergoes [[Redox Cycling]], generating superoxide and hydrogen peroxide that directly damage [[Cardiomyocytes]]. It inhibits mitochondrial complexes I and II, depletes [[Glutathione]], and promotes oxidative modification of sarcomeric and membrane proteins — all of which intensify ischemic injury and expand infarct size.

**Reperfusion injury amplification:** Upon restoration of blood flow, a burst of ROS generation triggers [[Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore]] opening, Ca²⁺ overload, and additional cell death. Adrenochrome, generated locally during catecholamine surge associated with MI (endogenous epinephrine release from the [[Adrenal gland]] and sympathetic nerve terminals), may amplify this reperfusion injury. Its redox-active quinone structure feeds ROS production precisely when the [[Myocardium]] is most vulnerable.

**Proarrhythmic potential:** Adrenochrome prolongs the cardiac action potential by inhibiting delayed rectifier potassium currents (I_Kr) and L-type calcium channels in isolated cardiomyocytes, creating an electrophysiological substrate for [[Arrhythmias]] — a common and lethal complication of acute MI.

**Clinical context:** [[Myeloperoxidase]] — which catalyzes adrenochrome formation from epinephrine — is elevated in coronary plaques and infarcted myocardium. Detection of adrenochrome-like oxidation products in ischemic cardiac tissue supports the concept of local adrenochrome generation during MI. Although causal quantification in human MI remains limited by analytical challenges, the convergence of adrenochrome's pro-oxidant, vasoconstrictive, and proarrhythmic properties positions it as a plausible contributor to MI pathophysiology — an area warranting further translational investigation.
