# Myocardium

The **myocardium** is the middle layer of the heart wall, situated between the epicardium (visceral layer of the pericardium) and the endocardium. It is composed primarily of [[Cardiomyocytes|cardiomyocytes]] — the contractile muscle cells that generate the mechanical force of the heartbeat. As the thickest layer of the heart wall, the myocardium is responsible for the pumping action that drives blood through the pulmonary and systemic circulations.

## Structure

The myocardium varies markedly in thickness across chambers: thickest in the left ventricular free wall (10–15 mm in adults), reflecting the high pressure required for systemic perfusion; substantially thinner in the right ventricle (3–5 mm); and thinnest in the atria (1–2 mm). This thickness gradient correlates with chamber-specific wall tension requirements.

The muscle fibers are organized into overlapping spiral and circumferential bands that wrap around the ventricular cavities, producing a wringing (clockwise torsion) motion during systole. Individual [[Cardiomyocytes]] are coupled end-to-end by intercalated discs — specialized membrane junctions containing gap junctions (connexin 43) for rapid electrical propagation and desmosomes/fasciae adherens for mechanical adhesion. This coupling allows the myocardium to function as an electrical and mechanical syncytium: a single action potential initiated at the sinoatrial node propagates across the entire myocardium within ~200 ms.

An extensive capillary network supports the high metabolic demand. Capillary density approaches one capillary per cardiomyocyte, maintaining a diffusion distance of only a few micrometers. The myocardium extracts approximately 70% of delivered oxygen at rest, leaving minimal reserve — increased demand is met primarily by coronary vasodilation and increased blood flow rather than increased extraction.

Embedded within the myocardium is the cardiac conduction system: the sinoatrial node (primary pacemaker), atrioventricular node, bundle of His, bundle branches, and [[Purkinje Fibers|Purkinje fibers]]. These specialized myocytes generate and propagate electrical impulses that orchestrate the sequence and timing of chamber contraction.

## Function

The fundamental function of the myocardium is excitation–contraction (E–C) coupling, the process linking the action potential to mechanical contraction:

1. Depolarization propagates along the sarcolemma and into T-tubules.
2. Voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ channels (Cav1.2) open, permitting a small Ca²⁺ influx.
3. The local Ca²⁺ rise opens ryanodine receptor type 2 (RyR2) on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, triggering calcium-induced calcium release (CICR).
4. Cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises from ~100 nM to ~1 μM, binding troponin C. This induces a conformational shift that moves tropomyosin away from actin's myosin-binding sites, permitting cross-bridge cycling and sarcomere shortening.
5. Relaxation ensues as SERCA2a pumps Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum and the sodium–calcium exchanger (NCX) extrudes Ca²⁺ across the sarcolemma.

The coordinated contraction of the myocardium generates cardiac output. Myocardial oxygen demand is governed by three principal determinants: heart rate (chronotropy), contractility (inotropy), and wall tension (determined by preload and afterload). Because the heart possesses minimal anaerobic capacity and depends almost entirely on oxidative phosphorylation, any reduction in oxygen supply rapidly produces contractile dysfunction.

## Response to Injury

The myocardium has limited regenerative capacity, and injury follows a stereotyped progression from acute cellular insult to chronic remodeling.

**Ischemia–reperfusion:** Coronary occlusion deprives cardiomyocytes of oxygen and substrates. ATP depletion leads to failure of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Ca²⁺-ATPase pumps, producing intracellular Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ overload, cell swelling, and activation of degradative enzymes. The hallmark histopathological finding is **contraction band necrosis** — hypercontracted sarcomeres forming dense eosinophilic bands. Necrotic cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit neutrophils within hours, followed by macrophages that clear debris and secrete cytokines ([[IL-6]], [[TNFα]], transforming growth factor β). The inflammatory phase transitions to repair as cardiac fibroblasts deposit collagen, forming a fibrotic scar. While scar formation prevents ventricular rupture, it reduces compliance and creates a substrate for re-entrant arrhythmias. Over weeks to months, the surviving myocardium undergoes remodeling — myocyte hypertrophy, extracellular matrix reorganization, chamber dilation — driving progression to [[Heart Failure|heart failure]].

**Catecholamine-mediated injury:** Sustained catecholamine excess — from pheochromocytoma, emotional stress (takotsubo cardiomyopathy), or exogenous infusion — injures the myocardium through a distinct but overlapping mechanism. β-adrenergic overactivation drives excessive Ca²⁺ influx and ryanodine receptor-mediated sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ leak, producing mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload, opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, and activation of apoptotic cascades. [[Adrenochrome]], an oxidation product of [[Epinephrine]], compounds this injury. Adrenochrome generates [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] via redox cycling, inhibits mitochondrial complexes I and II, and depletes [[Glutathione]]. In animal models, adrenochrome administration produces contraction band necrosis and electrocardiographic abnormalities resembling catecholamine cardiomyopathy. While the quantitative contribution of adrenochrome relative to direct catecholamine effects in human cardiac pathology remains debated, the observation connects oxidative catecholamine biochemistry directly to myocardial tissue damage.
