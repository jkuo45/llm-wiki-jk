# Adrenal gland

The **adrenal glands** (also known as **suprarenal glands**) are paired endocrine organs situated on the superior pole of each [[Kidney]]. Each gland is enclosed by a fibrous capsule and embedded in perirenal fat. Despite their small size (~4–5 g each), they are indispensable for stress adaptation, electrolyte homeostasis, and metabolic regulation. The gland is divided into two embryologically and functionally distinct regions: the outer [[Adrenal cortex]], derived from mesoderm, and the inner [[Adrenal medulla]], derived from neural crest cells. The [[Adrenal cortex]] synthesizes corticosteroids — [[Mineralocorticoids]] (primarily [[Aldosterone]]), [[Glucocorticoids]] (primarily [[Cortisol]]), and adrenal androgens (e.g., [[DHEA]]). The [[Adrenal medulla]] synthesizes [[Catecholamines]] — primarily [[Epinephrine]] (~80%) and [[Norepinephrine]] (~20%).

## Structure & Anatomy

The right adrenal gland is pyramidal in shape and sits superior to the right kidney, while the left adrenal gland is crescent-shaped and extends medially along the left kidney's superior border. Each gland measures approximately 5 × 3 × 1 cm and weighs 4–5 g in healthy adults.

**Blood supply:** The adrenal glands are among the most highly vascularized organs per gram of tissue. Arterial supply arises from three sources: the superior suprarenal arteries (branches of the inferior phrenic artery), the middle suprarenal artery (direct branch of the abdominal aorta), and the inferior suprarenal arteries (branches of the renal artery). These vessels form a subcapsular plexus that drains into the medullary sinusoids, creating a portal-like system that bathes medullary [[Chromaffin cells]] in high concentrations of [[Cortisol]] — a critical regulator of [[PNMT]] (phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase) expression. Venous drainage occurs via the suprarenal vein: the right suprarenal vein drains directly into the [[Inferior Vena Cava]], while the left suprarenal vein drains into the left renal vein.

**Innervation:** The adrenal medulla receives rich sympathetic innervation from preganglionic fibers traveling via the splanchnic nerves (primarily T10–L1). These fibers release [[Acetylcholine]], which acts on nicotinic receptors on [[Chromaffin cells]] to trigger exocytosis of catecholamine-containing secretory granules. In contrast, the [[Adrenal cortex]] receives minimal direct innervation and is regulated primarily by humoral signals ([[ACTH]], [[Angiotensin II]], [[Potassium]]).

## Adrenal Cortex

The [[Adrenal cortex]] constitutes approximately 80–90% of the gland's volume and is organized into three concentric zones, each with distinct enzymatic machinery:

### Zona Glomerulosa (outermost, ~15% of cortex)
Produces [[Mineralocorticoids]], principally [[Aldosterone]], which regulates sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the kidney. The primary regulatory axes are the [[Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System]] (RAAS) and plasma [[Potassium]] concentration. [[ACTH]] exerts a permissive but not primary regulatory role here.

### Zona Fasciculata (middle, ~70% of cortex)
Produces [[Glucocorticoids]] — primarily [[Cortisol]] (~95% of glucocorticoid activity in humans) and smaller amounts of [[Corticosterone]]. Synthesis is driven by the [[HPA Axis]]: [[CRH]] from the hypothalamus stimulates [[ACTH]] release from the anterior pituitary, which in turn upregulates cortical steroidogenesis. [[Cortisol]] exerts negative feedback at both the pituitary and hypothalamic levels.

### Zona Reticularis (innermost, ~15% of cortex)
Produces adrenal androgens, primarily [[DHEA]] and [[DHEA-Sulfate]], which serve as precursors for peripheral conversion to [[Testosterone]] and [[Estradiol]]. This zone also produces small amounts of [[Glucocorticoids]]. [[DHEA]] secretion peaks in early adulthood and declines markedly with age (adrenopause).

## Adrenal Medulla

The [[Adrenal medulla]] is functionally a modified sympathetic ganglion. Its primary cell type is the [[Chromaffin cells]] — modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons that have lost their axons and instead secrete their products directly into the bloodstream.

### Catecholamine Synthesis
The biosynthetic pathway proceeds: [[Tyrosine]] → L-DOPA (via [[Tyrosine Hydroxylase]], TH) → [[Dopamine]] (via DOPA decarboxylase) → [[Norepinephrine]] (via [[Dopamine Beta-Hydroxylase]], DBH) → [[Epinephrine]] (via [[PNMT]]). [[PNMT]] expression is induced by the high local concentration of [[Cortisol]] delivered via the medullary portal circulation — a classic example of endocrine enzyme induction. The medulla stores catecholamines in specialized secretory vesicles along with [[Chromogranins]], [[ATP]], and [[Neuropeptide Y]].

### Secretion and Composition
Sympathetic activation triggers [[Acetylcholine]] release from preganglionic fibers, which binds nicotinic receptors on [[Chromaffin cells]], depolarizing the membrane and opening voltage-gated calcium channels. The resulting calcium influx triggers exocytosis. Human medullary secretion is approximately 80% [[Epinephrine]] and 20% [[Norepinephrine]], though this ratio shifts toward [[Norepinephrine]] under conditions of chronic stress or [[Adrenal medulla]] hyperplasia.

### Adrenochrome Formation
Under conditions of [[Oxidative Stress]], [[Epinephrine]] released from the medulla can undergo oxidation to form [[Adrenochrome]]. This non-enzymatic or enzyme-catalyzed (e.g., by [[Myeloperoxidase]]) conversion is accelerated by transition metal ions and elevated ROS. [[Adrenochrome]] can further polymerize into melanin-like pigments, contributing to the pigmentation observed in the aging adrenal medulla. The [[Adrenochrome Pathway]] represents a shunt that diverts [[Epinephrine]] from receptor-mediated signaling toward oxidative byproducts, potentially contributing to medullary exhaustion under sustained sympathetic drive.

## Pathology

### Adrenal Insufficiency
- **Primary Adrenal Insufficiency ([[Addison's Disease]]):** Autoimmune destruction of the [[Adrenal cortex]] (~80% of cases in developed nations). Presents with hyperpigmentation (due to elevated [[ACTH]]), hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, and fatigue. Requires lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement.
- **Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency:** Pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction causing deficient [[ACTH]] or [[CRH]], respectively. Unlike primary disease, hyperpigmentation is absent and aldosterone production is partially preserved.

### Adrenal Hormone Excess
- **[[Cushing's Syndrome]]:** Chronic [[Cortisol]] excess, most commonly iatrogenic (glucocorticoid therapy). Endogenous causes include [[ACTH]]-secreting pituitary adenoma (Cushing's disease), ectopic [[ACTH]] secretion, or adrenal adenoma/carcinoma. Features include central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, and immune suppression.
- **[[Conn's Syndrome]] (Primary Hyperaldosteronism):** [[Aldosterone]] excess due to adrenal adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia. Manifests as hypertension with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis.
- **Adrenogenital Syndromes:** Excess adrenal androgen production due to congenital enzyme defects (e.g., [[21-Hydroxylase Deficiency]]), leading to virilization and salt-wasting.

### Pheochromocytoma
[[Pheochromocytoma]] is a [[Catecholamines]]-secreting tumor of [[Chromaffin cells]] in the [[Adrenal medulla]]. Paroxysmal hypertension, palpitations, headache, and diaphoresis are classic. Approximately 10% are extra-adrenal (paraganglioma), 10% bilateral, 10% malignant, and 10% hereditary (associated with [[MEN2]], [[VHL disease]], [[NF1]]). Surgical resection after preoperative alpha-blockade is the treatment of choice.
