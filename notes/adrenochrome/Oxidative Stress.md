# Oxidative Stress

Oxidative stress reflects an imbalance between the systemic manifestation of reactive oxygen species and a biological system's ability to readily detoxify the reactive intermediates or to repair the resulting damage. It is both a cause and a consequence of [[Advanced Glycation End Products|AGE]] formation and is a key factor in the development of chronic diseases.

## Definition and Conceptual Framework

The modern definition was formalized by [[Helmut Sies]]: oxidative stress is "an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants in favor of the oxidants, leading to a disruption of redox signaling and control and/or molecular damage" (Sies, 2015, *Redox Biol.* 4:180–183). This definition emphasizes that oxidative stress is not merely damage but fundamentally a disruption of physiological [[Redox signaling]].

| Species | Symbol | Half-life | Reactivity | Primary Source |
|---------|--------|-----------|------------|----------------|
| [[Superoxide anion]] | O₂⁻• | ~1–4 μs (in cells) | Moderate; dismutates to H₂O₂ | [[Mitochondrial complex I]]/[[III]], [[NADPH oxidase]] |
| [[Hydrogen peroxide]] | H₂O₂ | ~1 ms (in cells) | Moderate; diffuses through membranes | [[SOD]]-catalyzed dismutation, [[Peroxisomes]] |
| [[Hydroxyl radical]] | •OH | ~1 ns | Extremely high; reacts at diffusion limit | [[Fenton reaction]] (Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂) |
| [[Peroxynitrite]] | ONOO⁻ | ~10–20 ms | High; nitrates tyrosine residues | [[Superoxide anion]] + [[Nitric oxide]] |

## Sources of ROS

### Mitochondrial Sources

The [[Mitochondrial electron transport chain]] (ETC) is the primary endogenous source of ROS:
- **[[Complex I]] (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase)**: Generates O₂⁻• in the mitochondrial matrix during forward electron transport (especially during [[Reverse electron transport]] from [[Complex II]]).
- **[[Complex III]] (ubiquinol:cytochrome c oxidoreductase)**: Generates O₂⁻• to both the matrix and intermembrane space via the Q-cycle.
- **[[Complex II]] (succinate dehydrogenase)**: Under pathophysiological conditions can produce ROS, particularly when succinate accumulates.

### Enzymatic Sources

- **[[NADPH oxidase]] (NOX family)**: Transmembrane complexes (NOX1–5, DUOX1–2) that deliberately produce O₂⁻• or H₂O₂ for signaling and host defense.
- **[[Myeloperoxidase]] (MPO)**: Produces [[HOCl]] from H₂O₂ and Cl⁻ in [[Neutrophils]], contributing to inflammatory damage.
- **[[Xanthine oxidase]]**: Converts [[Hypoxanthine]] → [[Xanthine]] → [[Uric acid]], generating O₂⁻• and H₂O₂; activated during ischemia-reperfusion.
- **[[Cytochrome P450]] enzymes**: Often leak electrons to O₂ during catalytic cycling.
- **[[Monoamine oxidase]] ([[MAO]])**: Produces H₂O₂ during catecholamine deamination.

## Antioxidant Defense Systems

### Enzymatic Defenses

- **[[Superoxide dismutase]] (SOD)**: Three isoforms — [[SOD1]] (Cu/Zn, cytosol), [[SOD2]] (Mn, mitochondria), [[SOD3]] (Cu/Zn, extracellular) — dismutate O₂⁻• to H₂O₂.
- **[[Catalase]]**: Decomposes H₂O₂ to H₂O and O₂, primarily in peroxisomes. Has one of the highest turnover rates of any enzyme (~10⁷ M⁻¹s⁻¹).
- **[[Glutathione peroxidase]] (GPX)**: Four selenium-containing isoforms (GPX1–4) reduce H₂O₂ and organic hydroperoxides using [[Glutathione]] (GSH) as co-substrate.
- **[[Peroxiredoxin]] (PRDX)**: Six isoforms; reduce H₂O₂, organic hydroperoxides, and peroxynitrite with high affinity.
- **[[Thioredoxin reductase]] (TXNRD)**: Reduces oxidized [[Thioredoxin]], which in turn reduces oxidized PRDX and other targets.

### Non-Enzymatic Defenses

- **[[Glutathione]] (GSH)**: The most abundant intracellular thiol (1–10 mM); directly scavenges ROS and serves as GPX co-substrate.
- **[[Vitamin C]] (ascorbate)**: Major aqueous-phase antioxidant; reduces α-tocopheroxyl radical.
- **[[Vitamin E]] (α-tocopherol)**: Major lipid-phase chain-breaking antioxidant in biomembranes.
- **[[Uric acid]]**: Contributes ~60% of plasma antioxidant capacity.
- **[[Melatonin]]**: Directly scavenges •OH and ONOO⁻; also induces antioxidant enzyme expression.

## Role in Catecholamine Oxidation

Catecholamines ([[Epinephrine]], [[Norepinephrine]], [[Dopamine]]) are themselves prone to [[Autoxidation]] under oxidative stress. The sequence proceeds as:
1. Catechol → [[o-Semiquinone]] → [[o-Quinone]] (via [[Cytochrome c]] or metal-catalyzed oxidation).
2. o-Quinone undergoes cyclization to form indoline-5,6-dione ([[Aminochrome]]).
3. Aminochrome polymerizes to [[Melanins]] or undergoes [[Redox Cycling]], generating O₂⁻•.
4. O₂⁻• dismutates to H₂O₂, which can undergo [[Fenton chemistry]] to produce •OH.
This cascade establishes a feed-forward loop: oxidative stress drives catecholamine oxidation, which in turn amplifies oxidative stress.

## The "Two Faces" of ROS

### ROS as Damage Agents

Supraphysiological ROS directly damage:
- **[[Lipids]]**: Peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids ([[PUFAs]]) in membranes, generating reactive aldehydes ([[4-HNE]], [[MDA]]).
- **[[Proteins]]**: Carbonylation, nitration (tyrosine), sulfoxidation (methionine), and disulfide crosslinking.
- **[[DNA]]**: Base oxidation ([[8-oxo-dG]]), strand breaks, and abasic sites.

### ROS as Signaling Molecules

At physiological concentrations, H₂O₂ functions as a second messenger:
- Reversible oxidation of active-site cysteine residues in [[Protein tyrosine phosphatases]] (PTPs) regulates [[Receptor tyrosine kinase]] signaling.
- [[Peroxiredoxin]]-mediated H₂O₂ relay enables targeted oxidation of specific signaling proteins.
- [[Nrf2]]-dependent transcriptional programs respond to moderate ROS elevations.
- [[Mitohormesis]] is built entirely on adaptive ROS signaling.

## Hormetic and Pathophysiological Thresholds

The transition from signaling to damage is governed by redox thresholds:
- **Basal (10–100 nM H₂O₂)**: Homeostatic signaling; [[Peroxiredoxin]] and [[Glutathione peroxidase]] maintain set point.
- **Adaptive (100–500 nM H₂O₂)**: [[Nrf2]] activation, [[FOXO]] induction, [[Mitochondrial biogenesis]].
- **Stress (500 nM–1 μM H₂O₂)**: [[JNK]] and [[p38]] activation, [[Apoptosis]] priming.
- **Toxic (>1 μM H₂O₂)**: [[Cell necrosis]], [[mPTP]] opening, [[PARP]] hyperactivation.

These thresholds are cell-type and context dependent, influenced by baseline [[NADPH]]/[[NADP⁺]] ratios, [[Glutathione]] levels, and [[Thioredoxin]] reductase activity.
