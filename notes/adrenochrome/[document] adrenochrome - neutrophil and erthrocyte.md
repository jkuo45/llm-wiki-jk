where is [[Adrenochrome]] found - type of cells?

[[Adrenochrome]] is primarily produced in the body through the oxidation of [[Epinephrine|adrenaline (epinephrine)]], a hormone synthesized and released mainly by the [[Adrenal medulla]]. It is not stored in large quantities but forms as a metabolite or intermediate, especially under conditions involving [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] or [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species (ROS)]].

[acs.org](https://www.acs.org/molecule-of-the-week/archive/a/adrenochrome.html)

Main Sources and Cell/Tissue Types

- [[Adrenal medulla]] [[Chromaffin cells]]: These cells produce and store [[Epinephrine]]. Oxidation of [[Epinephrine]] (e.g., upon release or exposure to air/oxidants) can form [[Adrenochrome]] here. This is the classic biological source, noted since the 19th century when extracts from suprarenal (adrenal) glands turned reddish due to oxidation.
	[britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/science/adrenochrome)
- [[Neutrophils|Polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils/PMNs)]]: These immune cells actively oxidize [[Epinephrine|adrenaline]] to [[Adrenochrome]] via [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS (oxygen radicals)]] they produce, especially during [[Inflammation]]. This is described as a major pathway for [[Adrenaline catabolism]] in these cells.
	[pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2991537/)
- [[Cardiomyocytes|Cardiac cells (cardiomyocytes)]]: [[Epinephrine|Adrenaline]] oxidation to [[Adrenochrome]] has been observed in isolated rat [[Cardiomyocytes]], linked to potential [[Cardiotoxicity|cardiotoxic]] effects under stress or high [[Catecholamines|catecholamine]] levels.
	[pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/tx7000916)
- Brain ([[Neuromelanin]] pathway): [[Adrenochrome]] (or related [[Aminochromes]]) can form as an intermediate in [[Neuromelanin]] synthesis, particularly in [[Dopaminergic neurons]] (e.g., in the [[Substantia nigra]]). This is a normal physiological process, though excess may relate to oxidative damage.
	[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrenochrome)
- Other tissues: It can form wherever [[Epinephrine]] or [[Norepinephrine]] is present and oxidized (e.g., [[Sympathetic nerve endings]], [[Endothelial cells]], or inflamed tissues like [[Rheumatoid synovial fluid]]). It has been studied in contexts like [[Endothelial cells]] and under oxidative conditions.
	[sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/adrenochrome)

Key Context

[[Adrenochrome]] forms via both enzymatic and non-enzymatic oxidation (often involving [[Superoxide anion|superoxide radicals]]). It is unstable and can participate in [[Redox Cycling|redox cycling]], generating more [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]]. While it occurs endogenously in small amounts, it is not a primary "stored" molecule—most research focuses on its formation as a byproduct rather than a functional compound.

[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrenochrome)

Note: Much online discussion around [[Adrenochrome]] involves conspiracy theories (e.g., harvesting from living people), which lack scientific basis. It can be synthesized chemically in labs and is available for research; the body produces it naturally in trace amounts as described above.

is it found in [[Erythrocytes|erthroctye, rbc]]?

No, [[Adrenochrome]] is not typically "found" or stored as a normal component inside [[Erythrocytes|erythrocytes (red blood cells, RBCs)]]. However, there are some interactions and formation pathways involving [[Erythrocytes|RBCs]] or blood components.

Relevant Scientific Findings

- Limited formation/oxidation in blood: [[Epinephrine|Adrenaline (epinephrine)]] can be oxidized to [[Adrenochrome]] in blood, and [[Hemoglobin]] (abundant in [[Erythrocytes|RBCs]]) can catalyze this [[Peroxidation|peroxidation]]/oxidation process. This is more of a chemical reaction in the presence of oxygen or radicals rather than active production by [[Erythrocytes|RBCs]] themselves.
	[jbc.org](https://www.jbc.org/article/S0021-9258\(19\)44679-6/pdf)
- [[Erythrocytes|Erythrocyte]] [[Plasma membrane]] involvement: Studies have shown that the human [[Erythrocytes|erythrocyte]] [[Plasma membrane]] can convert [[Epinephrine|adrenaline]] to [[Aminochromes]] (including [[Adrenochrome]] and further derivatives) in a two-phase reaction.
	[pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8937691/)
- Effects on [[Erythrocytes|RBCs]] ([[Hemolysis]]): [[Adrenochrome]] can induce or promote [[Hemolysis]] (rupture of red blood cells) under certain experimental conditions. Older research (often from the 1960s–1970s, linked to outdated [[Adrenochrome Hypothesis|schizophrenia hypotheses]]) explored whether [[Erythrocytes|RBCs]] from some patients were more susceptible to [[Adrenochrome]]-induced lysis. Results were mixed and not strongly replicated.
	[link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00342246)
- No primary role: [[Erythrocytes]] lack the organelles and major oxidative enzymes (like those in [[Neutrophils]] or adrenal cells) that actively produce [[Adrenochrome]]. Any presence would be trace, transient, or from external oxidation in blood plasma.

Summary

[[Adrenochrome]] forms mainly via oxidation of [[Epinephrine|adrenaline]] in places like [[Adrenal medulla|adrenal]] [[Chromaffin cells]], [[Neutrophils]], or under [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] elsewhere. In blood/[[Erythrocytes|RBCs]], it is more relevant as a potential reactant or byproduct that can damage [[Erythrocytes|RBC]] membranes rather than a molecule normally present or produced inside healthy [[Erythrocytes|erythrocytes]].

[pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4505137/)

These findings come from older biochemical studies; modern research on [[Adrenochrome]] is limited outside of its chemistry and minor physiological roles. Conspiracy claims about it being harvested from blood are not supported by science.
