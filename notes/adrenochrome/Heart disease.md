# Heart disease

## Overview

Heart disease is a broad term encompassing a range of conditions that affect the structure and function of the heart. It is the leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually according to the World Health Organization. The term spans disorders of the coronary arteries, heart muscle, valves, electrical conduction system, and pericardium, each with distinct etiologies and clinical presentations.

## Types

- **Coronary artery disease (CAD)** — the most prevalent form, caused by [[Atherosclerosis]] of the coronary arteries. CAD manifests as stable angina, unstable angina, and [[Myocardial infarction]] (heart attack).
- **Heart failure** — a chronic condition in which the heart cannot pump sufficient blood to meet metabolic demands. May result from systolic dysfunction (reduced ejection fraction) or diastolic dysfunction (preserved ejection fraction).
- **Valvular heart disease** — stenosis or regurgitation of the mitral, aortic, tricuspid, or pulmonary valves, often from degenerative calcification, rheumatic fever, or congenital malformation.
- **Arrhythmias** — disorders of cardiac electrical activity, including atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, bradyarrhythmias, and conduction blocks.
- **Cardiomyopathies** — diseases of the heart muscle, classified as dilated, hypertrophic, restrictive, or arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy.
- **Congenital heart disease** — structural abnormalities present at birth, such as ventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, and coarctation of the aorta.
- **Pericarditis** — inflammation of the pericardium, which may be infectious, autoimmune, or post-infarction (Dressler syndrome).

## Risk Factors

**Non-modifiable:** Increasing age, male sex, and genetic predisposition (family history of premature CAD, specific variants in lipid metabolism genes).

**Modifiable:** Cigarette smoking, [[Hypertension]], dyslipidemia (elevated LDL, low HDL, high triglycerides), [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]], obesity, physical inactivity, atherogenic diet, excessive alcohol consumption, and chronic stress. These risk factors frequently cluster in a condition known as **metabolic syndrome**, defined by the presence of three or more of: abdominal obesity, elevated triglycerides, low HDL, hypertension, and elevated fasting glucose.

Emerging risk factors include chronic systemic [[Inflammation]] (elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein), sleep apnea, air pollution exposure, and psychosocial factors such as depression and social isolation.

## Pathophysiology

The central mechanism underlying most cardiovascular events is [[Atherosclerosis]]. The process begins with **endothelial injury** caused by hemodynamic stress (particularly at arterial bifurcations), hyperlipidemia, hypertension, toxins from cigarette smoke, and inflammatory cytokines. Injured endothelium becomes permeable to circulating low-density lipoproteins (LDL), which infiltrate the intima and undergo oxidative modification.

Oxidized LDL triggers an inflammatory response: endothelial cells express adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, ICAM-1) that recruit circulating monocytes. Monocytes differentiate into macrophages that engulf oxidized LDL via scavenger receptors, transforming into **foam cells**. Foam cell accumulation produces fatty streaks — the earliest visible atherosclerotic lesions.

Smooth muscle cells migrate from the media into the intima, proliferate, and secrete extracellular matrix components (collagen, proteoglycans), forming a **fibrous cap** over the lipid-rich necrotic core. Advanced plaques may become unstable through degradation of the fibrous cap by matrix metalloproteinases secreted by activated macrophages. **Plaque rupture** exposes thrombogenic core material to the bloodstream, triggering platelet aggregation and **thrombosis** — the proximate cause of most acute coronary syndromes (unstable angina, myocardial infarction).

## Catecholamine–Heart Connection

The autonomic nervous system tightly regulates cardiac function through catecholamines ([[Epinephrine]], [[Norepinephrine]]) released from sympathetic nerve terminals and the [[Adrenal gland]]. Catecholamines bind β1-adrenergic receptors on [[Cardiomyocytes]], increasing heart rate (chronotropy) and contractility (inotropy), which elevates myocardial oxygen demand.

While acute catecholamine surges are adaptive (fight-or-flight), chronic sympathetic overactivation is maladaptive. Sustained elevation of circulating catecholamines causes:

- Direct [[Oxidative Stress]] via auto-oxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction in [[Cardiomyocytes]].
- Calcium overload and apoptosis.
- Cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, ultimately leading to cardiomyopathic remodeling (catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy).

The adrenochrome hypothesis proposes that oxidation products of catecholamines, such as [[Adrenochrome]], may contribute to myocardial oxidative injury. Adrenochrome can undergo redox cycling, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage cellular lipids, proteins, and DNA. Some studies have detected adrenochrome-like oxidation products in cardiac tissue under conditions of catecholamine excess, though direct causal evidence in human heart disease remains limited. This intersection of catecholamine biochemistry and cardiac pathology represents an active area of investigation in understanding non-ischeamic contributors to heart failure and arrhythmogenesis.

## Prevention

**Primary prevention** focuses on reducing the lifetime risk of developing clinical heart disease in asymptomatic individuals. Lifestyle interventions include Mediterranean or DASH dietary patterns, ≥150 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity, smoking cessation, and maintaining a body mass index below 25 kg/m². Pharmacological strategies include statin therapy for dyslipidemia (LDL targets stratified by risk category), antihypertensive agents (target <130/80 mmHg), and low-dose aspirin only in select high-risk patients without bleeding risk (current guidelines recommend against routine aspirin for primary prevention in most older adults).

**Secondary prevention** after a cardiovascular event (e.g., myocardial infarction, revascularization) is more aggressive: high-intensity statins, dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus P2Y12 inhibitor for 6–12 months post-event), β-blockers, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Cardiac rehabilitation programs combining supervised exercise, risk factor management, and psychosocial support significantly reduce mortality.

## Connections

- [[Myocardial infarction]] — acute thrombosis of a coronary artery causing irreversible cardiomyocyte death; the most critical acute manifestation of heart disease.
- [[Arrhythmias]] — disordered electrical activity ranging from benign ectopy to lethal ventricular tachyarrhythmias; a common complication of ischaemic and structural heart disease.
- [[Heart failure]] — the clinical syndrome of inadequate cardiac output; a common end-stage pathway for most forms of heart disease.
- [[Atherosclerosis]] — the underlying pathological process in the majority of cardiovascular disease cases.
- [[Adrenochrome]] — an oxidation product of epinephrine implicated in catecholamine-induced cardiac oxidative stress.
- [[Coronary artery constriction]] — vasospastic component of ischaemia, distinct from fixed atherosclerotic obstruction.
- [[Cardiomyocytes]] — the contractile cells of the heart; their health is central to all forms of cardiac pathology.
