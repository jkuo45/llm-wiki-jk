# Cardiac arrest

**Cardiac arrest** is the sudden cessation of effective cardiac pumping, leading to absent blood flow, loss of consciousness, and death if untreated. It is a leading cause of death in the United States, with approximately 350,000 out-of-hospital cases annually. Time-critical: each minute without intervention decreases survival by 7–10%. Causes are broadly classified as electrical, including [[Ventricular Fibrillation]], pulseless [[Arrhythmias|Ventricular Tachycardia]], [[Asystole]], and [[PEA]], or mechanical (pump failure).

## Pathophysiology

Cardiac arrest represents an abrupt transition from organized rhythm to [[Ventricular Fibrillation]] or [[Asystole]]. Global [[Myocardial Ischemia]] triggers rapid [[ATP]] depletion, metabolic [[Acidosis]], and intracellular Ca²⁺ overload, impairing contractile function and promoting electrical instability. Upon return of spontaneous circulation ([[ROSC]]), [[Reperfusion Injury]] exacerbates tissue damage through oxidative stress, mitochondrial permeability transition, and [[NLRP3 Inflammasome]] activation. The post-cardiac arrest syndrome comprises three interrelated components: [[Myocardium|myocardial]] stunning (reversible left ventricular dysfunction), anoxic brain injury, and a systemic inflammatory response resembling [[Sepsis]], driven by cytokine release and [[Neutrophils|neutrophil]] activation.

## Etiology

The differential diagnosis of cardiac arrest is organized around the "H's and T's" mnemonic:

- **H's**: [[Hypoxia]], [[Hypovolemia]], Hydrogen ion ([[Acidosis]]), [[Hypokalemia]]/[[Hyperkalemia]], [[Hypothermia]]
- **T's**: [[Tension Pneumothorax]], [[Cardiac Tamponade]], [[Toxins]] (e.g., drug overdose, [[Sodium nitrite]], [[Methemoglobinemia|methemoglobin-inducing agents]]), Thrombosis ([[Myocardial infarction|coronary MI]]), Thrombosis ([[Pulmonary Embolism]])

[[Myocardial infarction|Acute coronary syndrome]] accounts for the majority of sudden cardiac arrest cases, particularly those presenting with shockable rhythms. Non-ischemic cardiomyopathies, channelopathies (e.g., long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome), and structural heart disease comprise the remainder.

## Treatment

Effective management follows the [[Chain of Survival]]: early recognition, early [[CPR]] (30:2 compression-to-ventilation ratio), early [[Defibrillation]], advanced life support, and post-arrest care.

**Airway and Breathing**: Bag-mask ventilation, supraglottic airway devices, or [[Intubation|endotracheal intubation]]. Capnography confirms tube placement and monitors CPR quality.

**Circulation**: High-quality chest compressions at 100–120/min, depth 5–6 cm, with minimal interruptions. [[IV|IV]]/[[IO]] access for drug delivery.

**Defibrillation**: For shockable rhythms ([[Ventricular Fibrillation]], pulseless [[Arrhythmias|VT]]), deliver biphasic defibrillation at 120–200 J. [[Epinephrine]] 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 minutes increases coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure and improves [[ROSC]] rates, but does not improve favorable neurological survival. The dose–response relationship remains debated — high-dose [[Epinephrine]] showed no benefit in randomized trials (Brown et al., 1992; Gueugniaud et al., *NEJM* 1998). [[Amiodarone]] (300 mg IV, then 150 mg) or [[Lidocaine]] (1–1.5 mg/kg) is given for shock-refractory VF/pulseless VT. [[Atropine]] is no longer recommended for routine use in [[PEA]] or [[Asystole]].

**Post-ROSC Care**: [[Targeted Temperature Management]] (TTM, 32–36°C for ≥24 hours) reduces neurological injury. Urgent [[PCI|percutaneous coronary intervention]] is indicated for [[STEMI|ST-elevation MI]] or high suspicion of coronary occlusion. Intensive care includes hemodynamic optimization, seizure control, and avoidance of hyperoxia and hyperglycemia.

## Use of Epinephrine

[[Epinephrine]] is a standard medication used during resuscitation for cardiac arrest to increase coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. Its alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictor effect augments aortic diastolic pressure, improving coronary blood flow during chest compressions. However, beta-adrenergic effects may increase myocardial oxygen demand, impair subendocardial perfusion, and exacerbate post-ROSC myocardial stunning. Despite improved [[ROSC]] rates, no controlled trial has demonstrated a survival benefit with favorable neurological outcome, spurring ongoing investigation into alternative vasopressors (e.g., vasopressin, [[Methylene blue]]) and dose-optimization strategies.

## Prognosis

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest yields [[ROSC]] in 30–40% of cases, with survival to hospital discharge at approximately 10%. Predictors of favorable outcome include initial shockable rhythm ([[Ventricular Fibrillation]]/pulseless VT), witnessed arrest, bystander [[CPR]], and short time to [[Defibrillation]]. Neurological recovery is assessed using the [[Cerebral Performance Category]] (CPC) scale. Poor prognostic indicators: prolonged no-flow time, non-shockable initial rhythm, refractory shock, and bilateral absence of pupillary/corneal reflexes at 72 hours post-arrest.
