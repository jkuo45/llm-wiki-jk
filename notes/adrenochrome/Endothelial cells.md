# Endothelial cells

**Endothelial cells** (ECs) form the innermost layer of all blood vessels and lymphatic vessels, constituting a simple squamous epithelium that lines the entire circulatory system. In an adult human, approximately 1 trillion endothelial cells weigh roughly 1 kg and cover a surface area of 4000–7000 m². Far from being an inert barrier, the endothelium is a highly active metabolic tissue that dynamically regulates vascular tone, hemostasis, inflammation, and angiogenesis.

## Structure

Endothelial cells exhibit structural heterogeneity adapted to the needs of the underlying tissue. Three major types are recognized:

- **Continuous endothelium:** Found in arteries, veins, and capillaries of the brain (forming the [[Blood-Brain Barrier]]), skeletal muscle, and lungs. Cells are connected by tight junctions that restrict paracellular transport, and the basement membrane is continuous.
- **Fenestrated endothelium:** Present in endocrine glands, renal glomeruli, and intestinal villi. Cells contain transcellular pores (fenestrae, 50–80 nm) closed by a diaphragm, allowing rapid exchange of small solutes.
- **Sinusoidal (discontinuous) endothelium:** Located in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. Cells have large gaps (100–200 nm) and a discontinuous or absent basement membrane, permitting passage of cells and large macromolecules.

The luminal surface is coated with the **glycocalyx**, a meshwork of membrane-bound proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and glycosaminoglycans (primarily heparan sulfate) that forms the first interface between blood and the vessel wall. This layer is critical for mechanotransduction of shear stress, thromboresistance, and regulation of vascular permeability. Adjacent endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions (claudins, occludins, JAMs) and adherens junctions (VE-cadherin), which together maintain barrier integrity. ECs also contain **Weibel-Palade bodies** — rod-shaped secretory organelles that store von Willebrand factor (vWF) and P-selectin for rapid release upon activation.

## Functions

### Barrier function and selective permeability
The endothelium provides a semi-permeable barrier that regulates the passage of fluids, solutes, and cells between blood and the interstitial space. Paracellular and transcellular pathways are tightly controlled by junctional complexes, caveolae-mediated transcytosis, and the glycocalyx.

### Thromboresistance
Under resting conditions, endothelial cells actively resist thrombus formation through the expression of anticoagulant molecules, including thrombomodulin, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, [[Nitric Oxide]] (NO), and prostacyclin (PGI₂). They also present [[Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor]] and the [[Protein C]] receptor on their surface.

### Regulation of vascular tone
Endothelial cells are the primary sensors and regulators of vascular diameter. They produce and release several vasoactive mediators:
- **[[Nitric Oxide]]** (NO): Synthesized by endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) in response to shear stress, acetylcholine, and bradykinin. NO diffuses to underlying vascular smooth muscle, activates guanylyl cyclase, and induces relaxation.
- **[[Endothelin-1]]** (ET-1): A potent vasoconstrictor peptide produced by ECs that acts on smooth muscle ETA receptors.
- **Prostacyclin** (PGI₂): A vasodilator prostanoid that also inhibits platelet aggregation.

The balance between NO and ET-1 determines basal vascular tone and is a key determinant of vascular health.

### Angiogenesis
Endothelial cells are the central cellular effectors of angiogenesis — the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones. In response to [[Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor]] (VEGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and angiopoietins, ECs proliferate, migrate, and form tubular structures. This process is essential for development, wound healing, and the female reproductive cycle, and is pathologically co-opted in tumor growth and diabetic retinopathy.

### Inflammation and leukocyte recruitment
Endothelial cells orchestrate the inflammatory cascade by regulating leukocyte adhesion, rolling, and transmigration. Upon activation by cytokines (e.g., [[Tumor Necrosis Factor α]], [[Interleukin 1β]]), ECs rapidly upregulate:
- **P-selectin** (from Weibel-Palade bodies) and **E-selectin**: Mediate leukocyte rolling.
- **[[ICAM-1]]** (Intercellular Adhesion Molecule 1) and **[[VCAM-1]]** (Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule 1): Facilitate firm adhesion and transendothelial migration.

This multi-step adhesion cascade is the gateway to tissue inflammation and is central to both protective immunity and chronic inflammatory pathology.

## Pathology

### Endothelial dysfunction
Endothelial dysfunction is a systemic pathological state characterized by impaired NO bioavailability, often due to reduced eNOS activity, eNOS uncoupling, or NO scavenging by [[Superoxide anion]]. The hallmark consequence is impaired vasodilation, which contributes to:
- **[[Hypertension]]**: Reduced NO-dependent vasodilation increases systemic vascular resistance.
- **[[Atherosclerosis]]**: Endothelial dysfunction is the initiating event in atherogenesis. Loss of NO promotes [[Low-Density Lipoprotein]] oxidation, monocyte adhesion, foam cell formation, and smooth muscle proliferation.

### Glycocalyx degradation
The endothelial glycocalyx is degraded by [[Matrix Metalloproteinases]], [[Heparanase]], and [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] in conditions such as [[Sepsis]], [[Diabetes]], and [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury]]. Loss of glycocalyx integrity leads to increased vascular permeability, edema, and loss of shear-stress sensing, further compounding endothelial dysfunction.

### Endothelial activation in inflammation
During systemic inflammation, endothelial cells become "activated" — expressing adhesion molecules, releasing vWF, and increasing permeability. While this is a normal host defense response, chronic or excessive activation contributes to vascular leak, multi-organ dysfunction, and tissue damage. In [[Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome]], pulmonary endothelial injury is the primary driver of proteinaceous pulmonary edema.

## Adrenochrome relevance

[[Adrenochrome]], the oxidized aminochrome derivative of [[Epinephrine]], has been shown to cause endothelial dysfunction through multiple mechanisms:

- **Reduced NO production:** Adrenochrome decreases eNOS activity and NO bioavailability in cultured endothelial cells, shifting the vasomotor balance toward constriction.
- **Induction of ROS:** Adrenochrome undergoes [[Redox Cycling]] within ECs, generating [[Superoxide anion]] and other reactive species that consume NO and cause oxidative damage to cellular components.
- **Increased adhesion molecule expression:** Exposure to adrenochrome upregulates [[ICAM-1]] expression on endothelial surfaces, promoting leukocyte adhesion and exacerbating vascular inflammation.
- **Microvascular constriction:** In isolated vessel preparations and in vivo models, adrenochrome promotes sustained constriction of resistance arteries, an effect that can be partially blocked by antioxidants.
- **Catecholamine oxidation products:** Beyond adrenochrome, other catecholamine oxidation products (e.g., [[Adrenolutin]], aminochrome-melanin) similarly impair endothelial function through oxidative and inflammatory pathways.

These findings position adrenochrome as a potential contributor to the vascular pathology observed in conditions of high sympathetic drive and oxidative stress, including [[Septic Shock]], [[Preeclampsia]], [[Myocardial infarction]], and [[Heart disease]].
