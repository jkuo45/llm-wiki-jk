# Arrhythmias

Arrhythmias are disorders of cardiac rhythm defined by abnormalities in heart rate, regularity, or impulse origin. Tachyarrhythmias exceed 100 beats per minute (bpm), bradyarrhythmias fall below 60 bpm, and irregular rhythms lack a consistent beat-to-beat interval. The underlying mechanisms fall into three broad categories: **abnormal automaticity** (ectopic pacemaker foci firing faster than or suppressing the [[SA Node|sinoatrial node]]), **triggered activity** arising from early or delayed afterdepolarizations (EADs, DADs), and **reentry** — self-sustaining circuits of excitation around anatomical or functional conduction block. Clinically important subtypes include [[Atrial Fibrillation|atrial fibrillation]] (AF — the most common sustained arrhythmia), [[Atrial Flutter|atrial flutter]], [[Supraventricular Tachycardia|supraventricular tachycardia]] (SVT), [[Ventricular Tachycardia|ventricular tachycardia]] (VT), [[Ventricular Fibrillation|ventricular fibrillation]] (VF — a lethal rhythm that causes sudden cardiac arrest), bradyarrhythmias such as [[Sick Sinus Syndrome|sick sinus syndrome]] and [[Heart Block|heart block]], and premature beats ([[Premature Atrial Contractions|PACs]], [[Premature Ventricular Contractions|PVCs]]).

## Mechanisms

**Reentry** is the most common mechanism of clinically significant tachyarrhythmias. It requires a substrate with two functionally distinct pathways differing in refractoriness and conduction velocity, a unidirectional block in one pathway, and a critical timing delay that allows the circulating wavefront to re-excite tissue just beyond the block. Typical reentry circuits underlie [[Atrial Fibrillation|AF]] (multiple wavelet reentry in the atria), [[Atrial Flutter|atrial flutter]] (a macroreentrant circuit in the right atrium), and scar-related [[Ventricular Tachycardia|VT]] (reentry around infarct border zones).

**Abnormal automaticity** occurs when non-pacemaker myocardial cells or latent pacemaker cells (e.g., in the atria, [[AV Node|AV junction]], or [[Purkinje Fibers|Purkinje fibers]]) depolarize spontaneously at a rate exceeding the [[SA Node|SA node]]. Enhanced automaticity is implicated in some forms of [[Atrial Tachycardia|atrial tachycardia]] and accelerated idioventricular rhythms.

**Triggered activity** arises from afterdepolarizations that reach threshold potential. **Early afterdepolarizations (EADs)** occur during phase 2 or 3 of the action potential, typically when repolarization is prolonged (as seen in long QT syndrome). EADs trigger [[Torsades de Pointes|torsades de pointes]], a polymorphic VT. **Delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs)** occur after full repolarization and are caused by intracellular [[Calcium Ions|calcium]] overload — common in [[Heart Failure|heart failure]] and digitalis toxicity — that activates the [[Sodium-Calcium Exchanger|Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger]], generating a transient inward current.

**Conduction block** manifests as [[SA Node|SA node]] dysfunction (sinus arrest, chronotropic incompetence) or AV block. First-degree AV block shows PR interval prolongation (>200 ms). Second-degree block includes Mobitz type I (Wenckebach, progressive PR lengthening then a dropped beat) and Mobitz type II (sudden dropped beats with constant PR). Third-degree (complete) heart block is total [[AV Node|AV]] dissociation, with atria and ventricles driven by independent pacemakers.

## Etiology & Risk Factors

Arrhythmias arise from a wide range of cardiac and systemic insults. **Structural heart disease** underlies most ventricular arrhythmias: [[Coronary Artery Disease|coronary artery disease]] and [[Myocardial Infarction|myocardial infarction]] create reentry substrates via scar formation; [[Heart Failure|heart failure]] and [[Cardiomyopathy|cardiomyopathies]] (dilated, hypertrophic, arrhythmogenic right ventricular) predispose to VT and VF. [[Hypertension|Hypertension]] causes left ventricular hypertrophy and atrial stretch, promoting AF. [[Valvular Heart Disease|Valvular disease]], especially [[Mitral Stenosis|mitral stenosis]] and [[Mitral Regurgitation|regurgitation]], chronically distends the atria.

**Electrolyte abnormalities** critically modulate membrane excitability: [[Hypokalemia|hypokalemia]] and [[Hypomagnesemia|hypomagnesemia]] increase EAD risk and potentiate digitalis-induced arrhythmias; [[Hyperkalemia|hyperkalemia]] produces progressive ECG changes (peaked T waves, widened QRS, sine-wave pattern culminating in VF/asystole); [[Hypocalcemia|hypocalcemia]] lengthens the ST segment. [[Myocardial Ischemia|Ischemia]] shortens refractoriness, slows conduction, and generates injury currents that trigger ectopy.

**Drugs and exogenous substances** include [[Sympathomimetics|sympathomimetics]] (cocaine, amphetamines), certain [[Antiarrhythmic Agents|antiarrhythmics]] (especially class IA and III drugs that prolong QT), [[Antibiotics|antibiotics]] (macrolides, fluoroquinolones), and [[Antipsychotics|antipsychotics]]. [[Hyperthyroidism|Hyperthyroidism]] increases automaticity and shortens atrial refractoriness, precipitating AF. [[Caffeine|Caffeine]] and [[Alcohol|alcohol]] are common triggers, with holiday heart syndrome describing AF after alcohol binges.

**Catecholamine excess** — from stress, exercise, or [[Pheochromocytoma|pheochromocytoma]] — powerfully stimulates β-adrenergic receptors, enhancing [[L-Type Calcium Channel|L-type calcium channel]] current and [[Ryanodine Receptor|ryanodine receptor]]-mediated SR calcium release, which promotes DADs and triggered activity. This is particularly relevant to [[Adrenochrome]]: the oxidative byproduct of excessive [[Catecholamines|catecholamines]] (especially [[Epinephrine|epinephrine]]) may contribute to cardiotoxicity and arrhythmia susceptibility through [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species]] generation and direct [[Adrenochrome|adrenochrome]]-mediated myocardial damage.

## Clinical & ECG

- **[[Atrial Fibrillation]]**: Irregularly irregular R-R intervals; absent P waves replaced by coarse or fine fibrillatory waves; narrow QRS unless pre-existing bundle branch block. Ventricular rates typically 100–160 bpm when uncontrolled.
- **[[Atrial Flutter]]**: Sawtooth flutter waves at ~300/min (atrial rate), with variable AV conduction (most commonly 2:1, yielding a ventricular rate of ~150 bpm). Carotid sinus massage or [[Adenosine|adenosine]] transiently slows AV conduction to reveal flutter waves.
- **[[Ventricular Tachycardia]]**: Wide QRS (≥120 ms) at ≥100 bpm; [[AV Dissociation|AV dissociation]] (independent P waves marching through) is a diagnostic hallmark. Monomorphic VT suggests scar-related reentry; polymorphic VT suggests ischemia, electrolyte derangement, or channelopathy.
- **[[Ventricular Fibrillation]]**: Chaotic, undulating baseline with no identifiable QRS complexes; no coordinated myocardial contraction — requires immediate [[Defibrillation|defibrillation]].
- **[[Heart Block]]**: First-degree: PR >200 ms. Second-degree Mobitz I: progressive PR prolongation then dropped QRS. Mobitz II: stable but prolonged PR with intermittent non-conducted P waves (often progresses to complete block). Third-degree: complete [[AV Dissociation|AV dissociation]] with an escape rhythm (narrow junctional at 40–60 bpm or wide ventricular at 20–40 bpm).
- **[[Premature Ventricular Contractions]]**: Early, wide QRS with a full compensatory pause. Complexes in bigeminy, trigeminy, couplets, or runs (non-sustained VT if ≥3 beats).
- **[[Long QT Syndrome]]**: QTc >470 ms (men) or >480 ms (women); predisposes to [[Torsades de Pointes|torsades de pointes]], especially with triggers such as bradycardia, hypokalemia, or QT-prolonging drugs.

## Treatment

The management of arrhythmias encompasses antiarrhythmic drugs, catheter ablation, device therapy, and treatment of underlying causes. The **Vaughan Williams classification** groups antiarrhythmics by mechanism: **Class I** ([[Sodium Channel Blockers|sodium channel blockers]] — e.g., [[Lidocaine|lidocaine]], [[Flecainide|flecainide]], [[Procainamide|procainamide]]), **Class II** ([[Beta Blockers|beta-blockers]] — e.g., [[Metoprolol|metoprolol]], [[Propranolol|propranolol]]), **Class III** ([[Potassium Channel Blockers|potassium channel blockers]] — e.g., [[Amiodarone|amiodarone]], [[Sotalol|sotalol]]), and **Class IV** ([[Calcium Channel Blockers|calcium channel blockers]] — e.g., [[Verapamil|verapamil]], [[Diltiazem|diltiazem]]).

For **[[Atrial Fibrillation]]**, a rate-control strategy (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, [[Digoxin|digoxin]]) is often first-line; rhythm control with [[Amiodarone|amiodarone]], [[Flecainide|flecainide]], or [[Propafenone|propafenone]] is selected for symptomatic patients. Thromboembolic prophylaxis with direct oral anticoagulants ([[DOACs|DOACs]] — [[Apixaban|apixaban]], [[Rivaroxaban|rivaroxaban]], [[Edoxaban|edoxaban]], [[Dabigatran|dabigatran]]) or warfarin is essential ([[CHA2DS2-VASc|CHA₂DS₂-VASc]] score guides decision-making).

For **[[Ventricular Tachycardia]]** and **[[Ventricular Fibrillation]]**, an [[Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator|ICD]] is standard for secondary prevention or high-risk primary prevention. Acute management of sustained VT: intravenous [[Amiodarone|amiodarone]] or [[Lidocaine|lidocaine]]; hemodynamically unstable VT/VF requires immediate [[Defibrillation|defibrillation]] per [[Advanced Cardiac Life Support|ACLS]] protocols. [[Catheter Ablation|Catheter ablation]] is effective for scar-related VT and for drug-refractory [[Atrial Fibrillation|AF]] (pulmonary vein isolation) and [[Atrial Flutter|atrial flutter]] (cavotricuspid isthmus ablation).

**Bradyarrhythmias** are treated with [[Pacemaker|permanent pacemaker]] implantation when symptomatic. Indications include [[Sick Sinus Syndrome|sick sinus syndrome]] with symptomatic pauses, third-degree AV block, Mobitz II second-degree block, and certain bradycardia-dependent tachyarrhythmias.

Emerging and adjunctive therapies include [[Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy|cardiac resynchronization therapy]] (CRT) for heart failure patients with wide QRS, [[Surgical Ablation|surgical]] (Maze) ablation for AF during concurrent cardiac surgery, and [[Neuromodulation|neuromodulatory]] approaches such as [[Stellate Ganglion Blockade|stellate ganglion blockade]] for refractory VT.
