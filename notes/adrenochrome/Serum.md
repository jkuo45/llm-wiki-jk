# Serum

Serum is the clear, pale-yellow liquid fraction of [[Blood]] that remains after coagulation, with a composition nearly identical to [[Plasma]] but lacking clotting factors such as [[Fibrinogen]], [[Prothrombin]], and [[Factor VIII]]. It constitutes approximately 50–55% of total [[Blood]] volume and serves as a versatile medium for transport, immune defense, and clinical diagnostics.

## Overview

Serum is obtained by allowing whole [[Blood]] to clot for 30–60 minutes at room temperature, then removing the clot by centrifugation (1,000–2,000 g for 10–15 minutes). The resulting supernatant is serum. Unlike [[Plasma]], which is prepared by adding anticoagulants (e.g., heparin, citrate, EDTA) to prevent clotting, serum retains the full complement of soluble factors released during coagulation, including [[Platelet]]-derived growth factors and proteolytic enzymes generated by the clotting cascade. The yield from whole [[Blood]] is approximately 45–55% of the original volume.

## Composition

Serum is an aqueous solution (≈91–92% water) containing a complex mixture of proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste products.

### Proteins

The total protein concentration of serum is approximately 60–80 mg/mL, with the two major fractions being:

- **[[Albumin]]** (~35–50 mg/mL): The most abundant serum protein, synthesized in the liver. It maintains [[Oncotic Pressure]], transports hydrophobic molecules (free fatty acids, bilirubin, drugs, thyroid hormones), and serves as an antioxidant via its free thiol group at Cys34.
- **Globulins** (~20–35 mg/mL): A heterogeneous group subdivided into α₁, α₂, β, and γ fractions. The γ-globulins correspond to immunoglobulins ([[Antibody|Antibodies]]), produced by [[B Cells|B lymphocytes]].

Additional proteins include complement factors (C3, C4), transferrin, haptoglobin, lipoproteins (HDL, LDL, VLDL), and acute-phase reactants such as [[C-Reactive Protein]] and serum amyloid A.

### Electrolytes

Serum electrolyte concentrations are tightly regulated:

### Other Components

Serum carries nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins), metabolic waste products ([[Urea]], [[Creatinine]], [[Bilirubin]]), hormones (thyroid hormones, cortisol, insulin, catecholamines), cytokines, growth factors, and circulating nucleic acids. It also contains numerous enzymes, including transaminases (ALT, AST), [[Alkaline Phosphatase]], [[Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase]] (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and [[Creatine Kinase]] (CK).

## Functions

### Transport and Carrier Function

Serum serves as the primary vehicle for distributing nutrients, hormones, and drugs throughout the body. [[Albumin]] and carrier globulins (e.g., [[Thyroid-Binding Globulin]], [[Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin]], [[Corticosteroid-Binding Globulin]]) solubilize and deliver hydrophobic cargo. Many therapeutic drugs circulate largely bound to serum proteins, which affects their pharmacokinetics, volume of distribution, and elimination half-life.

### Immune Defense

Serum contains [[Antibody|Antibodies]] (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE, IgD) that neutralize pathogens, opsonize targets for phagocytosis, and activate complement. The [[Complement System]] (classical, lectin, and alternative pathways) mediates direct lysis of microbes, opsonization, and chemotaxis. Serum immunoglobulins are the basis of humoral immunity and passive immunotherapy (e.g., intravenous immunoglobulin, or IVIG).

### pH Buffering

Serum bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻/CO₂ system) is the primary extracellular buffer, maintaining pH 7.35–7.45. [[Albumin]] and phosphate provide secondary buffering capacity. Imbalances manifest as metabolic alkalosis or acidosis, with characteristic changes in serum HCO₃⁻ and anion gap.

### Water and Electrolyte Balance

Serum composition reflects whole-body fluid and electrolyte homeostasis. The [[Kidneys]] regulate serum sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate concentrations through reabsorption, secretion, and excretion. Disturbances in serum electrolytes are common in renal disease, endocrine disorders, and diuretic therapy.

## Clinical Significance

### Diagnostic Biomarkers

Serum is the most widely used sample type in clinical laboratory medicine. Key biomarkers include:

- **[[Troponin I]] and [[Troponin T]]**: Cardiac-specific markers for [[Myocardial Infarction]] (MI) and myocardial injury.
- **[[C-Reactive Protein]] (CRP)**: Acute-phase reactant; elevated in [[Inflammation]] and infection.
- **[[Creatinine]] and [[Blood Urea Nitrogen]] (BUN)**: Markers of renal function; the BUN:creatinine ratio aids differential diagnosis of [[Acute Kidney Injury]].
- **[[Glucose]]**: Diagnostic for [[Diabetes Mellitus]] (fasting, postprandial, and HbA1c).
- **[[ALT]] (alanine transaminase)**, **[[AST]] (aspartate transaminase)**, **[[GGT]] (gamma-glutamyl transferase)**: Markers of [[Hepatocellular Injury]] and cholestasis.
- **[[Autoantibodies]]**: Antinuclear antibodies (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-[[TPO]], anti-[[tTG]] — serological hallmarks of autoimmune disease.
- **[[Tumor markers]]**: [[PSA]] (prostate), [[CA-125]] (ovarian), [[CEA]] (colorectal), [[AFP]] (hepatocellular), [[CA 19-9]] (pancreatic).

### Therapeutic Drug Monitoring

Serum drug-level monitoring is essential for drugs with narrow therapeutic indices:

- **[[Digoxin]]**: Therapeutic range 0.8–2.0 ng/mL; toxicity causes arrhythmias and neurological symptoms.
- **[[Lithium]]**: Therapeutic range 0.6–1.2 mM; toxicity at >1.5 mM causes tremor, renal impairment, and neurotoxicity.
- **[[Vancomycin]]**: Trough levels 10–20 µg/mL; nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity associated with supratherapeutic levels.
- Other monitored agents: [[Phenytoin]], [[Carbamazepine]], [[Valproic Acid]], [[Theophylline]], [[Cyclosporine]], [[Tacrolimus]].

### Serum vs. Plasma

The choice between serum and [[Plasma]] depends on the analyte. Serum is preferred for serological assays (immunology, virology), chemistry panels, and therapeutic drug monitoring. [[Plasma]] (collected with anticoagulant) is typically used for coagulation studies (PT, aPTT), [[Platelet]] function assays, and tests requiring immediate centrifugation to minimize cellular metabolism (e.g., [[Glucose]], [[Lactate]], [[Ammonia]]). Serum yields slightly higher volumes per unit [[Blood]] but requires clotting time, which may alter labile analytes such as [[Cytokines]] and [[Complement]].
