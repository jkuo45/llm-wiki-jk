# Cardiomyocytes

[[Cardiomyocytes]] are the contractile muscle cells of the [[Myocardium|myocardium]], responsible for generating the force of cardiac contractions. They are terminally differentiated, with limited regenerative capacity — adult human cardiomyocyte turnover is estimated at ~1% per year. The adult human heart contains approximately 2–4 billion cardiomyocytes, which occupy ~75% of the myocardial volume (though they constitute only ~30–40% of total cell number, with [[Fibroblasts|cardiac fibroblasts]] and endothelial cells comprising the remainder). Cardiomyocytes are connected end-to-end via [[Intercalated Discs|intercalated discs]], specialized membrane structures that contain gap junctions for electrical coupling and desmosomes for mechanical adhesion, enabling the synchronous contraction required for efficient pump function.

## Structure & Types

Three principal subtypes of cardiomyocytes exist, each adapted to its functional niche:

- **Atrial cardiomyocytes** — Smaller and thinner than ventricular cells, with less organized [[Sarcomere|sarcomeres]] and a less developed [[T-tubule|T-tubule system]]. They contain fewer [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and a less elaborate sarcoplasmic reticulum. Atrial cells also synthesize and secrete [[Atrial Natriuretic Peptide]] and [[B-type Natriuretic Peptide]], hormones that regulate blood volume and pressure.

- **Ventricular cardiomyocytes** — Large, rod-shaped cells (~100–150 μm long, ~20–30 μm wide) with highly organized sarcomeres, a well-developed T-tubule network that enables rapid [[Action Potential|action potential]] propagation into the cell interior, and abundant mitochondria that occupy 30–40% of cellular volume. The high mitochondrial density reflects the massive ATP demands of continuous contraction.

- **Conduction system cardiomyocytes** — Specialized myocytes forming the [[Sinoatrial Node|sinoatrial node]], [[Atrioventricular Node|atrioventricular node]], and [[Purkinje Fibers|Purkinje fibers]]. These cells are enriched in ion channels for automaticity and rapid impulse conduction but contain fewer contractile elements and mitochondria than working ventricular myocytes.

### Sarcomere Organization

The [[Sarcomere|sarcomere]] is the basic contractile unit, bounded by Z-discs and composed of interdigitating thick ([[Myosin|myosin]]) and thin ([[Actin|actin]]) filaments. [[Titin|Titin]] acts as a molecular spring that maintains sarcomere integrity and contributes to passive tension. The sliding filament theory — driven by myosin heads cycling along actin filaments under [[ATP|ATP]] hydrolysis — explains sarcomere shortening. Regulatory proteins [[Troponin|troponin]] (subunits C, I, T) and [[Tropomyosin|tropomyosin]] confer calcium sensitivity, forming the switch that couples electrical excitation to contraction.

## Excitation–Contraction Coupling

Excitation–contraction (E–C) coupling converts the electrical action potential into mechanical contraction. The process begins with depolarization opening [[L-type Calcium Channel|L-type calcium channels]] (Cav1.2) on the T-tubule membrane. The influx of Ca²⁺ triggers [[Ryanodine Receptor|ryanodine receptor type 2 (RyR2)]] on the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) to release stored Ca²⁺ — a mechanism termed calcium-induced calcium release (CICR). The resulting rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ (~100 nM to ~1 μM) binds [[Troponin C]], which induces a conformational shift that moves tropomyosin away from the myosin-binding sites on actin, permitting cross-bridge cycling and contraction. Relaxation occurs as the SR [[SERCA|SERCA2a pump]] resequesters Ca²⁺, aided by [[Sodium-Calcium Exchanger|NCX]] extrusion across the sarcolemma.

## Physiology

Cardiac performance is typically described by three regulatory axes:

- **Chronotropy** — Heart rate, determined primarily by the spontaneous depolarization rate of sinoatrial node cells, modulated by autonomic input ([[Sympathetic nervous system|sympathetic]] → acceleration via β1-adrenergic receptors; [[Parasympathetic nervous system|parasympathetic]] → deceleration via M2 muscarinic receptors).
- **Inotropy** — Contractile strength, regulated by the amplitude of the cytosolic Ca²⁺ transient and myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity. β-adrenergic stimulation enhances inotropy via [[Protein Kinase A|PKA]]-mediated phosphorylation of L-type Ca²⁺ channels, RyR2, phospholamban (which disinhibits SERCA2a), and troponin I.
- **Lusitropy** — Rate of relaxation, governed by SR Ca²⁺ reuptake and myofilament dissociation kinetics. β-adrenergic signaling accelerates lusitropy via phospholamban phosphorylation.

### Energy Metabolism

Cardiomyocytes have extraordinarily high energy demands, consuming ~30 kg of [[ATP]] daily. Under normal conditions, 60–90% of ATP derives from [[Fatty Acid Oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], with the remaining 10–40% from [[Glucose|glucose]] and [[Lactate|lactate]] oxidation. The heart has very limited glycolytic capacity and minimal glycogen stores, rendering it critically dependent on oxidative phosphorylation and vulnerable to [[Ischemia|ischemia]]. Mitochondria occupy up to 40% of cellular volume, arranged in highly ordered chains between myofibrils and beneath the sarcolemma. Substrate flexibility is a hallmark of the healthy heart, shifting toward greater glucose utilization under stress — a phenomenon that becomes maladaptive in pathological hypertrophy (the "metabolic remodeling" of [[Heart Failure|heart failure]]).

## Pathology

### Catecholamine-Mediated Cardiotoxicity

Excessive [[Catecholamines|catecholamines]] — whether from [[Stress|emotional or physiological stress]], [[Pheochromocytoma|pheochromocytoma]], or exogenous catecholamine infusion — produce well-characterized cardiotoxicity. The mechanisms include:

- **Ca²⁺ overload**: Sustained β-adrenergic activation drives excessive L-type Ca²⁺ current and RyR2-mediated SR Ca²⁺ leak, leading to cytosolic Ca²⁺ overload, mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uptake via the [[Mitochondrial Calcium Uniporter|mitochondrial calcium uniporter]], and opening of the [[Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore|mitochondrial permeability transition pore]].
- **ROS production**: Autoxidation of catecholamines generates [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species]] that impair mitochondrial respiration, oxidize sarcomeric proteins, and trigger [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] and [[Necrosis|necrosis]].
- **Contraction band necrosis**: A histopathological hallmark of catecholamine cardiotoxicity, characterized by hypercontracted sarcomeres and dense eosinophilic bands.

### Adrenochrome and Cardiomyocyte Injury

[[Adrenochrome]], an oxidation product of [[Epinephrine|epinephrine]], has been linked to direct cardiotoxicity in animal models. Adrenochrome generates superoxide via [[Redox Cycling|redox cycling]], inhibits mitochondrial complexes I and II, and depletes [[Glutathione|glutathione]]. In isolated cardiomyocyte preparations, adrenochrome prolongs the [[Action Potential|action potential]] by inhibiting delayed rectifier potassium currents (I_Kr) and L-type calcium channels, recapitulating features of [[Long QT Syndrome|long QT syndrome]]. This provides a mechanistic basis for the [[Arrhythmias|arrhythmias]] observed in catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy. However, the quantitative contribution of adrenochrome (versus direct catecholamine effects) in human cardiac pathology remains debated.

### Other Pathology

- **Hypertrophy**: Cardiomyocytes undergo hypertrophic growth in response to pressure overload (aortic stenosis, hypertension) or neurohumoral activation (angiotensin II, norepinephrine). Initially adaptive, sustained hypertrophy leads to diastolic dysfunction, fibrosis, and progression to heart failure.
- **Ischemia–Reperfusion Injury**: Acute [[Myocardial Infarction|myocardial infarction]] deprives cardiomyocytes of oxygen, causing ATP depletion, ion pump failure, and eventual necrosis. Paradoxically, reperfusion amplifies injury via a burst of ROS, [[Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore|mPTP opening]], and [[Calcium Overload|calcium overload]] — a phenomenon mitigated by ischemic preconditioning.
- **Fibrosis**: [[Cardiac Fibroblasts|Cardiac fibroblasts]] and immune cells secrete [[Transforming Growth Factor Beta|TGF-β]], driving extracellular matrix deposition that stiffens the myocardium and impairs both contraction and relaxation.

## Regeneration

Adult mammalian cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated, exiting the cell cycle shortly after birth. Unlike [[Zebrafish|zebrafish]] or neonatal mice (which regenerate heart tissue through cardiomyocyte proliferation for ~7 days post-birth), adult human cardiomyocytes are largely post-mitotic. Low-level turnover (~0.5–1% per year) occurs via division of pre-existing cardiomyocytes, insufficient to compensate for acute injury. Regenerative strategies under investigation include:

- **Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs)**: Transplanted as cell sheets or patches in preclinical models, showing electromechanical integration but limited long-term engraftment.
- **Cell cycle re-entry**: Forced expression of cyclins (Cyclin D2), CDKs, or microRNA manipulation to stimulate proliferation.
- **Cardiac reprogramming**: Direct conversion of cardiac [[Fibroblasts|fibroblasts]] into induced cardiomyocyte-like cells via transcription factor cocktails (GATA4, MEF2C, TBX5 — GMT factors).

## Connections

- [[Adrenochrome]]: Oxidation product of [[Epinephrine]] that causes redox cycling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and action potential prolongation in cardiomyocytes.
- [[Cardiotoxicity]]: Catecholamine excess and adrenochrome formation contribute to myocyte injury, contraction band necrosis, and arrhythmias.
- [[Epinephrine]]: Direct precursor of adrenochrome; β-adrenergic signaling regulates chronotropy, inotropy, and lusitropy in cardiomyocytes.
- [[Myocardium]]: The cardiac muscle tissue composed primarily of cardiomyocytes.
- [[Mitochondria]]: Abundant in ventricular cardiomyocytes (30–40% volume); central to energy metabolism and apoptosis regulation.
- [[Sarcomere]]: Basic contractile unit; dysregulation in hypertrophy and heart failure.
- [[Calcium]]: Central to excitation–contraction coupling; calcium overload underlies catecholamine cardiotoxicity.
- [[Heart Failure]]: End-stage consequence of pathological hypertrophy, fibrosis, and metabolic remodeling.
- [[Myocardial Infarction]]: Acute ischemic injury causing cardiomyocyte necrosis; reperfusion paradoxically amplifies damage.
- [[Arrhythmias]]: Adrenochrome prolongs action potential duration via I_Kr inhibition, contributing to long QT syndrome.
- [[Long QT Syndrome]]: Electrophysiological phenotype linked to adrenochrome-mediated potassium current inhibition.
- [[Regeneration]]: Limited adult cardiomyocyte turnover; iPSC-CM therapy and cell cycle re-entry as investigational approaches.
