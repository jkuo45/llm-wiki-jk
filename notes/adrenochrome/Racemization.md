# Racemization

**Racemization** is a chemical process in which one enantiomer of a compound is converted into an equal mixture of both enantiomers (a racemic mixture).

## Effect on Epinephrine
Pharmaceutical [[Epinephrine]] is typically the L-isomer. Over time, heat and pH changes can cause racemization to the D-isomer, which is significantly less biologically active, leading to a loss of therapeutic potency.

## Amino Acid Racemization in Aging Proteins

Beyond small-molecule racemization, amino acid residues within long-lived proteins undergo spontaneous, non-enzymatic racemization over the human lifespan. The reaction proceeds via abstraction of the α-proton adjacent to the carbonyl group, generating a planar carbanion intermediate that reprotonates from either face, yielding a mixture of L- and D-enantiomers. The rate of racemization depends on: (1) the steric and electronic properties of the amino acid side chain; (2) the local pH and temperature; (3) the protein's three-dimensional conformation, which can shield labile residues from solvent. [[Aspartic acid]] racemizes most rapidly (half-life ~15–30 years in human lens proteins at 37°C), followed by [[Serine]] and [[Alanine]]. Bulkier residues like [[Valine]] and [[Leucine]] racemize orders of magnitude more slowly.

## Aspartic Acid Racemization as a Molecular Clock

The accumulation of D-aspartic acid in metabolically inert tissues — particularly [[Tooth enamel]], [[Dentin]], [[Lens crystallins]], and [[Myelin]] — proceeds at a predictable rate and has been exploited as a molecular clock for age estimation in forensic science and archeology. The D/L aspartic acid ratio in human tooth dentin correlates with chronological age with a mean error of ±3–5 years. In the [[Lens]] of the eye, crystallin proteins synthesized before birth accumulate D-aspartate over decades, providing an independent assessment of biological age. The racemization rate constant (k_{rac}) at 37°C for aspartic acid in lens crystallins is approximately 1.5 × 10⁻³ year⁻¹.

## Connection to Glycation and Crosslinking

Racemization and [[Glycation]] are synergistic processes that compromise protein integrity with age. The introduction of D-amino acids disrupts local secondary structure (α-helices and β-sheets), increasing protein backbone flexibility and solvent exposure. This conformational loosening renders formerly buried residues more accessible to glycating agents ([[Glucose]], [[Methylglyoxal]]), accelerating [[Advanced Glycation End Products]] formation. Conversely, AGE crosslinks lock proteins into rigid conformations that may alter racemization kinetics. The combined load of racemized and glycated residues impairs [[Proteasomal]] and [[Autophagic]] clearance, since D-amino acid-containing proteins resist degradation by normal L-stereospecific proteases.

## Relevance to Cataract Formation and Longevity

[[Cataract]] — opacification of the lens — is the most thoroughly characterized pathology of racemization. Human lens γ-crystallins accumulate D-aspartic acid at specific positions (Asp-58, Asp-151) with age, and the extent of racemization correlates directly with cataract severity. The conformational change induced by D-Asp promotes protein unfolding, aggregation, and light scattering. Racemization has been proposed as a [[Biomarker of aging]] at the molecular level, with potential applications in [[Longevity]] research and [[Anti-aging]] intervention testing. Caloric restriction, which extends lifespan in multiple species, is associated with reduced racemization rates in rodent tissues, suggesting that interventions that slow macromolecular damage may modulate racemization kinetics.
