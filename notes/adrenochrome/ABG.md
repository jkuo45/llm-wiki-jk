# ABG

[[ABG]] ([[Arterial blood gas]]) is a diagnostic test that measures the partial pressures of oxygen (PaO₂) and carbon dioxide (PaCO₂), pH, bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), base excess (BE), and oxygen saturation (SaO₂) from an arterial blood sample. When performed with [[Co-oximetry]], ABG also quantifies hemoglobin species including [[Methemoglobin]] (MetHb), carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), [[Oxyhemoglobin]] (O₂Hb), deoxyhemoglobin (HHb), and sulfhemoglobin. ABG is a cornerstone of assessment in [[Critical Care]], [[Emergency Medicine]], and [[Pulmonology]]. Samples are typically drawn from the [[Radial artery]], [[Femoral artery]], or [[Brachial artery]].

## Parameters & Interpretation

The core ABG panel includes:

| Parameter | Normal Range | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|
| pH | 7.35–7.45 | Acidemia (<7.35) or alkalemia (>7.45) |
| PaO₂ | 80–100 mmHg | Assesses oxygenation; varies with age and FiO₂ |
| PaCO₂ | 35–45 mmHg | Reflects alveolar ventilation; key to respiratory acid–base status |
| HCO₃⁻ | 22–26 mEq/L | Metabolic component of acid–base balance |
| Base Excess (BE) | −2 to +2 mEq/L | Quantifies metabolic acid–base deviation |
| SaO₂ | 95–100% | Calculated from PaO₂ via oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve |
| Lactate | <2 mmol/L | Marker of tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism (see [[L-lactate]], [[D-lactate]]) |

### Acid–Base Disorders

Respiratory acidosis (↓pH, ↑PaCO₂) arises from hypoventilation; acute compensation involves cellular buffering while renal HCO₃⁻ retention requires days. Respiratory alkalosis (↑pH, ↓PaCO₂) results from hyperventilation with compensatory renal HCO₃⁻ excretion. Metabolic acidosis (↓pH, ↓HCO₃⁻) is categorized by the [[Anion gap]] (AG = Na⁺ − [Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻]; normal 8–12 mEq/L): an elevated AG indicates unmeasured anions (e.g., lactate, ketones, uremic toxins), as seen in [[Diabetic Ketoacidosis]], [[Lactic acidosis]], or intoxication. A normal-AG (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis occurs with bicarbonate losses (diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis). Metabolic alkalosis (↑pH, ↑HCO₃⁻) results from acid loss (vomiting, diuretics) or alkali administration.

### A‑a Gradient

The alveolar–arterial (A‑a) oxygen gradient (P(A‑a)O₂) distinguishes hypoventilation (normal gradient) from V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion impairment (elevated gradient). Calculated as: A‑a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂, where PAO₂ = (FiO₂ × [Patm − 47]) − (PaCO₂/0.8) at sea level. An elevated gradient suggests [[Pulmonary embolism]], [[ARDS]], [[Interstitial lung disease]], or right-to-left shunt.

## Co-oximetry

Modern ABG analyzers incorporate multi-wavelength [[Co-oximetry]] to directly measure fractional hemoglobin species:

- **O₂Hb** (Oxyhemoglobin): Normal >94%
- **HHb** (Deoxyhemoglobin): Normal <5%
- **COHb** (Carboxyhemoglobin): Normal <2% (smokers up to 10%)
- **MetHb** (Methemoglobin): Normal <1.5%
- **Sulfhemoglobin**: Normally absent

This capability is critical for the diagnosis of [[Methemoglobinemia]]. In methemoglobinemic patients, PaO₂ (measured by electrode) remains normal, but SaO₂ calculated from PaO₂ overestimates true oxygen saturation because MetHb cannot carry oxygen. The "saturation gap" — a disparity between calculated SaO₂ and measured O₂Hb — is a hallmark. MetHb levels >1.5% are abnormal; >15% produce [[Cyanosis]] and chocolate-brown blood; >50% can cause life-threatening tissue [[Hypoxia]], arrhythmias, seizures, and [[Cardiac arrest]]. [[CO-oximetry]] also identifies [[Carbon monoxide poisoning]] (elevated COHb) and sulfhemoglobinemia.

## Clinical Applications

**Respiratory Failure & ARDS**: The PaO₂/FiO₂ (P/F) ratio grades acute hypoxemic respiratory failure: mild ARDS 200–300 mmHg, moderate 100–200 mmHg, severe <100 mmHg (Berlin definition). ABG also tracks the response to [[Mechanical ventilation]], [[Positive end-expiratory pressure]], and [[Proning]].

**Shock & Sepsis**: Serial arterial [[Lactate]] measurement is integral to the [[Sepsis]] resuscitation bundle. Lactate clearance (>20% reduction over 6 hours) correlates with improved survival. ABG pH and base deficit guide the adequacy of resuscitation.

**Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)**: ABG reveals a high-anion-gap [[Metabolic acidosis]] with compensatory respiratory alkalosis. The anion gap tracks treatment response as ketones ([[Beta-hydroxybutyrate]], [[Acetoacetate]]) clear.

**Methemoglobinemia**: ABG with co-oximetry is the definitive diagnostic test. A normal PaO₂ with low measured SaO₂ (by co-oximetry) and an elevated MetHb fraction confirms the diagnosis. ABG also guides therapeutic decisions: exchange transfusion or [[Methylene blue]] therapy may be considered when MetHb exceeds 30% or symptoms are severe (see [[Exchange transfusion]], [[Methylene blue]]).

**Cardiac Arrest**: Intra-arrest ABG monitors the adequacy of [[CPR]]. Venous blood gas (VBG) is sometimes used as a less invasive alternative for pH and PCO₂ trends, though ABG remains superior for oxygenation assessment.

**Preoperative Assessment**: ABG is performed in high-risk surgical patients to identify occult respiratory or metabolic derangements before [[Anesthesia]].

## Limitations

ABG is an invasive, painful procedure requiring technical skill ([[Radial artery]] puncture or [[Arterial line]] placement). Complications include hematoma, arterial spasm, thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm, and rarely infection. It provides a single time-point assessment — not continuous monitoring. In hypotensive or vasoconstricted patients, the radial pulse may be impalpable, necessitating [[Femoral artery]] or [[Brachial artery]] access. Alternatives include [[Venous blood gas]] (for pH and PCO₂ trends, not oxygenation), [[Pulse oximetry]] (continuous SpO₂ but unreliable in methemoglobinemia or carbon monoxide poisoning), and [[Transcutaneous monitoring]] (tcPO₂/tcPCO₂) for neonates.
