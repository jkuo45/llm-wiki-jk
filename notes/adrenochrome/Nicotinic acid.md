# Nicotinic acid

## Overview

Nicotinic acid (C₆H₅NO₂, molar mass 123.11 g/mol), commonly called niacin, is a water-soluble vitamin belonging to the [[Vitamin B3]] family. It is an essential nutrient in humans, who lack the ability to synthesize it de novo in sufficient quantities. The recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for niacin is approximately 14–16 mg/day for adults, with requirements expressed in niacin equivalents (NE) to account for conversion from dietary tryptophan (60 mg tryptophan ≈ 1 mg NE). Primary dietary sources include meat (particularly liver and poultry), fish (tuna, salmon), poultry, and fortified grains. Niacin is historically notable as the curative factor for [[Pellagra]], a deficiency disease that was endemic in populations subsisting on maize-based diets lacking bioavailable niacin.

## Chemical Properties

Nicotinic acid is a pyridine-3-carboxylic acid (IUPAC: pyridine-3-carboxylic acid) — a white, crystalline solid (mp 236–239 °C) freely soluble in water (~18 g/L) and ethanol, stable to heat, light, and air. Key parameters: pKa 4.85 (carboxyl group), UV λmax ~262 nm, log P 0.36.

[[Nicotinamide]] (the amide form) is structurally related, replacing the carboxyl group with a carboxamide (-CONH₂). Both are equally effective as vitamins, but nicotinamide does not cause the niacin flush. [[Nicotinamide Riboside]] and [[NMN]] are additional NAD+ salvage precursors.

## NAD+ Biosynthesis

Nicotinic acid enters the **Preiss-Handler pathway**, the primary route for [[NAD+]] biosynthesis from dietary vitamin B3. The pathway proceeds in three steps: (1) NAPRT condenses nicotinic acid with PRPP → nicotinic acid mononucleotide (NaMN); (2) [[NMNAT]] adenylates NaMN → nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD); (3) NAD+ synthetase amidates NaAD → NAD+.

This operates alongside the salvage pathway ([[Nicotinamide]] → NMN → NAD+ via NAMPT/NMNAT) and the nucleoside salvage route ([[Nicotinamide Riboside]] → NMN → NAD+ via NRK1/2). Nicotinic acid bypasses NAMPT — the rate-limiting, NAD+-sensitive step of salvage — a feature exploited by high-dose niacin to boost NAD+ even when NAMPT is suppressed.

## Physiological Functions

As the obligate precursor of [[NAD+]] and its phosphorylated counterpart [[NADPH]], nicotinic acid is fundamental to over 500 enzymatic reactions. [[NAD+]] functions as:

- **Electron carrier** in redox reactions: Accepts hydride equivalents to form [[NADH]], which feeds the electron transport chain (Complex I) and drives oxidative phosphorylation. The [[NAD]]+/[[NADH]] ratio varies between ~0.1 (cytosol) and ~10 (mitochondrial matrix), reflecting compartment-specific metabolic states.
- **Substrate for NAD+-consuming enzymes:** [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT3]], [[SIRT6]], and other sirtuins (class III HDACs) cleave NAD+ to generate O-acetyl-ADP-ribose and nicotinamide, coupling deacetylation to energy status. [[PARP1]] and [[PARP]] family members polymerize ADP-ribose onto target proteins, consuming NAD+ in the process. [[CD157]] and [[SARM1]] also participate in NAD+ cleavage signaling.
- **Precursor to NADP+ and NADPH:** Via NAD+ kinase, NADPH is generated for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense (glutathione reduction, thioredoxin reductase).

The NAD+ pool size and its subcellular compartmentation (nuclear, mitochondrial, cytosolic) are dynamically regulated by biosynthesis, consumption, and salvage, making niacin availability a critical determinant of cellular resilience, especially in tissues with high energy demand (brain, heart, skeletal muscle).

## Clinical Applications

### Pellagra

Severe niacin deficiency causes [[Pellagra]] — the "four Ds": diarrhea, dermatitis (photosensitive), dementia, and death. Endemic in early 20th-century populations subsisting on maize (where niacin is chemically bound and tryptophan is low), pellagra was eliminated in developed nations by fortification but persists in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and India. Rapidly reversible with 100–300 mg/day niacin if caught before advanced neurodegeneration.

### Dyslipidemia

At pharmacological doses (500–2000 mg/day), niacin lowers hepatic VLDL secretion and triglycerides, reduces [[LDL]], and raises HDL cholesterol through inhibition of DGAT2. Randomized trials (AIM-HIGH, HPS2-THRIVE) showed modest additive benefit over statins with significant side effects, limiting its current role to specific dyslipidemias.

### Niacin Flush

The acute vasodilatory flush (erythema, warmth, pruritus) is mediated by [[GPR109A]] on Langerhans cells and macrophages, triggering prostaglandin D₂ release. The flush is dose-dependent, desensitizes with chronic use, and can be mitigated by aspirin or extended-release formulations. It remains the primary barrier to compliance.

### Adrenochrome Hypothesis

In a mid-20th-century line of thinking, [[Abram Hoffer]] and [[Humphry Osmond]] proposed that niacin could attenuate the formation of [[Adrenochrome]] by shunting epinephrine metabolism away from oxidative pathways. The rationale was that niacin, by enhancing [[NADPH]] availability via the Preiss-Handler pathway, supports reductive detoxification systems (e.g., [[DT-diaphorase]], [[NQO1]]) that reduce adrenochrome back to [[Leuco-adrenochrome]] or its precursors. This hypothesis — part of the broader [[Adrenochrome Hypothesis]] of schizophrenia — has not been validated by rigorous clinical evidence, and contemporary views attribute niacin's effects in psychiatric contexts to NAD+‑dependent neuronal energetics and [[SIRT1]]-mediated transcriptional regulation rather than direct adrenochrome antagonism.

## Side Effects

The most common is the niacin flush (up to 80% at initiation), which is benign but limits adherence. Additional adverse effects include hepatotoxicity at doses >2 g/day (especially extended-release), hyperglycemia (5–15% rise in fasting glucose), gastrointestinal discomfort, hyperuricemia, and rare macular edema at high doses.
