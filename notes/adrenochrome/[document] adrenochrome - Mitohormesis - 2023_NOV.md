## Abstract

Perturbation of mitochondrial function can trigger a host of cellular responses that seek to restore cellular metabolism, cytosolic proteostasis and redox homeostasis. In some cases, these responses persist even after the stress is relieved, leaving the cell or tissue in a less vulnerable state. This process-termed mitohormesis- is increasingly viewed as an important aspect of normal physiology and a critical modulator of various disease processes. Here, we review aspects of mitochondrial stress signaling that among other thing can rewire the cell’s metabolism, activate the integrated stress response and alter cytosolic quality control pathways. We also discuss how these pathways are implicated in various disease states from pathogen challenge to chemotherapeutic resistance and how their therapeutic manipulation can lead to new strategies for a host of chronic conditions including aging itself.

Many a tiger parent or exacting coach has invoked the words of the 19th century German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche, ‘what doesn’t kill you, makes you stronger’. While the psychological merits of such motivational approaches are at best uncertain, the biological ramifications of this dictum appear to have particular relevance to mitochondria. Roughly a decade ago, we had the opportunity to introduce the concept of [[Mitohormesis]] to the readers of this journal [^1]. The notion advanced then was that mild mitochondrial stress triggered a host of cellular changes leading to the rewiring of the cell’s metabolism, protein folding capacity and redox environment. These alterations collectively changed the cell or organism to a state that made them less vulnerable to future stress. This therefore represents a subcellular form of the more general process of [[hormesis]]- a principle that has broad implications across multiple aspects of biology [^2]. In the ensuing decade, these concepts have been substantially expanded and refined. In the following sections, we review and emphasize the recent progress on how mitochondrial stress is sensed and how the cell responds. Signals that provide an assessment of mitochondrial health have expanded beyond reactive oxygen species (ROS), calcium or metabolites, to include mitochondrial membrane potential, protease activity and protein import. We discuss both the cell and non-cell autonomous response to these danger signals, as well as the implication of mitohormesis in human disease and normal aging.

## Metabolic Stress Signaling

It is perhaps not surprising that disruption of mitochondrial function would lead to alterations in intracellular metabolism. Such links were made early on when the growth of cells depleted of mitochondrial DNA were shown to require supplementation with uridine and pyruvate [^3]. However, what intracellular signaling pathway coordinates this metabolic reprogramming in the setting of mitochondrial stress was for many years poorly understood. One early clue came from the analysis of mice that express a mutant form of the mitochondrial helicase Twinkle [^4]. These animals exhibit a normal lifespan but develop an age-dependent, progressive dysfunction of their respiratory chain and the presence of cytochrome c oxidase (COX)-deficient fibers in their skeletal muscle. Transcriptional analysis of the affected muscle noted multiple alterations to a wide array of metabolic genes including the mitochondrial-localizing gene product methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (Mthfd2) [^4]. The detection of MTHFD2 was surprising since this protein is highly expressed during development and in some tumors but generally has low expression in most adult tissues [^5]. The concerted induction of MTHFD2 and other metabolic genes was dependent on a conserved motif, known as an amino acid response element (AARE), found in the proximal promoter region of the induced genes. This AARE response element is known to bind the C/EBP and ATF family of transcription factors. The link between mitochondrial dysfunction, ATF family members and intracellular metabolism was further strengthened by a comprehensive analysis of a cellular model of mitochondrial DNA depletion [^6]. In this study, depleting mitochondrial DNA led to an ATF4-mediated increase in serine biosynthesis and transsulfuration. Serine has many metabolic fates; however, it is a major source of one-carbon units for folate metabolism, which in turn supports purine and thymidine synthesis. Metabolomic analysis demonstrated that respiratory chain deficiency led to increased serine and decreased formate levels. One critical step in the formation of formate from serine within the mitochondria involves the NAD-dependent activity of MTHFD2. One way to interpret this complex response is that mitochondrial perturbation or stress leads to reduced mitochondrial NAD <sup>+</sup> levels, impairing one carbon metabolism and resulting in a compensatory ATF4-dependent response to help maintain nucleotide levels and redox homeostasis. While these mitochondrial responses were seen in a variety of transformed cell lines, they were entirely consistent with observations from mouse models of mitochondrial deficiencies which also revealed a reprograming of serine and one carbon metabolism [^7]. In addition, NAD <sup>+</sup> levels have been implicated as an important mediator of mitochondrial stress signaling in other contexts [^8]

In the preceding examples, mitochondrial stress was induced predominantly by the depletion of mitochondrial DNA. The transcriptional program initiated by this stress appeared to be mediated by ATF4 binding to a conserved AARE element present within a host of metabolic response genes. Subsequent analysis using a wide array of strategies to invoke mitochondrial stress also implicated ATF4 as a common and critical response component [^9]. These strategies included treating cells with doxycycline to inhibit mitochondrial translation, actinonin to alter the stability of OXPHOS proteins, carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) to reduce mitochondrial membrane potential and MitoBloCK-6 to impair mitochondrial protein import. This suggests that in mammalian cells, ATF4 may be a common downstream mediator of the metabolic reprogramming that occurs following diverse mitochondrial stressors. With that said, other transcriptional mediators including ATF5, p53 and c-myc can also play a role– [^10]. For instance, evidence suggests that ATF5 partitions between the nucleus and the mitochondria in a fashion seemingly analogous to a yeast transcription factor, ATFS-1, that will be described in detail below [^10]. Similarly, p53 has both a nuclear and mitochondrial localization, and the subcellular localization is modulated by cellular stress [^12]. Finally, c-myc is markedly upregulated in various models of mitochondrial dysfunction where it appears to coordinate an adaptive, or sometimes maladaptive, response,[^11]. This may relate to previous observations that a large fraction of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes are transcriptionally regulated by c-myc [^14]. Of note, a recent mouse model in which c-myc was postnatally inactivated revealed the expected reduction in nuclear-encoded mitochondrial transcripts and the somewhat paradoxical phenotype of both premature aging and a longer lifespan [^15]. The former phenotype was related in part to the role of c-myc in maintaining redox balance and metabolic homeostasis, while the latter (i.e. longer lifespan) was a result of a dramatic reduction in cancer incidence in these myc-deficient animals [^15].

## Mitochondria and the ISR

ATF4 is one of the prototypical transcription factors known to be activated during the integrated stress response (ISR). As the name implies, the ISR is triggered by a wide array of cellular stressors. Common to all these perturbations is the phosphorylation of the translation initiation factor eIF2α, which reduces overall total protein synthesis, while simultaneously allowing preferential translation of certain factors such as ATF4 [^16]. The phosphorylation of eIF2α can be initiated by four kinases, each of which is triggered by different forms of cellular stress: PERK (ER stress), PKR (double-stranded RNA), GCN2 (amino acid starvation) and HRI (reduced heme levels). While the ISR is classically associated with stress emanating from the ER, it is becoming increasingly clear that signals emanating from mitochondria can also initiate this response. For instance, treating mammalian cells with either gamitrinib-triphenylphosphonium (GTPP)—a specific inhibitor of HSP90, a mitochondrial matrix chaperone, or inhibitors of the mitochondrial LON protease, triggers protein misfolding within the mitochondria and the subsequent induction of ATF4 and the ISR [^17]. This response was distinct from the well-studied UPR <sup>ER</sup> response induced by agents such as tunicamycin, which is classically accompanied by the induction of ER-resident proteins such as BIP [^17]. These mammalian examples that induce the ISR are also clearly distinct from the better studied examples in C. *elegans*. In worms, mitochondrial dysfunction caused by protein misfolding alters the subcellular localization of the transcription factor ATFS-1. In healthy mitochondria, ATFS-1 is imported into the organelle and subsequently degraded by the Lon protease, while during stress, ATFS-1 instead traffics to the nucleus to drive transcription of genes involved in the coordinated response known as the UPRmt,[^18]. Of note, the worm UPR <sup>mt</sup> is largely independent of ISR activation [^20]. However, as we will discuss, ATFS-1 subcellular distribution provides the first of many examples where an alteration in mitochondrial protein import provides a readout of mitochondrial health and a signal to induce a hormetic-type response.

One major question left unanswered by the previous studies was exactly how ATF4 was activated in response to mitochondrial dysfunction. The answer came when two groups, working with different mammalian cell lines, simultaneously implicated the HRI branch of the ISR as a critical aspect of this mitochondrial stress response,[^21]. While HRI is classically activated by heme depletion, and given that mitochondria are central to heme synthesis, activation of HRI by mitochondrial stress would see eminently plausible. However, somewhat surprisingly, a heme-dependent mechanism for HRI activation was quickly ruled out. Rather, both groups using unbiased, whole genome CRISPR-based screens, albeit with different cell types, different mitochondrial stressors and different readouts, converged on the poorly characterized protein death ligand signal enhancer 1 (DELE1). Previous studies had identified DELE1 through a yeast two hybrid screen as a mitochondrial protein that appeared to modify ligand-induced (e.g. TNF-α, TRAIL) cell death [^23]. However, what these two new studies both observed was that a wide array of mitochondrial stressors altered the mitochondrial/cytosolic distribution of DELE1 ([Figure 1](#F1)). With stress, the form of DELE1 that accumulated in the cytosol was smaller (DELE1-S) than what was seen in the mitochondria and its accumulation appeared to depend on the mitochondrial protease OMA1. Previous studies had demonstrated that OMA1 activity was markedly elevated following mitochondrial stress,[^24]. This led to a model in which diverse mitochondrial stressors activated OMA1-dependent DELE1 cleavage and its subsequent cytosolic accumulation. Moreover, this cleaved, cytosolic DELE1 appeared to directly bind and activate HRI and this interaction was required for subsequent activation of the ISR and ATF4.

![Figure 1:](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/3fe3/10632604/4a21fc7d07f6/nihms-1939566-f0001.jpg)

Mitochondrial stress can activate the integrated stress response (ISR) through a DELE1-dependent pathway. Various perturbations in mitochondrial function lead to activation of the OMA1 protease. This mitochondrial enyzme catalyzes the cleaveage of DELE1, resulting in a smaller cytosolic form of the protein (DELE1-S) that can bind and activate HRI. Activation of HRI results in the induction of the ISR, leading to ATF4 induction and the transcription of metabolic genes, and other downstream targets. Other stresses, like low iron levels or impaired translocation, can lead to the accumulation of full length DELE1 (DELE1-L) in the cytosol or on the outer mitochondrial membrane which can also activate HRI.

Follow up studies have further refined how DELE1-S is generated in the setting of mitochondrial stress. When this process occurs, it does not involve DELE1 already within the mitochondrial matrix, but rather targets DELE1 at the stage of mitochondrial protein import and processing. Various mitochondrial stressors make newly synthesized, incoming DELE1 susceptible to OMA1 mediated cleavage, while perturbations of protein import across the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) result in cytosolic accumulation of DELE1 that is independent of OMA1 [^26]. Initial studies suggested that activation of ATF4 in the setting of mitochondrial stress might have either beneficial or deleterious effects perhaps depending on the nature of the stress [^9]. However, several recent studies suggest a hormetic, protective role of DELE1 in cardiomyopathies driven by mitochondrial dysfunction– [^27] with at least one study implicating a role for this mitochondrial stress pathway in preventing ferroptosis [^27]. The latter observations are broadly consistent with very recent observations that DELE1 mitochondrial protein import is exquisitely sensitive to intracellular iron levels [^30]. Moreover, this recent study demonstrated that in the setting of iron deficiency, full length DELE1 (DELE1-L) accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane to activate the ISR, thereby obviating the requirement for OMA1-mediated cleavage [^30]. Finally, while a wide variety of diverse mitochondrial stressors result in a shared nuclear transcriptional response that is shaped by the common activation of the ISR, the mitochondrial transcriptional response (i.e. mitochondrial-encoded transcripts) appears quite variable, and highly dependent on the nature of the underlying mitochondrial stress [^31].

## Stress at the Border

The emerging theme from many of the studies discussed (i.e. ATSF-1, DELE1, etc.) is that protein import or processing is often used as a readout of mitochondrial health and a signal to induce a hormetic response. This concept had been first introduced in the context of mitochondrial quality control. In that case, impaired mitochondrial function resulted in the disruption of PINK1 mitochondrial import and the subsequent accumulation of PINK1 on the surface of the mitochondria, serves as a catalyst for Parkin-dependent [[mitophagy]] [^32]. These examples highlight that while the evolution of organelles within cells allowed for spatially restricted enzymatic processes and hence increased efficiency, this compartmentation also created boundaries that required additional strategies to maintain and monitor traffic across these subcellular borders. Within the mitochondria, different zip codes or sub-compartments exist including the mitochondrial matrix, the inner mitochondrial membrane, the inner mitochondrial space and outer mitochondrial membrane. Specific protein translocases are responsible for sorting an incoming precursor protein to the appropriate mitochondrial sub-compartment. Like any border dispute, impaired mitochondrial import can trigger significant issues on both sides of the boundary.

The main entry point for nuclear-encoded mitochondrial bound proteins is through the ‘translocase of the outer membrane’ (TOM complex). This entry of such proteins is aided by cytosolic chaperones including HSP70 and HSP90 [^35]. Mitochondrial membrane proteins that have long hydrophobic transmembrane domains are not effectively chaperoned by HSP70 and HSP90, and have therefore evolved their own strategy for mitochondrial delivery and for degradation, if their initial attempts at translocation or insertion fail. Evidence suggest that the ubiquilin (UBQLNs) family of proteins can bind these long hydrophobic transmembrane domains in order to prevent aggregation of mitochondrial membrane proteins in the cytosol [^36]. Previous work had connected UBQLNs to neurodegeneration with mutations in UBQLN2 identified as a causal gene in a dominantly inherited form of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [^37]. This again underscores the relationship between mitochondrial protein import, cytosolic proteostasis and human disease. It should be noted that while most mitochondrial precursor proteins are kept in an unfolded, import-competent state in the cytosol, exceptions exist. For instance, in yeast, accumulation of mitochondrial precursors with N-terminal targeting sequences are stored in stress-like granules termed MitoStores, a process regulated by cytosolic heat shock proteins [^38]. In mammalian cells, non-imported citrate synthase can accumulate in the cytosol where it can actually function enzymatically, thereby exerting a gain-of-function toxicity by synthesizing excess cytoplasmic citrate [^39]. In this case, defects in mitochondrial protein import can disrupt both protein and metabolic homeostasis.

Evidence suggests that specific stress-sensing pathways are activated if precursor proteins arrest within the TOM channel or on the outer mitochondrial membrane. In yeast, one such quality control mechanism is termed the mitochondrial protein translocation-associated degradation (mitoTAD) pathway [^40]. A key component of this pathway is the protein Ubx2, previously implicated in the export of misfolded proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum, as part of the endoplasmic reticulum associated degradation (ERAD) pathway,[^41]. A portion of Ubx2 can associate with the mitochondrial TOM complex where like in the ER, it can bind to the cytosolic AAA ATPase Cdc48 (p97/VCP in mammalian cells) [^40]. Cdc48/p97 is a multifunctional protein that extracts proteins from a wide range of membranes or macromolecular complexes. Evidence suggests that it accomplishes this extraction acting as an unfoldase that uses ATP hydrolysis from its two ATPase domains to pull the polypeptide substrate through the central pore of the molecule, in a fashion reminiscent of the 19S regulatory subunit of the proteasome [^43]. In this scenario, Cdc48 is believed to extract precursor proteins trapped within the TOM channel and then deliver these stalled proteins to the proteasome [^40]. A similar proteasomal-Cdc48 dependent pathway has also been recently implicated for the removal of misfolded proteins that localize to the outer mitochondrial membrane [^44]. In contrast, mistargeted tail-anchored (TA) proteins, namely TA proteins that are inappropriately sorted to the mitochondria, are extracted from the outer mitochondrial membrane by the mitochondrial AAA-ATPase Msp1/ATAD1 and then transferred to the ER to be either re-sorted or degraded [^45]. Additional roles for Msp1 are discussed below. For both proteins stalled within the TOM channel or for misfolded proteins on the mitochondrial surface, proteasomal degradation requires that the substrate is ubiquitinated. Interestingly, emerging evidence suggests that ubiquitination of proteins being imported into the mitochondria is dynamically regulated by E3 ligases and deubiquitinases (e.g. USP30) that specifically localize to the TOM channel,[^46].

An additional yeast quality control pathway that surveils for blocked mitochondrial protein import is the aptly named mitochondrial compromised protein import response or mitoCPR [^48]. Activation of mitoCPR was shown to be critical for cell viability in the setting of impaired mitochondrial protein import and was mediated by a mitochondrial-nuclear signaling pathway that led to the induction of the transcription factor PDR3. PDR3 in turn induces the expression of the adaptor protein Cis1 which can then recruit the AAA ATPase Msp1 to the mitochondria [^48]. Mutations in the human homologue of Msp1 has been implicated in a severe early-infantile encephalopathy [^49]. Msp1 appears to directly contact the proteasome allowing for efficient delivery of ubiquitinated, stalled proteins [^50]. Finally, when precursor proteins arrest inside translocases due to ribosome stalling another pathway involving the addition of C-terminal amino acids to the stalled polypeptide (CAT tailing) is activated to mediate proteasomal degradation [^51]. These CAT-tails can induce protein aggregation once inside the mitochondrial matrix. This aggregation is antagonized by Vms1, a ribosome-binding protein and a key component of a ribosome quality-control pathway for mitochondrial polypeptides (mitoRQC), that acts in concert with E3-ligase Ltn1 to maintain mitochondrial proteostasis [^52].

In yeast, mutations in the adenine nucleotide translocase Aac2p result in age-dependent mitochondrial degeneration and degenerative cell death [^33]. This yeast phenotype mimics what is seen in patients with mutations in isoform 1 of the adenine nucleotide translocase (*ANT1*) which also promotes ADP/ATP exchange across the mitochondrial inner membrane. In yeast, the mitochondrial dysfunction caused by expression of the mutant from of Aac2p results in massive cytosolic accumulation of mitochondrial precursors. These accumulated precursors trigger a form of cell death which has been termed mitochondrial precursor over-accumulation stress (mPOS) [^34]. A genetic screen to identify suppressors of mPOS-mediated cell death revealed numerous hits, with the majority of suppressors acting to modulate cytosolic protein chaperoning/degradation, mRNA silencing/turnover, ribosomal function/protein translation, transfer RNA methylation, or the target of rapamycin (TOR) signaling [^34]. These pathways all reflect mechanisms to modulate cytosolic protein homeostasis, suggesting a complex wiring exists between mitochondrial function and cytosolic proteostasis. These connections likely have significant implications for mitochondrial diseases, diseases of protein aggregation and normal aging.

Up until now, we have largely focused above on the proximal response to impaired mitochondrial protein import, but these local actions are best interpreted in the setting of the larger proteomic and transcriptional response to this stress. One initial foray into delineating this larger hormetic-type response was a study which employed a temperature-sensitive (ts) mutant in the MIA (mitochondrial intermembrane space assembly) pathway, the critical machinery for the import of proteins destined for the mitochondrial intermembrane space [^53]. Using this ts-mutant, the authors then catalogued the transcriptional and proteomic response to this mitochondrial import defect and the subsequent stress instigated by precursor over-accumulation in yeast. The coordinated response, which the authors deemed the unfolded protein response activated by mistargeting of proteins (UPRam), largely involved inhibiting expression and production of genes and proteins required for protein synthesis, leading to reduced overall protein synthesis, while simultaneously augmenting components of the proteasome, the major machinery for cellular protein clearance [^53]. These observations are congruent with the previously discussed mPOS response, which also implicated alterations in cytosolic protein synthesis as a response or modifier of mitochondrial protein import stress [^34].

A subsequent study further analyzed the transcriptome response to ‘clogger’ proteins, namely artificial protein constructs that are designed to be slowly and inefficiently imported into the mitochondria. Remarkably, expression of these clogger proteins induced changes that affected over 1/3 <sup>rd</sup> of the yeast transcriptome and predominantly increased genes encoding chaperones and components of the ubiquitin–proteasome-system, while repressing cytosolic ribosomes and enzymes of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) machinery [^54]. Remarkably, the bulk of these changes occurred quite quickly, with many evident within 30 minutes of inducing the protein import stress. Teasing out the mechanism of this broad transcriptional response provided some important connections and hierarchy to the word salad of previous studies (e.g. mitoTAD, mitoCPR, etc.) and resulted in what the authors called the Mitoprotein-Induced Stress Response [^55]. In this analysis, the accumulation of mitochondrial precursors in the cytosol initially triggers the activation of the transcription factor HSF1 [^54], an ancient transcription factor known to regulate a wide range of chaperones [^56]. Similar activation of HSF1 had been seen in other organisms where mitochondria are perturbed [^57]. In the context of unresolved ‘clogger’ proteins, HSF1 subsequently activates the transcription factor RPN4 [^54]. Previous studies had implicated RPN4 as an important regulator of the expression of multiple proteasomal subunits [^58]. Interestingly, in a negative feedback fashion, RPN4 was demonstrated to be a very short-lived protein that directly interacts with the proteasome [^58]. These observations provide a signaling pathway to explain the UPRam observation described previously [^53]. Besides inducing proteasomal components, RPN4 also increases the expression of Ubx2 and Cdc48, the central mediators of mitoTAD response [^40], and further activates the transcription factor PDR3, which in turn drives the expression of the adaptor protein Cis1, critical for the mitoCPR response [^48].

These observations suggest that in yeast, activation of HSF1 initiates the downstream induction of two other critical transcription factors (e.g. RPN4 and PDR3). Together, this response orchestrates a comprehensive multi-pronged response to mitochondrial import stress by generating an increase in cytosolic chaperone activity, augmenting the UPS (ubiquitin-proteasomal system) and activating the machinery to clear proteins that are clogging the mitochondrial translocase ([Figure 2](#F2)). In contrast, the observed repression of the OXPHOS machinery appears to be independent of this HSF1-RPN4-PDR3 axis, and instead likely involves the inhibition of the HAP complex, perhaps through some yet-to-be defined post-translational modification [^54]. The HAP complex is a multimeric transcription factor known to regulate mitochondrial gene expression and mediate the switch from fermentation to respiration in the setting of glucose depletion [^59]. While these studies suggest a unified and broad transcriptional program exists in yeast in response to mitochondrial stress, these responses are likely complemented by other, non-transcriptional components. For instance, unbiased mapping of redox-active cysteine thiols in proteins demonstrated that components of the translation apparatus are particularly vulnerable to mitochondria-derived oxidative stress [^60]. In addition to cysteine oxidation, oxidation of methionine residues, triggered by the release of mitochondrial ROS, have been shown to initiate phase separation of critical regulatory proteins that control overall translation,[^61]. This suggests that in yeast, the rise in intracellular ROS emanating from the mitochondria provide additional signals to attenuate global protein synthesis.

Finally, while mitochondrial import stress signaling has been extensively characterized in yeast, corresponding studies in mammalian cells are less well delineated. Similarly, while in yeast, these stresses result in the induction of chaperones, augmentation of the UPS and changes in metabolism-lasting changes that likely improve the overall fitness of the organism- true hormesis is difficult to assess in these simple models. As was the case for the observed evolutionary differences in the UPR <sup>mt</sup> response, it is likely that mitochondrial import stress will be sensed and resolved differently in more complex organisms. At least in worms, there appears to be a role for HSF1 for mediating mitochondrial stress, suggesting this aspect might be conserved– [^63]. However, the yeast transcription factors RPN4 or PDR3 have no clear homologues in higher organisms. Perhaps the best candidates are the mammalian factors NRF1/2, as they are known to transcriptionally regulate proteasomal components [^65] and also play a critical role in mitochondrial biogenesis [^66]. Indeed, a recent study in human cells implicated a NRF1- and HSF1-dependent pathway in the mammalian UPR <sup>mt</sup> response [^67]. Interestingly, this study suggested that in mammalian cells, while stimuli that activated the UPR <sup>mt</sup> response also activated the ISR, the UPR <sup>mt</sup> transcriptional response was not dependent on the usual ISR-ATF4 signaling pathway. Rather, in human cells, the UPR <sup>mt</sup> transcriptional response involved a dual signal of increased mitochondrial ROS and cytosolic protein accumulation [^67]. This dual signal led to redox-dependent alterations in cytosolic chaperones and the subsequent activation of HSF1, in a manner not too dissimilar to what we have described occurs in S. *cerevisiae*. Additional studies are however needed to further delineate how mammalian cells detect and respond to mitochondrial stress and what are the evolutionarily conserved elements of this response.

## Systemic Mediators of Mitochondrial Stress

Up to now, we have mainly focused on the cell autonomous signaling response to mitochondrial stress that serves to try and restore intracellular homeostasis. However, there is a growing body of evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction in one tissue can alter the activity and function of mitochondria in distal tissues and potentially have beneficial systemic effects. One of the first inkling of this phenomenon came from the analysis of worms in which nuclear-encoded cytochrome c oxidase-1 subunit Vb/COX4 was knocked down exclusively in neurons [^68]. This mitochondrial perturbation resulted in an increase lifespan for the organism, a longevity response that appeared to involve the worm’s UPR <sup>mt</sup>. Remarkably, mitochondrial perturbation in neurons activated a UPR <sup>mt</sup> response in the intestine. Since the intestine in worms is not directly innervated by the central nervous system, this suggested a soluble mediator might be at play, or what was aptly termed a mitokine [^68]. Subsequent studies have identified a host of factors that function as potential mitokines in C. *elegans* including various Wnt’s and insulin-like molecules, as well as several neurotransmitters and neuropeptides– [^69]. These soluble factors released in response to mitochondrial stress can activate the UPR <sup>mt</sup> response, at least in part, by the stress-induced activation of the worm’s sphingosine kinase ortholog SPHK-1, an enzyme which catalyzes the conversion of sphingosine to sphingosine-1-phosphate [^71]. Soluble factors presumably allow the organism to induce a concerted and coordinated response to stress. Such responses likely include alterations in organismal metabolism [^72] and increased resistance to invading pathogens [^70]. Remarkably, perturbations in neuronal mitochondria morphology (e.g. fusion/fission balance) can also orchestrate alterations in the mitochondrial morphology of distal tissues [^70].

Similar strategies have aided in the identification of mitokines in higher organisms. Using mice with a skeletal muscle-specific deficiency in Crif1, an integral protein of the large mitoribosomal subunit, helped identify GDF15, a member of the transforming growth factor beta superfamily, as a potential mammalian mitokine [^73]. A similar induction of GDF15 was seen using muscle-specific transgenic expression of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) which led to impaired mitochondrial function [^74]. In both examples, mitochondrial perturbation in skeletal muscle led to rise in circulating GDF15, which in turn, triggered a series of metabolic alterations in distal tissues including regulating body fat mass and reversing hepatic steatosis, acting therefore as a mitokine and a myokine,[^73]. Of note, activation of GDF15 in these and other studies appears to be downstream of the ISR– [^73]. These effects are likely to be relevant to humans. For instance, the widely used anti-diabetic drug metformin is known to induce mild mitochondrial dysfunction and also weight loss. Mechanistic analysis of various animal models and multiple patient cohorts suggests that the weight loss induced by this drug is dependent on the several-fold rise in serum GDF15 levels seen in patients taking metformin,[^76]. As observed in C. *elegans*, mitokines also appear to regulate the overall response to pathogens, and it would appear that this property is also shared by GDF15 [^78].

[[FGF21]] is an additional circulating factor known to be elevated in animal models and human subjects with impaired mitochondrial function– [^79]. Like GDF15, its secretion often appears to be downstream of an OMA1-DELE1-ATF4 regulated pathway,[^28]. Evidence suggests a role for [[FGF21]] in coordinating the complex autocrine and paracrine feedback loops that characterize the *in vivo* progression of mitochondrial diseases [^80]. This is in part due to [[FGF21]]’s pleotropic metabolic effects,[^80] which appear parallel but seemingly non-overlapping with the effects of GDF15 [^83]. While many of the effects of both [[FGF21]] and GDF15 appear conserved between mice and humans, significant differences exist. For instance, in mice, an increase in circulating [[FGF21]] is seen after only 6 hours of fasting while in human subjects, a notable rise in [[FGF21]] requires more than a week without food [^84].

Mitochondria-derived peptides (MDPs) represent another family of secreted factors that can mediate beneficial effects systemically. In theory, mtDNA could potentially contain hundreds of short open reading frames (sORFs) that encode putative microproteins [^85]. To date, eight MDPs have been described including humanin, MOTS-c, and small humanin-like peptides 1 to 6 (SHLPs 1–6) [^85]. MOTS-c is a 16 amino acid peptide encoded by the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene, while humanin is a 24 amino acid peptide encoded by a sORF in the 16S rRNA of mtDNA. MOTS-c appears to be an exercise-induced factor that can increase insulin sensitivity and visceral fat accumulation,[^86]. While levels of circulating MOTS-c rise acutely with exercise [^87], the peptide has also been observed to translocate to the nucleus during energetic stress [^88]. As such, these factors likely play both cell autonomous and non-cell autonomous functions ([Figure 3](#F3)). Similarly, humanin appears to have a wide range of anti-apoptotic and cytoprotective effects, especially within the nervous system [^85]. Additional methodologies are needed to understand the full mode of regulation and physiological effects of these MDPs. In addition, while the assumption is that these peptides are solely mitochondrially-derived, it should be noted that there are, for example, over a dozen distinct human *nuclear* loci that possess a sORF that could potentially encode full-length humanin-like peptides (such loci are termed nuclear mitochondrial pseudogenes or NUMT-pseudogenes) [^89]. Finally, while peptides derived from mitochondria are one form of trans-cellular signaling, it is also possible to transfer whole mitochondria between cells [^90]. Such transfer was first observed to occur through actin-based tunneling nanotubes [^91]. While initially observed in cultured cells, subsequent studies have described this process to occur in numerous *in vivo* settings, with broad potential implications in areas from tissue repair to cancer [^90].

## Mitochondrial Stress and Disease

The pathways described above have been linked tangentially or directly with a number of human diseases. Perhaps the strongest link comes within the nervous system. Defects in proteostasis and mitochondrial dysfunction are commonly co-occurring hallmarks of neurodegenerative diseases. Furthermore, recent genetic studies have identified that individuals with loss-of-function mutations in the widely expressed TOMM70 protein (a component of the outer mitochondrial TOM complex) primarily exhibit neurological disabilities [^92]. This suggests that the metabolically active, post-mitotic neuron might be particularly vulnerable to such perturbations. Consistent with this notion, UPR <sup>mt</sup> response genes appear to be elevated in patients with familiar or sporadic Alzheimer’s disease (AD) compared to controls,[^93]. Similar activation is seen in models of Parkinson’s disease (PD) [^95]. The basis for activating mitochondrial stress in these conditions is likely multifactorial but a number of studies have demonstrated the distinct protein aggregates seen in AD [^96], PD,[^97] or Huntington’s disease [^99] can all interfere with mitochondrial protein import. At least in lower organisms, a decline in import efficiency is linked to UPR <sup>mt</sup> activation [^19]. Conversely, defects in mitochondrial protein import appear to trigger a chain reaction that can lead to the accumulation and aggregation of non-mitochondrial proteins in the cytosol [^100].

Another area where mitochondrial stress signaling is likely to impact human disease is through the influence these pathways play in immune activation and pathogen responses. Numerous connections exist between mitochondria and the immune system [^101]. These include a role for mitochondria and [[mitophagy]] in inflammasome activation,[^102]. Similarly, the release of mitochondrial DNA can trigger critical innate immune pathways such as cGAS-STING [^104]. While these damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) sensing pathways are intimately linked to exogenous pathogens, evidence suggests that mitochondrial DNA-stimulated cGAS-STING activation likely contributes to the sterile inflammation seen in many age-related chronic diseases [^105]. Bacteria secrete a wide array of diffusible proteinaceous toxins that allow these microorganisms to fend off neighboring competing bacterial species [^106]. Such molecules appear to be critical for shaping the ecological diversity of the microbiome [^107]. Given the bacterial origin of mitochondria, it is not unexpected that invading microbes secrete factors that can target host mitochondria. Indeed, a survey of over 500 different bacterial species suggest that a significant fraction secrete factors that can influence and alter host mitochondrial function [^108]. In organisms such as C. *elegans* that are constantly immersed in a sea of microbes, this mitochondrial dysfunction is sensed, at least in part, by the UPRmt, that in turn activates a stress-induced pathway necessary for host defense [^18]. Other bacterial secreted factors can influence the balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission [^109]. These mitochondrial responses can potentially be employed for therapeutic benefit. For instance, widely used antibiotics like tetracyclines, a natural product derived from *Streptomyces*, can inhibit a wide spectrum of bacteria but can also impose a low level of mitochondrial stress on eukaryotic cells. Chemical derivatives of tetracycline that lose their direct antimicrobial function but still trigger a UPR <sup>mt</sup> /ATF4-dependent hormetic response, appear to be broadly protective against pathogen challenge [^110]. Moreover, related agents such as doxycycline appear to increase survival of bacterially-challenged animals through mechanism that are seemingly independent of their antibiotic effects [^111]. The survival benefits seen in the setting of bacterial sepsis appear to be due to the ability of doxycycline to inhibit mitochondrial protein import and induce a subsequent hormetic, protective effect. Interestingly, these beneficial responses could be phenocopied by other agents that provoke mild and transient perturbations of mitochondrial function [^111].

Mitochondrial signaling is also increasingly implicated in macrophage biology- a cell type involved in a multitude of processes from the inflammatory response to wound healing. Analysis of macrophages stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) demonstrated a marked accumulation of the tricarboxylic-acid (TCA) cycle intermediate succinate [^112]. This analysis subsequently demonstrated that LPS-induced succinate stabilized hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF1-α), leading to enhanced interleukin-1β production during inflammation. Subsequent studies demonstrated that mitochondrial ROS generated by succinate oxidation was the critical signaling intermediate required for HIF1-α stabilization [^113]. Of note, mice that are engineered to express an alternative oxidase (AOX) from *Ciona intestinalis, and* thereby are unable to generate sufficient levels of mitochondrial ROS, appear to be protected from LPS-induced mortality [^113]. Subsequent studies have indicated that besides HIF1-α, mitochondrial ROS generated by macrophages can also regulate additional transcription factors including heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) and NRF2 [^114]. In contrast, late-phase, pro-resolving macrophages, a critical component of wound repair, appear to alter their metabolism and shift away from ROS-dependent signaling, and instead, adopt a UPR <sup>mt</sup> signature [^115].

Mitochondrial ROS have also been recently implicated in the cellular response to alterations in the stiffness of the extracellular milieu. Alterations in the extracellular environment are increasingly implicated in pathological processes occurring in disease states including the tumor microenvironment [^116]. Recent data suggests cells can sense the physical properties of the extracellular matrix, in part, through a mitochondrial stress response that involves alterations in mitochondrial morphology and HSF1-dependent transcription [^117]. Activation of HSF-1 appears to be downstream of mitochondrial ROS production, while the induction of HSF-1 appears to subsequently activate pathways that ultimately restore redox homeostasis [^117]. Others have shown that altering the extracellular stiffness can activate a mitochondrial ROS-dependent pathway leading to NRF2 activation [^118]. Activation of this pathway could in turn regulate sensitivity to various chemotherapies [^118]. A role for mitochondrial signaling in resistance to chemotherapy was also seen in what are known as ‘persister’ cells. These cells are functionally defined as cells that remain alive following a cell-death-inducing challenge without the selection for resistance mutations. As such, persister cells are cells that exhibit a temporary, non-genetic survival advantage. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon centers around mitochondrial alterations in persister cells that activates the ISR, with subsequent HRI-mediated ATF4 activation [^119]. Finally, a recent study demonstrated another connection between mitochondria and chemotherapeutic resistance. In this case, signaling was achieved through a mitochondrial-to-nuclear pathway that involved chemotherapeutic-induced mitochondrial ROS production, nuclear redox-dependent activation of checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) and subsequent CHK1-dependent phosphorylation of mitochondrial single stranded DNA-binding protein 1 (SSBP1) [^120]. Moreover, in this study, loss of SSBP1 decreased nuclear H <sub>2</sub> O <sub>2</sub> levels and provided resistance to platinum-based agents in ovarian cancer models.

The hormetic pathways discussed are also important regulators of metabolic homeostasis. Several studies have assessed the systemic metabolic effects of impairing mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle. For instance, conditional skeletal muscle deletion of optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1), a protein required for fusion of the outer mitochondrial membrane and inner mitochondrial membrane, resulted in mitochondrial dysfunction within the skeletal muscle and the secretion of [[FGF21]] into the serum,[^121]. While the mitochondrial dysfunction was confined to muscle, these animals exhibited a wide range of aging and metabolic perturbations,[^121]. Interestingly, in at least one of these studies, mutant mice appeared resistant to age- and diet-induced weight gain and insulin resistance, a phenotype dependent on [[FGF21]] secretion [^122]. Muscle-specific deletion of FUNDC1, another gene involved in mitochondrial fission/fusion and [[mitophagy]], resulted in impaired muscle mitochondrial quality but appeared to mitigate the effects of a high fat diet on obesity and insulin resistance [^123]. In contrast, total body deletion of FUNDC1 appears to exacerbate metabolic homeostasis [^124]. Similarly, perturbation of mitochondrial matrix homeostasis by skeletal muscle deletion of LONP1, an evolutionarily conserved serine peptidase that localizes to the mitochondrial matrix, also appeared to result in an almost complete resistance to diet-induced obesity [^125]. Again, this skeletal muscle specific perturbation resulted in the induction of UPR <sup>mt</sup> signaling in the muscle accompanied by secretion of various myokines such as [[FGF21]] and GDF15 [^125], which potentially mediated the salutatory metabolic benefits. Finally, the hypothalamus is a pivotal control center for systemic metabolism and proopiomelanocortin (POMC)-producing neurons in the hypothalamus play a key role in energy balance. Previous studies have demonstrated that POMC neuronal activity and function is strongly influenced by mitochondrial function– [^126]. Interesting, a recent study noted that when both copies of the mitoribosomal protein CR6-interacting factor 1 (Crif1) were deleted in POMC neurons, severe mitochondrial stress ensued, and these animals exhibited spontaneous obesity [^128]. In contrast, deletion of one allele of *Crif1* produced a milder mitochondrial stress, resulting in resistance to obesity through cell-non-autonomous mitochondrial stress signaling between the hypothalamus and adipose tissues. The latter signaling appeared to involve the mitochondrial micropeptide MOTS-c [^128]. These studies provide a nice genetic example of the principles underlying mitohormesis, wherein a large stress may be harmful, while the same stress, at lower intensity, can provide benefit ([Figure 4](#F4)).

## Summary and Future Perspectives

Although mitochondria have been classically viewed as semi-autonomous, power-producing factories, as noted above, the integrity of these organelles are constantly monitored and adjusted and have widespread implications for human health. This raises the question whether mitochondrial stress can be harnessed to extend lifespan and treat human disease. As discussed, certain widely used agents might already be employing these concepts. Over 150 million people take metformin for adult-onset Type II diabetes. Although its mechanism of action is complex, evidence supports mild inhibition of the mitochondrial electron transport chain as one key element for its beneficial actions [^129]. The fact that this medicine might have a number of other salutatory effects on human health (e.g. various association studies suggesting reduced incidence of age-related cancers, Alzheimer’s disease, etc.) demonstrates the potential to exploit mitohormesis for therapeutic benefit. As previously noted, the use of metformin in patients correlates with a rise in circulating GDF15, a putative myokine linked to the observed therapeutic weight loss seen in patients,[^76]. Physical exercise might be a low cost, non-pharmacological means to achieve the same end. Evidence supports the benefits of exercise in preventing or treating a wide range of conditions [^130]. Rodent models suggest that exercise stimulates a UPR <sup>mt</sup> response in muscle [^131], as well as other tissues [^128]. Similarly, various potentially therapeutic mitokines including GDF15 [^132] and MOT-c,[^87] are markedly stimulated by exercise, suggesting the potential to therapeutically exploit these factors as exercise mimetics. With that said, for those inclined to be couch potatoes, it would appear that too much exercise in humans actually impairs mitochondrial function, reduces ROS production and worsens metabolic health [^133]. These redox dependent effects are consistent with previous intervention studies that came to the conclusions that antioxidants actually blocked the beneficial effects of moderate exercise [^134]. This again speaks to the Goldilocks challenge of ‘drugging’ these hormetic-like pathways, as a moderate level of mitochondrial ROS has benefits, while too much or too little ROS appears harmful.

While stimulating mitohormesis might be beneficial in preventing aging and age-related diseases, interrupting these adaptive pathways may be useful strategy for treating cancer. Activation of UPR <sup>mt</sup> and mitohormesis appears to increase the metastatic potential of cancer cells and in breast cancer, an increased expression of a UPR <sup>mt</sup> gene signature appears to convey a worse prognosis [^135]. Similarly, expression of the mitochondrial chaperone HSP60 is associated with reduced survival in a wide variety of tumor types [^136]. These observations are consistent with other studies showing that cancer cells appear particularly vulnerable to disruption of mitochondrial quality control, as evident by observations showing that inhibiting mitochondrial matrix proteases such as ClpP [^137] or LONP1 [^138] result in selective tumor killing.

As noted, a number of studies have suggested that manipulating mitohormesis might be effective in prolonging lifespan and extending healthspan. We’ve already mentioned this in the context of worms [^68], and similar observations have been made in diverse species from flies [^139] to fish [^140]. In mammalian models, analysis of conplastic mice, namely mice with identical nuclear DNA but different wild-type mtDNA variants, demonstrated that mitochondrial variants can exert profound effects on median lifespan and the rate that age-related pathologies begin to manifest [^141]. Of note, the conplastic strain that lived longest actually exhibited a mild increase in mitochondrial ROS, a redox stress implicated in triggering the subsequent beneficial hormetic response [^141]. A similar ROS-induced lifespan extension effect has been seen in worms [^142], with the benefit of this redox stress appearing to occur very early in development [^143]. Other studies have noted a connection between mild mitochondrial stress and the maintenance of age-related proteostasis [^63]. As mentioned, the widely used drug metformin may extend lifespan through its hormetic activity [^144]. Agents such as urolithin A that target mitochondrial quality control and stress pathways are increasingly being evaluated in randomized clinical trials in humans,[^145], with some evidence, albeit modest, for benefit. Evidence also suggests that the repurposing of other existing agents that can trigger mitohormesis might also be beneficial in combating aging [^147].

Hopefully, the next generation of therapeutics will likely be informed by a better understanding and delineation of the various pathways linked to mitohormesis. To date, the focus has largely been on more tractable experimental models such as yeast and worms. As noted, evolutionary conservation of these pathways exists but to what degree the lessons learned in these model organisms can be directly applied to mammals, much less humans, remains an important avenue of future investigations. While the discovery of OMA1 processing of DELE1 to activate the ISR represent a major step forward, understanding how OMA1 senses stress, and how the mitochondrial proteome can be dynamically remodeled by potentially other resident, stress-sensitive proteases will likely provide additional important clues. Similarly, it is unlikely that DELE1 is the only such mediator and as such, general unbiased strategies to assess intra-organelle communication are needed. In that regard, the recently described [[TransitID]], that ingeniously uses proximity biotinylation, represents one such potential strategy [^148]. Finally, changes in mitochondrial membrane potential or bursts of ROS production are often transient phenomenon. Better tools to quantitatively and simultaneously measure various mitochondrial inputs and outputs in real-time (e.g. NAD <sup>+</sup>, substrates, membrane potential, etc.) would likely yield significant insights.

While technological challenges remain, and many questions remain unresolved, the totality of these studies all point to the notion that transiently perturbing mitochondrial function can result in stable alterations that collectively provide lasting beneficial effects for the organism. Undoubtedly, somewhere, Nietzsche must be smiling.
