# Protein Carbonylation

[[Protein Carbonylation]] is a hallmark oxidative modification in which carbonyl groups (aldehydes or ketones) are introduced onto protein side chains, principally on lysine, arginine, proline, and threonine. It is generally irreversible and marks proteins for degradation or, when proteostasis is overwhelmed, for aggregation. Carbonylation is among the most widely used biomarkers of cumulative [[Oxidative Stress]] and [[Aging]].

## Mechanisms

Carbonyls arise through three main routes: (1) metal-catalyzed oxidation by [[Fenton Reaction]]-generated [[Hydroxyl radical]]; (2) Michael addition of lipid peroxidation products such as 4-hydroxynonenal and malondialdehyde to nucleophilic residues; and (3) direct oxidation of amino acid side chains. Electrophilic quinones from the [[Aminochromes]] and catechol axis—including [[Adrenochrome]] and [[Dopaminochrome]]—can also alkylate proteins, indirectly promoting carbonyl formation.

> [!warning]
> Because carbonylated proteins resist degradation, they accumulate in long-lived cells such as the catecholaminergic neurons of the [[Substantia Nigra]], where adrenochrome/neuromelanin chemistry is active—linking carbonylation to [[Parkinson's Disease]] pathogenesis.

## Relevance to Adrenochrome, Neuromelanin, and Methemoglobin

Catechol-quinone and aminochrome intermediates bind and oxidize proteins, contributing to carbonyl load alongside [[Tyrosine Nitration]]. In hemoglobin, oxidative damage and the [[Methemoglobin]]/nitrite redox cycle generate carbonyls on globin, a measurable index of nitrosative/oxidative injury. Carbonylation thus spans the glyco-oxidative, aminochrome, and nitrosative axes documented across the vault.
