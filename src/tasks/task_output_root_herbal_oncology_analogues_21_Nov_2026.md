# Root and Rhizome Botanicals with Dandelion-Like Anticancer Profiles — Mechanistic Analogues, Human Evidence, and the Bioavailability Failure Mode

Date: 21_Nov_2026 09:08 PM PST

> [!note] Relationship to the companion note
> `src/tasks/task_output_dandelion_root_cancer_21_Nov_2026.md` established the dandelion root extract profile: extrinsic, FADD-dependent, caspase-8-mediated apoptosis with cFLIP suppression; mitochondrial ROS generation selective for cancer cells; pro-death autophagy; PI3K/Akt and NF-κB/STAT3 suppression; interference with glycerophospholipid metabolism via CHKA; inulin as a dominant root fructan; no human efficacy trial; and estrogenic activity as a safety constraint. This note asks which other roots, rhizomes and root barks occupy the same mechanistic space, and what the human trial record for each looks like.

Evidence tiers as in the companion note: Tier 1 = human randomized trial; Tier 2 = human observational, case or single-arm trial evidence; Tier 3 = rodent in vivo; Tier 4 = in vitro; Tier 5 = hypothesis or mechanistic prediction.

## The four axes of similarity

Dandelion's profile decomposes into four separable axes, and not every analogue matches on all four. Matching one axis is not the same as being a functional substitute.

| Axis | Dandelion root extract phenotype | Compounds that reproduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical class | Pentacyclic triterpene (taraxasterol, ψ-taraxasterol, α-/β-amyrin, lupeol), α-methylene-γ-lactone sesquiterpene lactones (taraxacin), phenolic acids, flavones, fructans | Lupeol/ursolic acid (Arctium, Tripterygium), celastrol/pristimerin (Tripterygium), parthenolide (feverfew, Magnolia root bark, Tithonia), baicalin/baicalein (Scutellaria root), curcumin (turmeric rhizome), 6-shogaol/6-gingerol (ginger rhizome), inulin (chicory root) |
| Death mechanism | Extrinsic apoptosis with caspase-8 and FADD dependency, cFLIP suppression, mitochondrial depolarisation and ROS, pro-death autophagy | Withanolide E (cFLIP degradation), parthenolide (NF-κB/STAT3 inhibition and TRAIL re-sensitisation), 6-shogaol (cFLIP-L down-regulation, TRAIL re-sensitisation), 6-gingerol (cIAP1 down-regulation) |
| Signaling and metabolism | PI3K/Akt suppression, NF-κB and STAT3 suppression, HIF-1α/VEGF down, CHKA/phosphocholine interference, hexokinase inhibition, iron handling | Parthenolide and ACT001 (NF-κB, STAT3, PAI-1/PI3K/AKT), withaferin A (Hsp90, FOXO3a/BIM, ROS), Scutellaria root (STAT3 in EGFR-TKI-resistant lung cancer), taraxasterol analogues, chicory inulin (SCFA and microbial fermentation rather than direct cytotoxicity) |
| Translational reality | No human efficacy trial; no pharmacokinetic data; estrogenic activity; CYP1A2/3A4 inhibition; dasatinib bioavailability reduced ~50 % in rats | Feverfew-parthenolide (no detectable plasma concentration at 4 mg/day), withaferin A (no detectable plasma concentration at 216 mg/day), Echinacea root (interferes with doxorubicin in opposite direction), Brucea oil emulsion (adjuvant use in China without randomised evidence) |

## Asteraceae and adjacent sesquiterpene lactones

The closest chemical relative of dandelion's taraxacin class is parthenolide, the principal sesquiterpene lactone of feverfew (*Tanacetum parthenium*). Both carry an α-methylene-γ-lactone ring, and the shared mechanism is covalent alkylation of nucleophilic cysteine residues — IKKβ Cys179 and p65 Cys38 for parthenolide, the same chemistry that makes taraxacin a redox-active electrophile.

### Feverfew (parthenolide)

Parthenolide suppresses [[NF-κB]] by preventing IKK complex recruitment to the TNF receptor, blocking IKK activation, IκBα degradation, p65 nuclear translocation and transactivation, while sustaining JNK activation; the combination sensitises human cancer cells to TNF-α-induced apoptosis, and JNK inhibition or dominant-negative JNK1/2 abolishes the effect (Zhang et al., *Carcinogenesis* 2004, PMID 15256485). In breast cancer cells it reverses TRAIL resistance through JNK-dependent XIAP cleavage and feed-forward caspase-3 activation, independent of NF-κB DNA binding (Nakshatri et al., *Oncogene* 2004, PMID 15286701). In renal cell carcinoma xenografts, subcutaneous or oral parthenolide suppressed tumour growth via reduced IL-8 and VEGF, with decreased nuclear NF-κB and downstream MMP-9, Bcl-xL and COX-2 (Oka et al., *Int J Cancer* 2007, PMID 17290398). In colorectal COLO 205 cells it depleted glutathione and protein thiols, raised ROS and calcium, induced ER stress (GRP78), and apoptosis was completely blocked by N-acetylcysteine and sensitised by buthionine sulfoximine (Zhang et al., *Cancer Lett* 2004). It also reduces the cancer stem-like cell fraction, and parthenolide and dimethylaminoparthenolide (DMAPT) kill breast cancer stem-like cells by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction (Carlisi et al., *Cell Death Dis* 2016, PMID 27077810).

The human record is the informative part. In a phase I dose-escalation trial of standardised feverfew delivering 1–4 mg/day parthenolide in cancer patients, treatment was well tolerated with no dose-limiting toxicity and the maximum tolerated dose was not reached, but parthenolide was not detectable in plasma at any dose (limit of detection 0.5 ng/ml), so pharmacokinetics could not be completed (Curry et al., *Invest New Drugs* 2004, PMID 15122077). This is the same wall dandelion root extract has not yet hit: a tolerated, orally administered plant preparation that delivers no measurable parent compound.

The way past the wall was semi-synthesis rather than better extraction. ACT001 (dimethylamino-micheliolide fumarate) is a parthenolide derivative with adequate oral bioavailability, blood–brain barrier penetration, and inhibition of NF-κB, STAT3 and PAI-1/PI3K/AKT. In the first-in-human phase 1 (ACTRN12616000228482, Australia) 24 patients were enrolled across six dose levels with no dose-limiting toxicity; among 19 patients with recurrent malignant glioma, one complete remission was ongoing at 27 months and three had stable disease of at least 6 months; plasma half-life was 3–4 hours with linear pharmacokinetics (Lickliter et al., *JCO* 2021, 39:2037). In the Chinese single-arm phase 1 (ChiCTR-OIC-17013604) 16 advanced glioma patients were enrolled with no DLT or MTD identified and linear exposure; pathological responses were reported in a subset (Wang et al., *JCO* 2021, 39:2044). In the first-in-child phase 1 of DIPG and DMG, 29 patients were enrolled with no DLT and no grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse event, and 8 of 14 DMG patients treated at ≥700 mg/m² had objective response or stable disease, including one with 14 months SD and one confirmed partial response; a phase 2 was planned at 875 mg/m² (Ziegler et al., *Neuro-Oncology* 2024, PMC11183933). ACT001 also reduced PD-L1 transcription and CD4+ Treg cells, and a 1b/2a study in 45 patients with brain metastases reported fewer whole-brain-radiation adverse events and reduced intracranial burden (ChiCTR2000037739, *JCO* 2022, 40:e14006).

Tier 1, and the only plant-derived anticancer agent in this note with published human efficacy signal above case-report level.

### Magnolia root bark as a parthenolide source

Parthenolide occurs in both Asteraceae and Magnoliaceae, and the root bark of *Magnolia* species is the industrially relevant source. A survey of 967 *Magnolia biondii* root bark samples found parthenolide content from 0.00 to 8.68 % of dry weight, mean 4.06 %, with the absence of parthenolide traced to a single-base deletion in the *MbiPTS* monooxygenase gene that blocks costunolide epoxidation; a KASP marker was developed for breeding (Yin et al., *J For Res* 2026, DOI 10.1007/s11676-026-02089-w). A separate survey of four *Magnolia* species reports mean root-bark parthenolide of 5.10 % dry weight in the highest-yielding species with 2.43 %/4.07 %/4.31 % at 2/3/4 years of tree age and a 3.51–6.04 % seasonal range (Liu et al., *J Nanjing For Univ* 2024, 24(2):227–233). Note that the Chinese-language forestry literature and the Nankai announcement disagree on which species is the source (*M. biondii* vs *M. delavayi*); the two are frequently conflated in the parthenolide-sourcing literature, and the achievable content range across sources is roughly 4–9 %.

This is directly relevant to the dandelion question: it demonstrates that a root-bark matrix can carry a sesquiterpene lactone at ten times the concentration of the aerial parts of the source herb, and that genetic and agronomic characterisation can make a chemotype reproducible. It is also the structural template for a general claim — an anticancer sesquiterpene lactone deliverable from a root, at standardised content, with a derived drug form.

### Tithonia diversifolia (Mexican sunflower)

*Tithonia diversifolia* leaf is used in Central America for haematomas and wounds, and its sesquiterpene lactones diversifolin, diversifolin methyl ether and tirotundin inhibit NF-κB activation without affecting cyclooxygenase-1 or phospholipase A2, with the activity attributed to alkylation of cysteine residues in the NF-κB DNA-binding domain (Rüngeler et al., *Planta Med* 1998, PMID 9810261). Isolated lactones from the flowers show cytotoxicity against KB, MCF-7, HepG2 and A549 cells with IC50 values of 0.28–3.76 µM, with tagitinin C the most potent at 0.28 µM (KB), 1.18 µM (MCF-7), 1.38 µM (HepG2) and 1.15 µM (A549), comparable to ellipticine (Vietnam J Sci Technol 2026). Tier 4, plus a traditional-use rationale.

## Pentacyclic triterpenes and steroidal lactones

Dandelion's taraxasterol and lupeol are pentacyclic triterpenes. Three other root or bark sources carry this class with stronger potency, and in each case the toxicity window is narrower.

### Withania somnifera root (ashwagandha) — withanolides

Withanolide E is the closest mechanistic analogue to dandelion root extract anywhere in the literature: it sensitises renal carcinoma cells to TRAIL by a rapid decrease in cellular cFLIP, enhancing the extrinsic death-receptor pathway rather than the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, and it was the most effective and least toxic of the withanolides tested (Henrich et al., *Cell Death Dis* 2015, PMID 25719250). Withanolide E enhanced death-receptor-induced apoptosis in vivo in ACHN xenografts in combination with the DR5 agonist drozitumab. A structure–activity study across 50 000 compounds identified 17β-hydroxywithanolide E as the lead TRAIL sensitiser (Amar et al., *J Med Chem* 2017, 60:3039–3051). Withaferin A additionally acts through Hsp90 inhibition, FOXO3a/BIM-dependent apoptosis, ROS generation and G2/M arrest (Yang et al., *PLoS ONE* 2014, PMID 24533037), and it kills breast cancer stem cells.

Root extract itself is the clinically used form. A standardised 5 % withanolide *W. somnifera* root extract (Viwithan) gave IC50 220 µg/mL against B16F1 murine melanoma at 24 h — the most sensitive of the lines tested — versus 1830 µg/mL (HepG2), 968 µg/mL (HeLa) and 2715 µg/mL (HCT116), with molecular docking indicating withanolide binding preference for MCL-1 (Sudeep et al., *Pharmacognosy Mag* 2017, PMC5822503). A root ethanol/ethyl acetate and butanol fraction pair gave CC50 51.8 and 40.1 µg/mL against Ca9-22 oral squamous carcinoma, comparable to 5-FU at 69.4 µM and melphalan at 36.3 µM on the same line (Hosami et al., *Front Pharmacol* 2024, 10:1325272). Aqueous root extract raised caspase-8, -9 and -3/7 activity and lowered glutathione in THP-1 leukaemic cells while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, with no corresponding cytotoxicity in donor PBMCs (Mandal et al., 2018, PMC5891897).

The human record shows the same bioavailability wall. In a phase 1 dose-escalation study of a standardised root extract containing 4.5 % withaferin A in advanced high-grade osteosarcoma, 13 patients received 72–216 mg/day; 11 grade 1–2 adverse events occurred (abnormal LFTs 5/13, rash 2/13) with no grade 3–4 events, but withaferin A was not detectable in any of 42 pharmacokinetic samples (Pires et al., 2020, PMID 30904387, PMC7125369). A phase 1/2 of withaferin A plus liposomal doxorubicin in recurrent ovarian cancer is under way (NCT05610735, started 24 September 2024, phase 1 n = 18, phase 2 n = 54, estimated completion 2028). One earlier human study is worth recording because it is often miscited: an open-label non-randomised trial of *W. somnifera* root extract 2 g every 8 hours alongside chemotherapy in 100 breast cancer patients reported quality-of-life improvement, not tumour response (Biswal et al., 2013, PMID 23142798).

The vault's [[Withaferin A]] note currently covers only its SIRT3-dependent anti-fibrotic action; the cFLIP/TRAIL-sensitisation axis is the oncology-relevant one and is absent from it.

### Tripterygium wilfordii root — celastrol, pristimerin, triptolide

*T. wilfordii* root is the source of the pentacyclic triterpene family celastrol and pristimerin, structurally close to taraxasterol, and of the diterpenoid triepoxide triptolide, which is not a triterpene but shares the same broad-spectrum profile: apoptosis induction, autophagy, senescence, and activity against multidrug-resistant and stem-like cells (Gao et al., *Theranostics* 2021, PMID 34158845). Triptolide's mechanism is covalent binding to the XPB subunit of transcription factor TFIIH, causing RPB1 hyperphosphorylation and rapid degradation — a transcriptional shutdown rather than a receptor-level one.

The clinical data are more mature than for dandelion and are instructive in the other direction. Minnelide, a water-soluble phosphate prodrug of triptolide, has been tested in nine registered oncology trials. In the first-in-human phase 1 in advanced gastrointestinal carcinoma (23 pancreatic, 10 colorectal, 9 other), 45 patients were enrolled and 42 dosed; grade ≥3 toxicity occurred in 69 % with neutropenia 38 %, two patients had severe cerebellar toxicity associated with roughly double the triptolide exposure of other participants, ORR was 4 % and disease control rate 54 % (Propper et al., *Oncologist* 2024, 29:132). The phase 2 in refractory pancreatic cancer (NCT03117920) completed in 2023 with no results posted. The NCI phase 2 in advanced refractory adenosquamous carcinoma of the pancreas (NCT04896073) enrolled 16, and in 12 evaluable patients there were no objective responses, grade 4 haematological adverse events in 8.7–25 %, and median overall survival 4.91 months (95 % CI 1.96–7.85) (Zhao et al., *Future Oncol* 2022, 18:2475). The AML trial NCT03760523 was terminated for dose-limiting toxicity and NCT03347994 withdrawn. A phase 1 of Minnelide plus paclitaxel in advanced gastric cancer reported partial response or stable disease in 71.5 % with median PFS 4.5 months and OS 10.7 months, and confirmed ORR of 50 % in paclitaxel-naive patients (NCT05566834, *JCO* 2024, 42:4033).

A quantitative adverse-event synthesis across 46 trials and 2437 *T. wilfordii*-treated participants gives hematotoxicity 5.66 % and leukopenia 5.74 %, with intestinal toxicity the most common category overall and reproductive toxicity the second (He et al., *Front Pharmacol* 2019, 10:1250, PMC6851843). Preparations from different manufacturers varied widely in chemical composition.

The instructive comparison with dandelion is that *T. wilfordii* did reach randomised-adjacent clinical evidence, and the result was a narrow therapeutic window and mostly disease stabilisation rather than response. Better bioavailability than dandelion, worse selectivity.

### Arctium lappa (burdock) root

Burdock root is the closest Asteraceae root by chemistry: it contains the same triterpenes dandelion does — lupeol, ursolic and oleanolic acids — plus lignans (arctigenin, arctiin, diarctigenin) and the same phenolic acids (caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid) and flavonoids (quercetin). It is also an inulin source.

An ethanol extract of *A. lappa* seeds inhibited COLO 205 colorectal carcinoma with IC50 11.80 µg/mL while sparing human skin fibroblasts at IC50 1485 µg/mL — a selectivity index of about 126 — with caspase-3 up 4.07-fold, caspase-9 up 3.66-fold, [[p53]] up 2.69-fold, [[Bax]] up 22.44-fold, Bcl-2 down 13.33-fold, [[MMP]]-2 and -9 down about 1.3-fold, NF-κB down, and IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α reduced; molecular docking placed arctigenin on TGF-βR1 (Hassan et al., *Biomed Res Ther* 2025, 11:960). In Jurkat leukemic T cells, the ethyl acetate root extract gave IC50 102.2 ± 42.4 µg/mL with raised caspase-3/7 and altered mitochondrial membrane potential, and no extract was cytotoxic to 3T3 cells (Don & Yap, *Biomed Pharmacother* 2019, 110:918–929). Root extracts also showed selective antiproliferative activity against K562, MCF-7 and 786-O with dichloromethane fractions the only active ones, while hydroethanolic extracts were the strongest radical scavengers (Bian et al., *BMC Complement Altern Med* 2011, 11:25, PMC3073957). A 2021 study of burdock root against AGS, HT-29 and KYSE-30 reported apoptosis induction and TLR-4/AKT/ERK upregulation, with the authors noting that in vivo work was still needed (Anticancer Research of Iranian ethnopharmacological plants, PMC8653648).

Note that the burdock literature has already hit the same extract-dependence trap: the solvent fraction determines whether the extract is cytotoxic or merely antioxidant, which is exactly the variable that makes a "root extract" an undefined product.

## Flavaone and polyphenol roots

### Scutellaria baicalensis root (baicalin, baicalein, wogonin)

Scutellaria root is a flavonoid root with direct mechanistic overlap with dandelion's STAT3 and PI3K/Akt findings. The ethanol root extract reduced viability and colony formation in four EGFR-TKI-resistant human lung cancer lines (H1299 wild-type but TKI-resistant, H1975 acquired, PC9/ER erlotinib-resistant, PC9/GR gefitinib-resistant), induced nuclear fragmentation and PARP and caspase-3 cleavage, and dephosphorylated [[STAT3]]; overexpression of constitutively active STAT3 reversed the apoptosis (Liu et al., *Int J Mol Sci* 2021, 22:5181). Baicalin and baicalein act through PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK and NF-κB in digestive-system tumours, with baicalein showing p53-dependent G1 arrest and in vivo suppression of HT-29 xenografts (Jang et al., *Int J Mol Sci* 2023, 24:1954). A meta-analysis of 12 preclinical HCC studies reported significant reductions in transplanted tumour weight for every component form: baicalein SMD −4.80 (95 % CI −6.66 to −2.95), baicalin −2.28, wogonin −1.41, whole plant −0.86 (Ma et al., *Ann Med* 2024, 56:27, PMC10795786).

The clinical status is the usual one for a flavonoid: poor solubility and low oral bioavailability have kept baicalin in preclinical work, with nano-delivery systems under development (Baicalin review, *Naunyn-Schmiedeberg's Arch Pharmacol* 2026). The one genuinely clinical Scutellaria product is PHY906/YIV-906, a multi-herb formulation containing *S. baicalensis* alongside *Coptis* and *Phellodendron*, which has been tested as a chemotherapy adjuvant; the trial record is in pancreatic, colorectal and hepatic malignancy, and the efficacy signal claimed in review literature is for reduced chemotherapy toxicity rather than added tumour response.

The vault already holds [[Curcumin]], which shares the polyphenol-root archetype and the same bioavailability constraint, and [[Quercetin]], [[Apigenin]] and [[Luteolin]] for the constituent-level view.

### Turmeric rhizome (curcumin)

Curcumin's overlap with dandelion is on the signaling axis rather than the death-mechanism axis: P300/CBP HAT inhibition, [[NF-κB]] suppression, PI3K/Akt and MAPK damping, and induction of p16-dependent senescence at 10–40 µM. The vault's [[Curcumin]] note records the potency ceiling (oral bioavailability under 1 %) and the standard formulation responses (liposomal, Meriva, theracurmin, piperine co-administration). Notably, curcumin's senescence-inducing activity is the inverse of dandelion's pro-death-autophagy profile: dandelion drives cancer cells into autophagy-dependent death, curcumin drives them into senescence.

### Ginger rhizome (6-gingerol, 6-shogaol)

Ginger is the closest match to dandelion on the specific node dandelion suppresses. In gastric cancer cells, 6-gingerol enhanced TRAIL-induced viability reduction by inhibiting TRAIL-induced NF-κB activation, which down-regulated cIAP1 and increased caspase-3/7 activation; 6-shogaol alone reduced viability by damaging microtubules and inducing mitotic arrest (Ishiguro et al., *Biochem Biophys Res Commun* 2007, 362:218, PMID 17706603). In renal carcinoma Caki cells, 6-shogaol sensitised to TRAIL through ROS-mediated cytochrome c release and down-regulation of cFLIP-L (Han et al., *Chem Biol Interact* 2015, 228:69, PMID 25619640) — mechanistically the same node as dandelion's cFLIP suppression and withanolide E's cFLIP degradation. In TRAIL-resistant Huh7 liver cancer cells, 6-shogaol attenuated autophagy flux (raising p62 and LC3-II) and sensitised to TRAIL through p53 and ROS (Nazim & Park, *Int J Mol Med* 2019, 43:701). It also inhibits PI3K/Akt/mTOR and blocks Akt directly (Huang et al., 2021; Pei 2021), with reported selectivity: at 80 µM it was toxic to SW480 and SW620 colon cancer cells at 95 % and 90 % while reducing WI38 fibroblast viability by only 17 % (6-shogaol review, *Chin Med* 2023, 18:139).

## Fructan roots: chicory as the microbiome analogue

Dandelion root's dominant compound by mass is inulin (16.1 g/100 g dry weight in defatted root), and the prebiotic route to anticancer effect is a different mechanism from the cytotoxic triterpenoid route. Chicory root (*Cichorium intybus*) is the industrial source of purified inulin and the best-characterised root for this axis, with 14.9–18.3 % inulin fresh weight.

The human data for the microbiome route are better developed than for any direct-cytotoxicity root. A randomised double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in healthy adults with mild constipation consuming 12 g/day of chicory-derived inulin for 4 weeks found that the only genera consistently affected were *Bifidobacterium*, *Anaerostipes* and *Bilophila*, with reduced *Bilophila* abundance linked to improved constipation-related quality of life (Vanhoutvin et al., *Gut* 2017, 66:1968). In a 2,2-dimethylhydrazine-induced rat colorectal carcinogenesis model, inulin alone suppressed aberrant crypt foci formation by 51.2 % and a galacto-oligosaccharide-plus-inulin combination by 55.8 %, with better short-chain fatty acid and bacterial enzyme outcomes than either alone (Qamar et al., *Nutrients* 2016, 8:465). In mice, chicory root flour produced cancer-protective and prebiotic effects distinguishable from those of purified inulin, with most effects stronger in males than females (Pouille et al., *J Funct Foods* 2020, 104174).

A 2023 review is explicit that the sign of the inulin effect on colorectal cancer depends on context: inulin-type fructans produce predictable microbiome shifts and are beneficial for chemoprevention, but the same fermentability can aggravate colitis in susceptible individuals, so the axis is not uniformly protective (Gastroenterology Report, 2023, doi:10.1093/gastro/goae058). The vault's [[Inulin]] note already covers the prebiotic and nano-delivery roles.

## Quassinoid roots and bark: Brucea javanica and Ailanthus altissima

Quassinoids are a structurally distinct class — C20 quassinoids with a fused lactone ring — that share dandelion's selectivity profile for tumour cells and its low-nanomolar potency range.

*Brucea javanica* fruit and seed oil (BJOE) is used in China as an intravenous adjuvant to chemotherapy. A meta-analysis of 1399 lung cancer patients treated with BJOE alongside a docetaxel-cisplatin regimen reported improved treatment effect (RR 1.36, 95 % CI 1.23–1.51), quality of life (RR 2.11, 95 % CI 1.66–2.67) and side-effect profile versus the chemotherapy regimen alone (Mei et al., 2021, cited in Nutrients 2025, 17:3285). No randomised evidence for the isolated quassinoids exists, and the review literature states plainly that clinical data do not support the quassinoid compounds themselves. In vitro, BJ root and bark fractions and isolated bruceacanthinones gave A549 IC50 of 50.0 ± 5.2 to 80.5 ± 1.8 µg/mL, less potent than the crude extract; bruceantin-class quassinoids inhibited MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 with IC50 0.063–0.238 µM, and 3-OH glycosylation reduced cytotoxicity (Jiang et al., *Fitoterapia* 2015, PMID 26071073). Brusatol plus cisplatin suppressed A549 xenograft growth more effectively than cisplatin alone.

*Ailanthus altissima* root bark and bark yield ailanthone, a quassinoid with reported activity in melanoma, AML, bladder, lung, breast, gastric, prostate and vestibular schwannoma lines; it induces apoptosis and G0/G1 arrest in MCF-7 with caspase-3 and Bax up and Bcl-2 down (Wang et al., *Oncol Lett* 2018, 15:6022, PMC5840722), and in gastric cancer organoids it represses base-excision repair via p23 down-regulation, with disease-free survival in the high-p23 versus low-p23 groups used as the clinical correlate (Wang et al., *Int J Biol Sci* 2021, 17:3471, PMC8326126).

## The cautionary analogue: Echinacea root

One root preparation in this class has been shown to work against the chemotherapy rather than with it, and it is worth recording because it is the same direction of error as dandelion's estrogenicity.

*Echinacea angustifolia* root extract fractions increased cancer cell growth in the presence of doxorubicin: at 0.05 and 0.5 µM doxorubicin, cynarine increased HeLa cell growth by 48–125 % and 29–101 %, and chicoric acid increased growth by 23–100 %; in MCF-7, the ethyl acetate fraction increased growth by 20–25 % and chicoric acid by 10–15 % (Huntimer et al., *Chem Biodivers* 2006, 3:695, PMID 17193302). The same root species, however, is cytotoxic on its own: hexanic root extracts of *E. pallida*, *E. angustifolia* and *E. purpurea* reduced viability of MIA PaCa-2 pancreatic and COLO320 colon cancer cells, with *E. pallida* IC50 46.41 µg/mL and 10.55 µg/mL respectively and apoptosis via caspase-3/7 and DNA fragmentation (Chicca et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2007, 110:148, PMID 17052874). *E. purpurea* root extract reduced A549 lung cancer viability with dose and time dependence, early apoptosis and sub-G1 accumulation, and a selectivity index above 2 versus MRC-5 fibroblasts (Hosami et al., *BMC Complement Med Ther* 2021, 21:37, PMC7809807).

The pattern — a root that is protective of tumour cells against a cytotoxic drug while being cytotoxic on its own — is the exact failure mode to check for in any root preparation proposed alongside chemotherapy.

## Cross-cutting findings

### The bioavailability wall, not the mechanism, is the discriminating variable

Across every plant in this note that reached a human trial, the constraint was the same one dandelion root extract has not yet been tested against:

| Agent | Route | Plasma detection | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parthenolide (feverfew) | Oral, 1–4 mg/day | Not detectable at 4 mg/day (LOD 0.5 ng/ml) | Phase I could not complete pharmacokinetics |
| Withaferin A (ashwagandha root) | Oral, up to 216 mg/day | Not detectable in any of 42 samples | Phase I could not characterise exposure |
| Dandelion root extract | Oral | No pharmacokinetic study published | No target-concentration estimate exists |
| ACT001 (parthenolide derivative) | Oral | Linear exposure, t½ 3–4 h | Phase 1 efficacy signal in glioma became reportable |
| Minnelide (triptolide prodrug) | IV and oral phosphate prodrug | Exposure measurable; triptolide measurable in plasma | Phase 1 and phase 2 results publishable, with DLTs |

Two independent routes around the wall exist: chemical derivatisation into a bioavailable prodrug or semi-synthetic analogue (ACT001, Minnelide), and delivery engineering (nanoparticles, liposomes, micelles, phytosome). Neither has been attempted for dandelion root extract, whose lactone and triterpene classes are chemically amenable to both — the α-methylene-γ-lactone of taraxacin and the triterpene skeleton of taraxasterol are exactly the handles used in the parthenolide and triptolide derivative programmes.

### Extract standardisation is a prerequisite, and the literature shows it is often ignored

The same root can be cytotoxic or antioxidant depending on the solvent fraction (Arctium lappa: dichloromethane fractions antiproliferative, hydroethanolic fractions radical-scavenging; dandelion: aqueous DRE kills colon cancer cells while the 2008 leaf/flower/root comparison found root and flower extracts had no effect on growth of MCF-7/AZ or LNCaP cells). Product content also varies by plant part, season, tree age and provenance (Magnolia root bark parthenolide 0–8.68 % across 967 samples; *T. wilfordii* preparations varying between manufacturers). Any comparative claim between root botanicals that does not specify solvent, part, and assayed content is not interpretable.

### The selectivity story is more fragile than the case reports imply

Dandelion's most-cited result is the >95 % selective programmed cell death in colon cancer cells at 48 h. Three of the analogues reviewed here show the same result being complicated by context: Echinacea root protects tumour cells from doxorubicin; the same breast cancer stem cells that respond to dandelion extract in 2D require roughly seven times the concentration in 3D spheroids; and *Echinacea*'s own cytotoxicity appears only in specific lines and fractions. Selectivity is a property of the preparation-line pairing, not of the plant.

### The estrogenicity and drug-interaction constraint is not unique to dandelion

Dandelion's CYP1A2/3A4 inhibition, ~50 % reduction of dasatinib bioavailability in rats, and estrogenic activity in MCF-7 and immature rats are properties of its phenolic acids and flavonoid fraction. The equivalents elsewhere: *T. wilfordii*'s narrow therapeutic window with dose-limiting neutropenia and cerebellar toxicity at high exposure; *E. angustifolia* root's interference with doxorubicin; ACT001's NF-κB and STAT3 suppression coupled with reported CD4+ Treg reduction in patients, which is an immunomodulatory effect that must be reconciled with concurrent PD-1 therapy. Every root in this class has an interaction profile, and the interactions are usually with the co-administered drug rather than with the tumour.

## Tier 5 hypotheses worth stating as such

These are mechanistically motivated but untested, and are labelled as hypotheses rather than findings.

1. **A sesquiterpene-lactone-standardised root blend** would be expected to reproduce dandelion's extrinsic, FADD-dependent apoptosis with cFLIP suppression, given that taraxacin, parthenolide and withanolide E all converge on the same node. The prediction is falsifiable: cFLIP western blot plus dominant-negative FADD and IETD-fmk rescue in the same model.
2. **Combined dandelion root extract plus a parthenolide source** would be expected to show additive rather than synergistic cFLIP suppression, on the assumption that taraxacin and parthenolide act on the same downstream node rather than on distinct targets. The 2016 dandelion observation that the individual triterpenes α-amyrin, β-amyrin and lupeol were less active than the unfractionated extract, and additive rather than synergistic in combination, is the relevant precedent.
3. **A triterpene-standardised root blend** (dandelion taraxasterol plus *Arctium* lupeol plus *T. wilfordii* celastrol) would be expected to act on the same pentacyclic-triterpene targets, with additive rather than synergistic cytotoxicity — the same pattern the Ovadje group reported for the individual dandelion triterpenes.
4. **CHKA/phosphocholine interference would be expected to be additive with glycolysis-targeting agents** rather than to substitute for them, since the dandelion extract and the taraxasterol hexokinase data implicate two different rate-limiting steps of the [[Warburg Effect]].
5. **A fructan-rich root would be expected to contribute only through the microbiome axis**, not through direct cytotoxicity. The predicted signature is short-chain-fatty-acid and bile-acid changes with no effect on tumour-cell viability in vitro. The chicory-versus-purified-inulin divergence in mice is the direct test of this.
6. **Any root with documented estrogenic activity would be expected to be contraindicated in hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer regardless of its cytotoxicity**, on the strength of the *T. mongolicum* estrogenic data and the fact that the dandelion preclinical evidence base is dominated by breast and TNBC lines.
