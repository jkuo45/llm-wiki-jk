# Eryptosis — excess death signaling and under-clearance; evidence synthesis

Scope: mechanism-curated summary of [[Eryptosis]] as the regulated cell death (RCD) of mature erythrocytes, its position in longevity/anti-aging arguments, the quantified (and unquantified) share of erythrocyte turnover attributable to it, and the two failure modes — accelerated eryptosis and insufficient eryptosis/under-clearance. Evidence tiers: Tier 1 = human RCT/intervention; Tier 2 = human cohort/association; Tier 3 = rodent/in-vivo animal; Tier 4 = in-vitro or ex-vivo human blood/cells; Tier 5 = hypothesis. No Tier 1 evidence exists for eryptosis modulation as an intervention.

## Mechanistic core (brief)

Mature erythrocytes are anucleate and organelle-free; they cannot execute mitochondrial apoptosis. [[Eryptosis]] is defined by Ca²⁺-dependent execution: cytosolic Ca²⁺ entry through cation channels (TRPC6, NMDA/AMPA, [[Piezo1]], Cav2.1) activates the [[Gardos Channel|Gardos channel (KCNN4)]] → K⁺/Cl⁻/water efflux → cell shrinkage; inhibits flippase and activates [[Scramblase]] → [[Phosphatidylserine|phosphatidylserine (PS)]] externalization; activates calpain → cytoskeletal proteolysis/blebbing. PS-positive cells are removed by efferocytosis within **minutes** (senescent cells: days), so PS positivity in aged cells denotes eryptotic activation. Eryptosis is non-lytic, membrane-intact, and PS exposure is anti-inflammatory; [[Hemolysis]] is the pro-inflammatory accidental alternative whose blockade by prior eryptotic clearance is the principal physiological rationale. See [[_document_ - Current understanding of eryptosis mechanisms, physiological functions, role in disease, pharmacological applications, and nomenclature recommendations|Tkachenko et al., Cell Death Dis 2025;16:467]] and the [[Eryptosis]] note for the full signalling map.

## Fraction of cells dying by this route

A normal-population fraction of erythrocytes undergoing eryptosis is **not quantified** in the cited literature; do not cite one. What is quantified:

- Erythrocyte lifespan ≈ **120 days**; turnover ≈ **~1% of red-cell mass per day** (commonly reported as ~2–3 × 10⁶ cells s⁻¹). Eryptotic removal is the stressed-cell subset of this flux; [[Erythrocytes]] note and Tkachenko et al. 2025.
- Baseline PS exposure ≈ **0.5–0.6%** of erythrocytes in reference cohorts (report from PV/ET literature; not an eryptosis-specific assay — annexin V binding also marks other states).
- Aged cells are more eryptosis-prone (oxidation-induced PS exposure), and PS-positive senescent cells are scored as eryptotic (Tkachenko 2025).
- Disease raises the fraction measurably: e.g. lead-exposed workers 2.82% vs 0.1% PS-positive (vault capture of occupational data); 3-fold increase with PD-peritonitis day 1 vs controls; eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² → higher eryptosis vs eGFR ≥ 60 (p < 0.001) (Clementi 2022).

Interpretation: eryptosis is the dominant RCD of erythrocytes but the split between eryptotic clearance and senescent-antigen/IgG clearance at steady state is unpublished. Any figure other than "not quantified" is unsupported by the cited sources.

## Position in longevity / anti-aging arguments

Eryptosis is a quality-control clearance arm, not a senescence regulator sensu stricto. The defensible longevity framings:

- **Prevention of lytic, pro-inflammatory disposal.** [[Hemolysis]] releases Hb/heme/ATP/IL-33/HSP70 → TLR4/NF-κB endothelial activation, NETs, complement recruitment, heme-driven necroptosis/HO-1 ferroptosis/NLRP3 pyroptosis, renal tubular hemoprotein injury. Eryptosis removes compromised cells before lysis; PS externalization is anti-inflammatory and macrophage uptake is pro-resolving (HO-1 up-regulation, pro-inflammatory cytokines down; experimental intracerebral hemorrhage). Tier 4–3.
- **Systemic redox and erythrocyte energy metabolism as a longevity readout.** [[Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase|G6PD]]/PPP → NADPH → glutathione reductase; energy depletion → PKCα → Ca²⁺; AMPK restrains eryptosis (AMPKα-null mice: splenomegaly, anemia — Tier 3); NO → sGC → cGMP → [[cGMP-Dependent Protein Kinase I|cGKI]] suppresses eryptosis (eNOS-null mice clear RBCs faster — Tier 3); [[Erythropoietin|EPO]] inhibits eryptosis acutely but sustained high EPO increases eryptosis-prone cells (Tier 2 cohort/Tier 4).
- **Aging of the erythrocyte compartment raises susceptibility.** Susceptibility to eryptosis increases with cell age, partly via oxidative sensitivity; NAC blocked eryptosis in vitro and prolonged mouse RBC half-life in vivo (Tier 3–4). Human translation unreported.
- **Host defense.** *Plasmodium*-infected RBCs are cleared by eryptosis; G6PD deficiency, sickle trait, thalassemia trait confer partial protection by lowering the eryptotic threshold (Tier 2). This bounds how aggressively eryptosis should be suppressed.
- **Biomarker framing.** Eryptosis indexes oxidative/uremic/inflammatory load; correlations with MPO, Cu/Zn SOD, IL-6 (Clementi 2022; Tier 2) and with sepsis endotoxin activity/IL-6 (Tier 2). Useful as exposure/severity marker; not validated as a longevity surrogate endpoint.

What eryptosis does NOT imply for longevity: no evidence that modulating eryptosis extends human lifespan or healthspan; all intervention data are animal/ex-vivo. Treat anti-eryptotic pharmacology as preclinical.

## Consequences of accelerated eryptosis

### Anemia and erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) hypo-responsiveness
- CKD/ESRD: RBC lifespan shortened to **60–90 d** (Frontiers Pharmacol 2025; PMID 41383483); anemia prevalence rises with CKD stage; >90% of dialysis patients. ESA/iron correction is suboptimal in roughly half of treated patients. Higher inflammation, parathyroid hormone, lower albumin, ferritin correlated with eryptosis biomarkers (Gok 2022; PMID 35469112). Proc: eGFR < 60 vs ≥ 60 → higher eryptosis (p < 0.001); eryptosis correlates with MPO (ρ = 0.77, p = 0.01), IL-6 (ρ = 0.52, p = 0.05).
- Anemia sequelae in CKD association cohorts: impaired quality of life, cardiovascular complications, disease progression, cognitive impairment, insomnia, higher mortality (Bissinger, FEBS J 2019). Tier 2 — associational, not causal.
- Eryptosis contributes across: cancer (anemia driven by turnover; cisplatin/topotecan/tamoxifen/sorafenib/sunitinib pro-eryptotic), heart failure, hepatic failure, sepsis, malaria; "anaemia of chronic disease" repeatedly tracks eryptosis.

### PS-mediated procoagulant activity
- Externalized PS provides the catalytic surface for prothrombinase assembly (factor X activation); in some pathological conditions RBCs contribute up to **~40% of thrombin generation** (Tkachenko 2025; report, no primary figure re-derived here).
- RBC-derived microvesicles expose PS and trigger thrombin generation via **factor XII (contact pathway), tissue-factor-independent** (van der Meijden 2012; J Thromb Haemost 10:1355–62).
- PS-exposing RBCs bind platelets (CD36 / PS receptors) and endothelium (**CXCL16/SR-PSOX**, CD36, thrombospondin-1). Adhesion under arterial shear rates demonstrated ex vivo; attenuated by annexin V or CXCL16 blockade/silencing (Borst 2012; Am J Physiol Cell Physiol 302:C644–51).
- Cirrhosis: PS on blood and endothelial cells shortens coagulation time, raises thrombin/fibrin formation (Wu 2016; Liver Int 36:1800–10).

### Endothelial dysfunction and uptake
- PS/CXCL16-mediated adhesion; subsequent **uptake of eryptotic cells by endothelial cells, with induction of endothelial ferroptosis** (Tkachenko 2026; Biochim Biophys Acta Mol Cell Res 1873(5):120161; PMID 42155670). Tier 4→3 mechanistic; human outcome data absent.
- Concurrent pro-resolution arm: macrophage phagocytosis of eryptotic cells reprograms toward pro-resolving phenotype; hemorrhagic stroke context (HO-1 dependent). Net in-vivo sign depends on compartment (endothelium/immune pro-inflammatory vs macrophage pro-resolution).

### Microcirculatory impairment
- Adhesion → microaggregates → reduced capillary flow; documented in sickle cell disease and diabetes; implicated in Long COVID fibrin-amyloid microclots (hypothesis) and hemolytic uremic syndrome.
- In CKD ESRD review: eryptotic cells may adhere to vascular wall and impair microcirculation; risk amplified when ESA doses forced to high targets (Lang 2017; Kidney Blood Press Res 42:749–60).

### Inflammatory signaling — dual arm
- Pro-resolution: efferocytosis, HO-1 up-regulation, cytokine suppression (intracerebral hemorrhage models; Tier 3).
- Pro-inflammatory: eryptosis correlates with IL-6, MPO, CRP (peritonitis, appendicitis, sepsis); EVs shed from eryptotic RBCs carry DAMPs (found in stored red-cell concentrates); hemolysis if clearance-saturated → full DAMP cascade (TLR4/complement/NETs/inflammasome).

### Feed-forward loop (mechanistic description, not a clinical entity)
Oxidative stress / uremic toxins / energy depletion → cation-channel opening → Ca²⁺ → PS externalization → accelerated efferocytosis → anemia → hypoxia / ESA escalation → more circulating eryptotic-prone cells; concurrently PS/endothelium adhesion → microvascular compromise → local hypoxia and ROS → further channel activation. Each link reported individually; end-to-end loop not experimentally closed in humans.

## Consequences of insufficient eryptosis or under-clearance

Two distinct deficits are often conflated:

- **Insufficient death signaling** — damaged cells fail to initiate eryptosis (or it is suppressed pharmacologically).
- **Under-clearance** — eryptosis executes (PS exposure, shrinkage) but efferocytosis is saturated or blocked, so PS-positive cells remain in circulation.

The documented consequences fall into two arms.

### Damaged cells persist → hemolysis cascade
- [[Hemolysis]] releases Hb, heme, ATP, IL-33, HSP70. Free Hb scavenges [[Nitric Oxide|NO]] → vasoconstriction, platelet activation, endothelial dysfunction; free heme drives Fenton chemistry, TLR4/NF-κB endothelial activation, TNF-α release, NET formation, complement recruitment, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and heme-driven programmed death of endothelial/immune cells (necroptosis, HO-1-dependent ferroptosis, pyroptosis). Tier 4/3; hemolysis pathophysiology is well established in human disease (sickle cell, thalassemia, transfusion reactions, mechanical valves).
- Renal filtering of released hemoproteins causes tubular injury; haptoglobin and hemopexin buffering is saturable. Tier 2/4.
- [[Bilirubin]] and bile-acid excess in hepatic failure create a vicious cycle with erythrocyte Ca²⁺ influx/ceramide formation → more eryptosis, not less — an example of the two failure modes co-existing.

### PS-positive cells persist → procoagulant and pro-adhesive burden
- If efferocytosis is saturated (massive synchronized cell death, as after oxidant exposure) or impaired (Kupffer-cell dysfunction, stabilin-1/2 signalling defects, or EV clearance overload), PS-exposing RBCs remain in circulation. They retain the procoagulant surface and adhesive phenotype described above (CXCL16/SR-PSOX, CD36), so the thrombotic/microcirculatory risk persists instead of resolving within minutes. Tier 4; direct human outcome data absent.
- RBCs do not clear locally; hepatic sinusoid stabilin-1/2 recognition and Kupffer-cell uptake dominate (80% of RBC-derived vesicles removed within 5 min experimentally). Reduced hepatic clearance or transfusion of stored concentrates (which accumulate PS-positive EVs and DAMP-carrying microvesicles) can prolong PS exposure in recipients; clinical consequences are unquantified. Tier 3–4/2 (storage lesion association).
- CD47–SIRPα is the constitutive "don't-eat-me" brake; aged RBCs normally downregulate it. Pathological CD47 retention or SIRPα signalling imbalance would delay clearance; direct clinical correlates are unverified. Tier 5/4.

### Host-defence consequence
- *Plasmodium*-infected erythrocytes depend on eryptosis for clearance; suppression leaves infected cells circulating. This is the principal in-vivo cost of anti-eryptotic therapy, alongside unresolved interference with cytostatic tumour apoptosis. Tier 3/2 (epidemiology of protective G6PD, sickle, thalassemia traits).

### Clinical status
- A defined human "under-eryptosis" syndrome is **not established**. The failure mode manifests as hemolysis (when cells lyse rather than being cleared) or, less often measured, as persistent PS-positive cells after massive oxidative/uremic insult. No biomarker-based diagnostic threshold or interventional trial exists. Treat as a mechanistic counterweight, not a recognized indication.

## Evidence limits and translation gaps

- No human RCT of anti-eryptotic therapy exists; ESRD efficacy of eryptosis inhibitors (NAC, NO/cGMP axis, ion-channel modulators, EPO) is unestablished; interventional claims are Tier 3–4 only.
- The under-clearance arm is inferred from hemolysis physiology and clearance-pathway cell biology, not from an interventional human cohort; saturation of hepatic/Kupffer clearance and transfusion-related PS/EV burden are unquantified clinically.
- Normal-population eryptotic fraction: not quantified. Annexin V also binds non-eryptotic states; assay standardization is an active recommendation of Tkachenko 2025.
- Causality: most disease associations are cohort/ex-vivo; confounding by uremia, inflammation and ESA dose is rarely fully adjusted.
- Suppression risks: malaria parasite clearance; tumor-cell apoptotic synergism with cytostatics (unresolved); hemolysis protection lost; EV-DAMP burden uncertain.

## Bottom line

Eryptosis is the non-lytic, Ca²⁺/PS-executed quality-control clearance of damaged erythrocytes and the erythrocyte tier of the same damaged-cell-removal principle that apoptosis serves in nucleated tissue. It fails in two directions. **Excess** converts protection into pathology: anemia with ESA hypo-responsiveness, PS-driven thrombin generation and microvascular obstruction, endothelial adhesion with ferroptotic injury, and a pro-inflammatory skew at the endothelium and in sepsis. **Insufficient eryptosis / under-clearance** leaves damaged or PS-positive cells to lyse or persist: NO scavenging, heme/TLR4 inflammatory injury, renal hemoprotein nephropathy, and a procoagulant/pro-adhesive burden without timely macrophage uptake; the clearest in-vivo cost is loss of *Plasmodium* clearance and increased hemolysis-driven vascular injury. Both failure modes converge on anemia and vascular dysfunction but by different routes. Clinically, the excess arm is documented across CKD/ESRD, diabetes, heart failure and cirrhosis; the under-clearance arm is mechanistically supported but lacks a defined human syndrome or interventional data. The normal eryptotic fraction is not quantified; all intervention data are preclinical. For longevity/anti-aging, eryptosis is best treated as a **biomarker of systemic oxidative/uremic/inflammatory load and an upstream driver of vascular/hematologic decline**, not as an established anti-aging target.
