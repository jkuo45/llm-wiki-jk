# Aldose reductase

## Overview

Aldose reductase (AKR1B1) is a cytosolic aldo-keto reductase that catalyzes the first and rate-limiting step of the [[Polyol Pathway]], reducing glucose to sorbitol using NADPH. Under normoglycemia its activity is modest, but during hyperglycemia up to one-third of cellular glucose may flow through this route, linking the enzyme to diabetic tissue injury.

## Mechanism & Pathways

The enzyme reduces glucose to sorbitol, which is then oxidized to [[Fructose]] by sorbitol dehydrogenase. Because sorbitol is poorly membrane-permeable, it accumulates osmotically in tissues lacking efficient sorbitol dehydrogenase, contributing to osmotic damage in lens, nerve, and kidney. The pathway also consumes NADPH, depleting the reducing equivalents needed for [[Glutathione]] regeneration and thereby promoting oxidative stress. Aldose reductase activity thereby feeds glycation: the more reactive [[Fructose]] produced feeds further [[Advanced Glycation End Products]] formation.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Aldose reductase is a well-established contributor to diabetic complications including neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, and cataract. It is a validated therapeutic target; aldose reductase inhibitors have been developed but shown mixed clinical efficacy. Notably, [[Aminoguanidine]] — best known as an AGE inhibitor — also inhibits aldose reductase, exemplifying the polypharmacology that complicates attribution of its effects in diabetic models.

> [!important]
> Aminoguanidine's simultaneous inhibition of AGE formation, aldose reductase, MAO, iNOS, and diamine oxidase means benefits and side effects cannot be attributed to a single molecular target.
