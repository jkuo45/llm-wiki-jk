# Argyria

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

Argyria is a permanent blue-gray discoloration of the skin, eyes, and mucous membranes caused by the systemic deposition of silver after chronic exposure to large doses of [[Silver]] or [[Silver compounds]]. Ingested or inhaled silver is absorbed into the circulatory system and transported to tissues as [[Silver sulfide albuminates|protein-bound silver sulfide]]. Within tissues, silver is either reduced to elemental metallic silver (by ascorbic acid or catecholamines) or precipitates as silver sulfide granules; both forms accumulate in the dermis, around sweat glands, and in basement membranes, producing the characteristic slate-blue pigmentation. The process is irreversible because the deposited granules are not readily cleared.

> [!info]
> Mild generalized argyria is sometimes mistaken for cyanosis; the bluish tint of argyria does not clear with oxygen and persists indefinitely.

## Clinical Presentation

The hallmark is progressive cutaneous pigmentation, most evident in sun-exposed areas, the sclera, and nail beds. Although cosmetically disfiguring and permanent, argyria is generally considered benign and does not otherwise harm health. Diagnosis is largely clinical, supported by a history of silver exposure (colloidal silver supplements, silver-containing topical agents, silver nanoparticles in consumer products) and sometimes skin biopsy showing silver granules.

## Therapeutics & Prevention

There is no effective treatment; laser therapy has been attempted with limited success. Prevention centers on avoiding chronic ingestion of silver products. The condition illustrates the systemic toxicology of silver beyond its antimicrobial applications, connecting the chemistry of [[Silver ion|silver ions]] to oxidative stress and tissue deposition.
