# Autophagosome-lysosome fusion

**Autophagosome-lysosome fusion** is the terminal degradative step of [[Autophagy]] in which double-membrane autophagosomes dock with and fuse to [[Lysosome]]s, delivering engulfed cytoplasm, damaged organelles, and protein aggregates for hydrolytic degradation. This event defines the completion of autophagic flux and is essential for clearance of cellular waste.

## Mechanism & Regulation

Fusion is executed by SNARE machinery, tethering complexes, and small GTPases, and requires an intact lysosomal membrane. The master regulator of this terminal step is [[TFEB]], a transcription factor that coordinates lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy gene expression through the CLEAR network. Because [[SIRT2]] stabilizes TFEB mRNA and [[SIRT1]] deacetylates TFEB to promote nuclear translocation, [[Sirtuins]] influence autophagosome-lysosome fusion through multiple post-translational mechanisms, linking NAD+-dependent signaling to the terminal degradative step of [[Macroautophagy]].

## Role in Senescence & Proteostasis

Efficient autophagosome-lysosome fusion is critical for clearing damaged proteins and organelles that accumulate during stress and aging. In senescent cells, autophagic dysfunction and impaired lysosomal fusion contribute to the accumulation of [[Lipofuscin]] and senescent cell debris, whereas boosting autophagic flux is considered a senomorphic strategy. TFEB-driven lysosomal biogenesis and fusion competence are therefore intimately linked to proteostasis and the maintenance of the senescent phenotype.

## Clinical Relevance

Impairments in autophagosome-lysosome fusion underlie lysosomal storage disorders and neurodegenerative proteinopathies. Strategies that activate TFEB or replenish NAD+ to sustain sirtuin function represent emerging interventions to restore autophagic clearance during aging.
