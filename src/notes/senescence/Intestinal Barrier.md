# Intestinal Barrier

The **Intestinal Barrier** is the physical and biochemical interface of the gut that separates the intestinal lumen from the systemic circulation. It comprises the intestinal epithelium, its intercellular tight junctions, the overlying mucus layer, and immune defenses, and is critical for preventing translocation of harmful bacteria and toxins into the bloodstream.

## Mechanism & Regulation

Barrier integrity depends on intact epithelial cells, well-formed tight junctions, goblet-cell-derived mucus, and local immune surveillance. Disruption of the barrier ("leaky gut") permits microbial product translocation and drives systemic [[Inflammation]]. The [[Gut Microbiome]] interacts bidirectionally with the barrier: dysbiosis impairs it, while beneficial bacteria and their metabolites (notably [[Short-chain Fatty Acids]]) reinforce it.

## NAD+ Precursors and Barrier Function

[[NMN]] and [[Nicotinamide Riboside]] improve intestinal barrier function. In colitis models, NMN increased the expression of tight-junction proteins and improved mucus secretion, attenuating intestinal mucosal permeability. Long-term NMN treatment increased goblet cell numbers, mucus thickness, and tight junction protein expression, maintaining the integrity of the intestinal epithelium. NAD+-precursor-activated [[Sirtuins]] also protect intestinal [[Endothelial Cells]] against oxidative injury, collectively reinforcing the barrier.

## Clinical Relevance

Barrier dysfunction is implicated in inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic endotoxemia, and age-related inflammation. Because the intestinal barrier connects the [[Gut Microbiome]] and [[Intestinal Stem Cell]] regeneration to systemic immune status, strengthening it via NAD+ metabolism is a promising strategy for dampening gut-derived inflammation in aging.
