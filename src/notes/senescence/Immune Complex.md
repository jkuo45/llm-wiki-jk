# Immune Complex

An **immune complex** is an aggregate formed when antibodies bind soluble antigens, typically comprising immunoglobulin and antigen in varying ratios. Immune complexes activate complement, engage Fc receptors on phagocytes, and deposit in tissues, driving type III hypersensitivity reactions such as serum sickness, rheumatoid vasculitis, and glomerulonephritis.

## Distinction from Innate Signaling Platforms

In the context of innate immunity, the term "immune complex" is sometimes loosely applied to [[Inflammasome]]s, but these are functionally distinct. Inflammasomes are cytosolic supramolecular multiprotein scaffolds, not antibody-based aggregates. They assemble in response to pathogens or sterile danger signals to activate inflammatory caspases (e.g., Caspase-1), cleaving pro-[[IL-1β]] and pro-[[IL-18]] and driving [[Pyroptosis]]. NLRP3-containing inflammasomes are the archetypal example.

## Intersection with Senescence & Inflammation

Inflammasomes serve as a key intersection between senescence, damage-associated molecular patterns, and [[Inflammation]]. Their activation links cellular damage signals to IL-1 family cytokine release, reinforcing the inflammatory milieu that sustains the senescent phenotype. The senescent cell secretome can itself prime or trigger inflammasome responses in immune and stromal cells.

## Clinical Relevance

True immune complexes cause classical autoimmune tissue injury, whereas inflammasome dysregulation contributes to autoinflammatory disease, gout, and chronic age-related inflammation. Disambiguating these two "complexes" is important for accurate mechanistic framing in immunosenescence research.
