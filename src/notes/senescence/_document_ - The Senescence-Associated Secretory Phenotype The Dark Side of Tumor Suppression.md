## Abstract

[[Senescence|Cellular senescence]] is a tumor-suppressive mechanism that permanently arrests cells at risk for malignant transformation. However, accumulating evidence shows that [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] can have deleterious effects on the [[Tumor Microenvironment|tissue microenvironment]]. The most significant of these effects is the acquisition of a [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] that turns senescent fibroblasts into proinflammatory cells that have the ability to promote [[Cancer|tumor progression]].

> [!info] Keywords
> aging, cancer, inflammation, proliferation, invasion

## INTRODUCTION

The [[Tumor Microenvironment|tissue microenvironment]] is defined by the phenotypes of the cells in the immediate area and by the physical and chemical properties of the soluble and insoluble factors surrounding cells within a given tissue. These properties include temperature and oxygen tension, as well as various molecules that may be produced locally—for example, growth factors and [[Cytokines|cytokines]]. Further, cells within tissues form a dynamic network that contributes to their microenvironment. At the same time, the tissue microenvironment regulates cell behavior. This reciprocal relationship determines tissue function and repair and is also central to a number of pathologies, including [[Cancer]].

A permissive microenvironment supports and promotes tumor growth and cancer cell aggressiveness ([^1] – [^2]). Alterations in the cellular and molecular composition of the connective tissues surrounding carcinomas allow tumors to evade detection by the [[Immune System|immune system]] as well as to proliferate inappropriately, invade the surrounding tissue structure, and eventually [[Metastasis|metastasize]]. The synergy between an altered microenvironment and the genetic alterations acquired by tumor cells allows these cells to evade preventive mechanisms and become fully malignant. [[Senescence|Cellular senescence]] is now recognized as a potent tumor-suppressive mechanism that arrests the growth of cells at risk for malignant transformation ([^3] – [^8]). However, recent studies show that [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] develop altered secretory activities that may induce changes in the [[Tumor Microenvironment|tissue microenvironment]], relaxing its control over cell behavior and promoting [[Tumorigenesis|tumorigenesis]] ([^9] – [^13]).

> [!important] Dual Role of Senescence
> How can the senescence response be both tumor suppressive and procarcinogenic? It is important to consider that a biological process such as [[Senescence|cellular senescence]] can be both beneficial and deleterious. The idea that processes can have such dual effects is consistent with a major evolutionary theory of aging termed [[Antagonistic Pleiotropy|antagonistic pleiotropy]] ([^14]). The [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] represents one of the darkest sides of the senescence response and is the focus of this review. We particularly emphasize the potential effects of the SASP ([^15]) on cell behavior in the context of tumor progression.

## CELLULAR SENESCENCE

[[Senescence|Cellular senescence]] occurs in culture and in vivo as a response to excessive extracellular or intracellular stress. The senescence program locks the cells into a cell-cycle arrest that prevents the spread of damage to the next cell generation and precludes potential malignant transformation ([^14]). [[Senescent Cells|Senescent cells]] have been shown to accumulate over the life span of rodents, nonhuman primates, and humans ([^16]). These cells are found primarily in renewable tissues and in tissues that experience prolonged [[Inflammation|inflammation]].

A plethora of stresses can provoke [[Senescence|cellular senescence]] ([^17], [^18]). These stresses include [[Telomere|telomeric dysfunction]] (telomere uncapping) resulting from repeated cell division (termed [[Replicative Senescence|replicative senescence]]), mitochondrial deterioration, [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], severe or irreparable [[DNA Damage|DNA damage]] and [[Chromatin|chromatin]] disruption (genotoxic stress), and the expression of certain oncogenes ([[Oncogene-Induced Senescence|oncogene-induced senescence]]) (see **[Figure 1](#F1)**) ([^19] – [^20]). Stresses that cause [[Senescence|cellular senescence]] can be induced by external or internal chemical and physical insults encountered during the course of the life span, during therapeutic interventions (for example, X-irradiation or chemotherapy), or as a consequence of endogenous processes such as oxidative respiration and mitogenic signals. External mitogenic signals, for example growth-related oncogene alpha ([[GROα]]/CXCL1) secretion by tumor cells in close proximity to normal cells ([^21]) or circulating angiotensin II ([^22], [^23]), have also been shown to induce [[Senescence|cellular senescence]]. All somatic cells that have the ability to divide can undergo senescence. Regardless of the disparate mechanisms of senescence-inducing stresses, the senescence program is activated once a cell has sensed a critical level of damage or dysfunction. So far, the senescence growth arrest has been shown to depend on the activities of the major tumor-suppressor pathways controlled by [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] and [[Retinoblastoma Protein|Rb (retinoblastoma protein)]], as well as by [[p53]]. Some of the molecules involved in pathways upstream and downstream of the [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] have been used as markers to detect [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] in culture and in vivo.

![Figure 1](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/049e/4166495/b9ce69bf2bad/nihms-627997-f0001.jpg)

Multiple types of stimuli can provoke [[Senescence|cellular senescence]] and a [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]]. When irreversible cell-cycle arrest is triggered by severe [[DNA Damage|DNA damage]] (i.e., dysfunctional [[Telomere|telomeres]] or oncogenic stress), the SASP occurs in [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]]. However, when a senescent-like phenotype is triggered in cells that overexpress cell-cycle inhibitors such as [[p16 INK4a|p16]] or [[p21 CIP1|p21]], cells undergo a growth arrest with many characteristics of senescent cells, but not a SASP.

## THE SECRETORY PHENOTYPE OF SENESCENT CELLS

The senescent phenotype is not limited to an arrest of cell proliferation. In fact, a [[Senescent Cells|senescent cell]] is a potentially persisting cell that is metabolically active and has undergone widespread changes in protein expression and secretion, ultimately developing the [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]]. This phenotype has also been termed the [[Senescence-Messaging Secretome|senescence-messaging secretome]] ([^24]). We recently provided a large-scale characterization of the SASP, using antibody arrays to quantitatively measure factors secreted by human fibroblasts and epithelial cells ([^13]), as well as mouse fibroblasts (J.P. Coppé & J. Campisi, unpublished data). The potential existence of the SASP was already suggested by large-scale comparative gene (mRNA) expression studies performed on [[Fibroblasts|fibroblasts]] from different-aged donors and different tissues of origin ([^25] – [^28]). Among the cells that have been shown to senesce and secrete biologically active molecules are liver stellate cells ([^29]), [[Endothelial Cells|endothelial cells]] ([^25], [^30] – [^31]), and epithelial cells of the retinal pigment, mammary gland, colon, lung, pancreas, and prostate ([^5], [^13], [^25], [^26], [^32] – [^34]).

Senescence-associated changes in gene expression are specific and mostly conserved within individual cell types. Most differences between the molecular signatures of presenescent and senescent cells entail cell-cycle- and metabolism-related genes, as well as genes encoding the secretory proteins that constitute the [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]]. The SASP includes several families of soluble and insoluble factors (see **[Table 1](#T1)**). These factors can affect surrounding cells by activating various cell-surface receptors and corresponding signal transduction pathways that may lead to multiple pathologies, including [[Cancer]]. SASP proteases can have three major effects: (*a*) shedding of membrane-associated proteins, resulting in soluble versions of membrane-bound receptors, (*b*) cleavage/degradation of signaling molecules, and/or (*c*) degradation or processing of the ECM. These activities provide potent mechanisms by which [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] can modify the [[Tumor Microenvironment|tissue microenvironment]]. In the following sections, we discuss these SASP subsets and some of their known paracrine effects on nearby cells, with an emphasis on their ability to facilitate [[Cancer|cancer progression]].

### Soluble Signaling Factors as Major Components of the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype

[[Senescent Cells|Senescent cells]] secrete interleukins, inflammatory [[Cytokines|cytokines]], and growth factors that can affect surrounding cells.

#### IL-6

The most prominent cytokine of the [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] is [[IL-6]], a pleiotropic proinflammatory cytokine (see **[Figure 2](#F2)**). [[IL-6]] has been shown to be associated with [[DNA Damage|DNA damage]]– and oncogenic stress–induced senescence of mouse and human keratinocytes, [[Melanocytes|melanocytes]], monocytes, [[Fibroblasts|fibroblasts]], and epithelial cells ([^12], [^13], [^35], [^36]). Further, [[IL-6]] secretion appears to be directly controlled by persistent DNA-damage signaling ([[ATM]] and CHK2), independent of the [[p53]] pathway ([^37]). Through [[IL-6]] expression, [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] can directly affect neighboring cells that express the IL-6R (gp80) and gp130 signaling complex at their surface, such as [[Epithelial Cells|epithelial]] and [[Endothelial Cells|endothelial cells]] of various functions and origins.

![Figure 2](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/049e/4166495/c95c8a49be4b/nihms-627997-f0002.jpg)

Human fibroblasts, either presenescent (PRE) or senescent (SEN), were immunostained for the inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-6 and [[IL-8]], as well as the senescence marker [[p16 INK4a|p16]]. Cells were made senescent either by replicative exhaustion (REP) or ionizing radiation (IR) or by expression of oncogenic [[RAS]] (RAS).

#### IL-1

Another interleukin signaling pathway demonstrated to be upregulated by [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] is that of [[IL-1|IL-1]] ([^38], [^39]). Both [[IL-1α]] and [[IL-1β]] are overexpressed and secreted by senescent [[Endothelial Cells|endothelial cells]] ([^40]), [[Fibroblasts|fibroblasts]] ([^41], [^42]), and chemotherapy-induced senescent epithelial cells ([^33]). These cytokines can affect neighboring cells through the cell-surface receptors (IL-1 receptor/Toll-like receptor superfamily), which act primarily to trigger the nuclear factor kappa B and activating protein 1 pathways ([^43]).

#### Chemokines (CXCL and CCL)

Most [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] overexpress [[IL-8]] (CXCL-8) (see **[Figure 2](#F2)**), along with [[GROα]] and GROβ (CXCL-1 and -2; the murine CXCL-1 is named KC) ([^36], [^44], [^45]). CCL family members that are generally upregulated in senescent cells include MCP-2, -4, and -1 (CCL-8, -13, and -2); HCC-4 (CCL-16); eotaxin-3 (CCL-26); and macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-3α and -1α (CCL-20, -3). MCP-3 (CCL-7) is overexpressed by senescent liver stellate cells and by prostate and skin fibroblasts. Fibroblasts induced to senesce by oncogenic [[RAS]] secrete high levels of MCP-3 as well as I-309 (CCL-1). In addition, both fibroblasts induced to senesce by [[RAS]] and stellate cells induced to senesce by liver damage secrete high levels of another two members of the CXCL family, GCP-2 (CXCL-6) and ENA-78 (CXCL-5). Overexpression of PF-4 (CXCL-4) and SDF-1 (CXCL-12) was observed in senescent prostate fibroblasts ([^28], [^46]). Recently, it was shown that cells undergoing [[Oncogene-Induced Senescence|oncogene-induced senescence]] secrete multiple CXCR-2 (IL-8RB)-binding chemokines ([^11]). It was proposed that [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] activate a self-amplifying secretory network in which CXCR-2-binding chemokines reinforce growth arrest.

> [!tip] Self-Amplifying SASP Network
> CXCR-2-binding chemokines (IL-8, GROα, GROβ) secreted by senescent cells activate an autocrine loop through [[CXCR2]], reinforcing both growth arrest and the SASP — a positive feedback mechanism that locks in the senescent phenotype.

#### IGF pathway

The insulin-like growth factor ([[IGF]])/IGF receptor network may also contribute to the effect [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] exert on their microenvironment. Senescent endothelial, epithelial, and fibroblast cells express high levels of almost all the IGF-binding proteins ([[IGFBP]]-2, -3, -4, -5, and -6) ([^13], [^47], [^48]) and their regulators, IGFBP-rP1 and -rP2 [also known as connective tissue growth factor ([[Connective Tissue Growth Factor|CTGF]])] ([^27], [^49]). Recently, activation of the [[BRAF]] oncogene in primary fibroblasts was shown to lead to the secretion of IGFBP-7, which acts through autocrine/paracrine pathways to induce senescence and [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] in neighboring cells ([^50]).

#### Other soluble factors

There are additional soluble factors associated with the SASP. For example, inflammatory cytokines such as the colony-stimulating factors (CSFs, including [[GM-CSF]] and G-CSF) are secreted at high levels by senescent fibroblasts ([^13]). In addition, osteoprotegerin, a secreted decoy receptor for tumor necrosis factor alpha, is present at high levels in the extracellular milieu of senescent fibroblasts. Other molecules upregulated at senescence include [[Prostaglandin E2|prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)]] ([^35], [^51]) and Cox-2, the enzyme responsible for the production of PGE2 and other prostaglandins.

### Extracellular Proteases as an Important Subset of the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype

In addition to secreting soluble signaling cytokines and growth factors, [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] also secrete proteases such as matrix metalloproteinases ([[MMP-9|MMPs]]).

#### MMP family

The MMP family members that are consistently upregulated in human and mouse fibroblasts undergoing replicative or stress-induced senescence are stromelysin-1 and -2 ([[MMP-3|MMP-3]] and MMP-10, respectively) and collagenase-1 ([[MMP-1|MMP-1]]) ([^52] – [^54]). In some instances, the MMP-1 and -3 produced by senescent cells ([^55]) can also regulate the activity of the soluble factors present in the SASP. For example, these MMPs can cleave MCP-1, -2, and -4 and [[IL-8]] ([^56]). A variety of other CXCL/CCL family members that constitute the SASP can also be cleaved by [[MMP-9|MMP-9]], MMP-2, or MMP-7. These CXCL and CCL cytokines can originate from neighboring cells, such as leukocytes or tumor cells ([^57]).

#### Serine proteases and their inhibitors

Another family of proteases involved in carcinogenesis and present in the SASP comprises serine proteases and regulators of the plasminogen activation pathway. Members of this family include urokinase- or tissue-type plasminogen activators ([[uPA/uPAR|uPA or tPA]], respectively), the uPA receptor ([[uPA/uPAR|uPAR]]), and inhibitors of these serine proteases ([[PAI-1|PAI-1 and -2]]) ([^58]). Indeed, a *>*> 50-fold increase in plasminogen activator activity has been reported in senescent endothelial cells and lung and skin fibroblasts ([^59], [^60]). [[PAI-1]] is also upregulated in fibroblasts and endothelial cells from aged donors ([^61] – [^62]). Like the CXCR-2 cytokines, [[PAI-1]] also seems to reinforce the senescence growth arrest ([^63]).

### Extracellular Insoluble Molecules

[[Fibronectin]] is a large multidomain glycoprotein found in connective tissue, on cell surfaces, and in plasma and other body fluids. It interacts with a variety of macromolecules, including cell-surface receptors, components of the cytoskeleton, and other ECM molecules. Through its interactions with cell-surface receptors, primarily integrins, fibronectin can affect cell adhesion, survival, growth, and migration. [[Fibronectin]] production is upregulated in premature aging Werner syndrome fibroblasts ([^64]). Moreover, cells undergoing senescence in culture and in vivo ([^65]) increase fibronectin expression.

### Nonprotein Secretions

As a result of senescence-induced changes in cellular metabolism, [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] may exert influences on tissue microenvironments due to the secretion of molecules other than proteins. These molecules include [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species]] and transported ions. For example, senescent cells have been shown to release nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species due to alterations in inducible nitric oxide synthase, endothelial nitric oxide synthase, and superoxide-dismutase activities ([^66] – [^67]). These reactive molecules are known modulators of cellular phenotype, such as the differentiation of monocytes. In addition, these molecules can enhance cancer cell aggressiveness and can promote [[Aging|aging]] and age-related degeneration ([^68], [^69]).

## SPECIFICITY OF THE SENESCENCE-ASSOCIATED SECRETORY PHENOTYPE

Despite the fact that a significant number of factors increase their secretion upon senescence, the [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] is not a general or nonspecific upregulator of secretion. The levels of expression of many secreted factors do not change when cells senesce. Interestingly, among these unchanged secreted molecules are anti-inflammatory soluble factors such as IL-2, -4, -10, -11, and -12 ([^13]). Fractalkine (CX3CL-1), GCP-2, GITR, PDGF-BB, and LIGHT (all essential to leukocyte differentiation or proliferation) also remain unchanged when fibroblasts are induced to senesce by X-irradiation, [[RAS]] overexpression, or replicative exhaustion. Intriguingly, no factor was significantly downregulated in different senescent states ([^13]).

Despite a specific, conserved core of upregulated and unchanged secreted molecules, different senescent states appear to display some unique features. Whereas cells induced to senesce by replicative exhaustion, telomere disruption, X-irradiation, or chromatin disruption seem to express closely related SASPs ([^13], [^37]), fibroblasts induced to senesce by oncogenic [[RAS]] oversecrete more [[GM-CSF]], [[IL-6]], -7, [[IL-8]], -1β, -13, and [[GROα]] than do cells induced to senesce by other means. Moreover, such cells secrete high levels of factors such as ENA-78, I-309, G-CSF, and interferon (IFN)-γ that are not secreted by other senescent cells. By contrast, cells induced to senesce by overexpression of the [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] tumor-suppressor protein do not express a SASP despite other hallmarks of senescence (J.P. Coppé, F. Rodier & J. Campisi, unpublished data). Cells that senesce with dysfunctional [[p53]] develop a SASP that resembles the SASP caused by oncogenic [[RAS]] ([^13]). Thus, although a core of SASP factors is a feature of all senescent cells (with the exception of [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]]-induced senescence), there are variations in the quantity and quality of the SASP that depend on the cell type and senescence inducer.

Another feature of the SASP is its dynamic development over time ([^13]). In culture, cells develop a full SASP *>*> 5 days after senescence induction, and the cells' growth arrests within 24 h of damage. Not all SASP factors begin to be secreted at the same time. This gradual phenotypic transition is a feature conserved between cell types and senescence inducers. Genetic alterations, such as loss of [[p53]] or gain of oncogenic [[RAS]], lead to a more rapid acquisition of the SASP, suggesting that the SASP is a specific program triggered by genotoxic stress.

Finally, mouse senescent fibroblasts also display a SASP. Under standard cell-culture conditions, which include 20% oxygen, mouse cells undergo an arrest that has been termed senescence but that does not include a SASP. By contrast, under physiological 3% oxygen, the mouse SASP closely resembles the human SASP. These findings suggest that the senescent cell secretome is specific and evolutionarily conserved (J.P. Coppé & J. Campisi, unpublished data).

## REGULATORY MECHANISMS OF THE SENESCENCE-ASSOCIATED SECRETORY PHENOTYPE

### Intracellular Signaling, Transcription, and Chromatin: Locking in the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype

Overall, the gene (mRNA) expression profiles of [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] determined by microarrays resemble the profiles of secreted proteins determined by antibody arrays (18; J.P. Coppé & J. Campisi, unpublished data). This finding suggests that the secretory phenotype of senescent cells is at least partly regulated at the transcriptional level. However, because the changes in gene expression that occur at senescence are so widespread, the transcriptional control may well be at the level of [[Chromatin|chromatin]] organization, rather than due to changes in specific transcription factors. In fact, dramatic chromatin alterations are known to occur at senescence ([^70] – [^71]). Further support for the idea that gene expression specific to the senescence program may be partially attributed to larger changes in chromatin conformation is suggested by the physical clustering of genes that constitute the SASP (J.P. Coppé & J. Campisi, unpublished data).

Our data suggest that a large proportion of the SASP of senescent fibroblasts is irreversible once established ([^13]). Indeed, senescent human fibroblasts that express low levels of [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] can revert and resume proliferation upon [[p53]] inactivation ([^72]). These reverted cells, however, retain the SASP ([^13]). This may imply that, once senescence is established, unknown mechanisms—potentially related to chromatin alterations—permanently lock the SASP in an irreversible open chromatin confirmation, analogous to the way the [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]]/[[Retinoblastoma Protein|Rb]] pathway is proposed to lock growth-promoting genes into a heterochromatic state ([^73]). Another implication of these findings is that the senescence-associated cell-cycle arrest and the SASP can be uncoupled (see **[Figure 1](#F1)**). Further, the SASP is a more permanent characteristic of senescence than is the growth-arrested state.

### Cell-Nonautonomous Tumor Suppressors: The Guardians of the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype

The [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] tumor suppressor is a positive regulator of the [[Retinoblastoma Protein|Rb]] tumor-suppressor pathway. High levels of ectopic expression of [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] induce senescence. Induction of endogenous [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] is associated with tumor prevention and the general age-associated decline in stem cell and tissue function ([^74] – [^75]). Although [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] is a very efficient inducer of cell-cycle arrest (including the senescence-associated arrest), [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] does not seem to play a major role in the development of the SASP (J.P. Coppé, F. Rodier & J. Campisi, unpublished data). Cells induced to senesce by ectopic [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] expression secrete significantly lower levels of SASP factors compared to cells induced to senesce by most other senescence inducers. Senescence induced by [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] has potential therapeutic applications. As an example, [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] gene therapy for [[Rheumatoid Arthritis|rheumatoid arthritis]] was demonstrated to efficiently stop the disease evolution and decrease the inflammatory state ([^76]). In [[Cancer]], such an approach could take advantage of both the cell-autonomous properties (inhibition of cell growth) and the cell-nonautonomous properties (senescence arrest without a SASP) of [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]]. That is, [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]] induction/delivery could actively suppress cell proliferation without triggering the proinflammatory SASP.

The [[p53]] tumor suppressor can also promote [[Aging|aging]] ([^77], [^78]) and senescence ([^4], [^7], [^8]) in mice (see **[Figure 3](#F3)**). Along with mutations in [[p16 INK4a|p16^INK4a]], mutations leading to [[p53]] inactivation occur very frequently in cancer cells; that is, [[p53]] is well known to act as a cell-autonomous tumor suppressor by controlling [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] and cell-cycle arrest, both in culture and in vivo. However, [[p53]] mutations have also been found in the stromal vicinity of carcinomas, and this [[p53]]-deficient stroma was shown to promote [[Tumorigenesis|tumorigenesis]] ([^21]). These data suggest that [[p53]] may have beneficial cell-nonautonomous effects in preventing [[Cancer|cancer development]] ([^21], [^79]). Strikingly, we found that [[p53]] actively restrains the SASP, suggesting a potential mechanism by which [[p53]] may suppress [[Tumorigenesis|tumorigenesis]]—that is, by restraining the development of a protumorigenic/proinflammatory [[Tumor Microenvironment|tissue microenvironment]] ([^13]). Thus, loss of [[p53]] activity by senescing or damaged fibroblasts greatly enhances the SASP and the stimulatory effects of the SASP on malignant epithelial cells (as discussed further below) ([^13]).

![Figure 3](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/049e/4166495/6244fcc2828a/nihms-627997-f0003.jpg)

The [[DNA Damage Response|DNA damage signaling pathway]] leads to the activation of the [[p53]] tumor suppressor. Activated [[p53]] triggers cell fate decisions, such as senescence or [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. Depending on the cell context, [[p53]] can suppress [[Cancer]] through transient cell-cycle arrest and activation of the DNA-repair machinery. Additionally, [[p53]] restrains the [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]]. Regulation of the SASP by [[p53]] suggests a cell-nonautonomous function of this tumor suppressor. In the short term, the SASP may promote tissue repair. In the long term, it may promote chronic [[Inflammation|inflammation]], which in turn can drive [[Cancer]] and [[Aging|aging]].

## EFFECTS ON CELL BEHAVIOR

Factors secreted by [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] can promote tumor development in vivo and malignant phenotypes such as proliferation and invasiveness in cell-culture models. These effects have been observed in a number of tissues, including breast ([^9], [^13], [^53], [^54], [^80]), skin ([^81]), [[Prostate Cancer|prostate]] ([^13], [^82]), [[Pancreatic Cancer|pancreas]] ([^83]), and oropharyngial mucosa ([^10]). The effects of the complex SASP are, of course, dependent on the tissue context. Thus, different models show different effects of the SASP. In the following sections, we discuss in greater detail the various behavioral changes cells can undergo when residing in the proximity of senescent cells and how the senescent [[Tumor Microenvironment|tissue microenvironment]] can facilitate tumor initiation and progression.

### The Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype Promotes Cell Proliferation

One of the most protumorigenic effects of the SASP is to promote the proliferation of epithelial cells.

#### Breast cancer

In the case of breast epithelial cells, senescent human fibroblasts can stimulate the growth of premalignant and malignant mammary epithelial cells ([^9], [^13], [^53]). This stimulation may be due in large measure to secretion of [[GROα]], which is a prominent SASP component (J.P. Coppé & J. Campisi, unpublished data). Irradiated stromal cells, which are presumed to be senescent, have been shown to perturb mammary epithelial microenvironment and to fuel inappropriate epithelial cell growth in the [[Mammary Gland|mammary gland]] ([^80], [^84]). Furthermore, MMPs secreted by senescent fibroblasts have been shown to be responsible for the higher tumorigenicity of breast epithelial cell xenografts in mice, most likely by allowing mitogenic and chemotactic signals greater access to breast cancer cells ([^54], [^80]). In addition to secreted soluble factors, there is evidence that the matrix laid down by senescent cells can also stimulate mammary epithelial cell growth ([^9]).

#### Prostate cancer

Fibroblasts from the human prostate gland that undergo senescence in culture have been shown to create a local tissue environment that favors [[Prostate Cancer|prostate epithelial cell]] hyperproliferation, in part owing to amphiregulin secretion ([^28]). Furthermore, [[Connective Tissue Growth Factor|CTGF]] (or IGFBP-rP2) is upregulated in senescent fibroblasts ([^27]), and this protein was shown to regulate [[Prostate Cancer|prostate tumor progression]] in xenografts and to be expressed by the cancer-associated reactive stroma ([^85]). Whereas upregulation of CXCR-4 is observed in most cancer cells, only senescent stromal cells of the prostate display high levels of expression of its ligand SDF-1α (CXCL-12). Thus, SDF-1α secretion by senescent fibroblasts may therefore play a selective role in fueling [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer]]. It has recently been determined that senescence induced by irradiation in [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer patients]] is associated with a significantly increased release of exosome-like microvesicles ([^86]). This novel secretory phenotype depends on the activation of [[p53]]. Finally, the propensity of [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer patients]] to relapse after chemotherapy may be due to the accumulation of senescent tumor cells with inflammatory characteristics ([^13]).

#### Other carcinomas

In the skin, unidentified factors secreted by human fibroblasts were shown to be capable of inducing clonal expansion of [[Keratinocytes|keratinocytes]] ([^87]). In addition, senescent endometrial fibroblasts promoted anchorage-independent epithelial cell growth, primarily because of [[IL-1|IL-1]] oversecretion ([^42]). In the orobucal cavity, tobacco-driven senescence of supportive stromal cells was shown to stimulate the hyperplastic growth of epithelial cells and was associated with the loss of tight junctions and epithelial integrity ([^10]).

#### Melanoma

[[Melanoma|Melanocytic nevi (moles)]] are often composed of senescent [[Melanocytes|melanocytes]] that are induced owing to oncogenic mutations in [[BRAF]] (V600E mutations) ([^6]). Only rare cell variants in nevi can evolve into [[Melanoma|melanoma]]. Malignant [[Melanocytes|melanocytes]] express high levels of the CXCR-2 receptor ([^88]) and can be stimulated to grow by its ligands [[GROα]] ([^89]) and [[IL-8]] ([^90]). Given that both [[GROα]] and [[IL-8]] form part of the core SASP, the senescent microenvironment may therefore stimulate the proliferation of rare premalignant cells in nevi, thereby leading to the development of [[Melanoma|melanoma]].

#### Other tumor-associated cells

During [[Angiogenesis|angiogenesis]], [[Endothelial Cells|endothelial cells]] can undergo proliferation, which is stimulated by [[VEGF]], [[IL-8]], I-309, and eotaxin ([^91] – [^92]). The proangiogenic effects of the SASP were shown in vivo in mouse xenografts of breast cancer cells. The blood vessel density was significantly higher when the tumors developed in the presence of senescent but not presenescent fibroblasts ([^92]). [[RAS]]-driven tumors are also known to contain significant numbers of senescent cells ([^5]), and these tumors are also highly vascularized ([^93]). Many of the SASP factors can also affect leukocyte proliferation during the course of [[Cancer|cancer development]]. For example, IL-7 directly promotes lymphocyte proliferation in peripheral tissues, and [[GM-CSF]] stimulates myeloid suppressor cells, which are known to have important immunosuppressive functions that affect [[Cancer|cancer progression]] ([^94]).

### The Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype Stimulates Cell Motility (Invasion, Migration, Metastasis)

[[Senescent Cells|Senescent cells]] secrete an array of factors that can create a gradient to promote cell migration and invasion.

#### Epithelial cells

In [[Pancreatic Cancer|pancreatic cancer]], [[HGF]], and to a lesser degree basic fibroblast growth factor ([[bFGF]]), promotes cancer cell invasion in culture and can potentially drive cancer dissemination in vivo ([^83], [^95]). In [[Breast Cancer|breast cancer]], high levels of [[IL-6]] and [[IL-8]] secreted by senescent fibroblasts are responsible for enhancing the invasiveness of a panel of cancer cell lines in cell-culture models ([^13]). Moreover, the secretion of MMP-2 and -3 by senescent cells can also promote the invasion of multiple epithelial cell types ([^53], [^54], [^80], [^96], [^97]). Other proteases, such as [[uPA/uPAR|uPA]] and its regulator ([[PAI-1|PAI-1]]), are likewise implicated in cancer cell invasion. Senescent stromal cells may promote an [[Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition|epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition]] ([^13]), which is an important phenotypic switch that enables cancer cells to migrate and invade. Through use of co-cultures of smokeless tobacco extract–exposed fibroblasts and human epidermal [[Keratinocytes|keratinocytes]], factors secreted by extract-modified fibroblasts increased the invasiveness of partially transformed epithelial cells in conjunction with a loss of [[E-cadherin|E-cadherin]], zonula occludens 1, and involucrin expression ([^10]). Thus, senescent cells and the SASP can induce phenotypes in nearby human epithelial cells that are common during [[Cancer|cancer progression]].

#### Endothelial cells

In cell-culture models, [[Endothelial Cells|endothelial cells]] are induced to migrate by factors secreted by senescent fibroblasts ([^92]). This is in part due to [[VEGF]] secretion and chemokine gradients set up by senescent cells ([^98]). [[Angiogenesis|Neoangiogenesis]], which is dependent on endothelial cell motility and invasion, is enhanced in xenograft models containing senescent fibroblasts ([^92]). Further, it is known that IL-1, a SASP component, activates the endothelium and consequently increases the adhesive potential of cancer cells to vessel walls ([^99]). Thus, senescent cells may promote extravasation of cancer cells to secondary [[Metastasis|metastatic sites]]. However, the effects of senescent cells on [[Angiogenesis|angiogenesis]] may depend on cell type. For example, senescent keratinocytes oversecrete maspin, which displays paracrine antiangiogenic activity and acts as a dominant inhibitor of endothelial cell migration ([^100]).

#### Leukocytes

Senescent fibroblasts may promote leukocyte recruitment because they chronically release chemokines ([^101]). In [[p53]]-deficient [[RAS]]-driven tumors induced to senesce through reestablishment of [[p53]] function ([^8]), [[Innate Immune Cells|innate immune cells]] were shown to migrate into the vicinity of the senescent tumor area. CSF-1, [[CXCL1]], or [[CCL2]] and [[ICAM-1]] transcripts were found to be higher in these senescent tumor masses, and they may be responsible for the immune response. For example, [[Neutrophils|neutrophils]] express CXCR-1, -2, and -4 to sense their microenvironment and to invade tissues; [[Eosinophils|eosinophils]] use the broad-spectrum receptor CCR-3 to fulfill their function; monocytes use CCR-1, -2, and -5, CXCR-4, and CX3CR1 to extravasate and enter peripheral sites, where they differentiate; [[Natural Killer Cells|natural killer cells]] express CCR-2 and -5, CXCR-4, CX3CR1, and XCR1; and immature myeloid dendritic cells display CCR-1, -2, -5, and -6 and CXCR-4, which facilitate their transport, migration, and function ([^101] – [^102]).

### The Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype Regulates Cell Differentiation

The factors secreted by senescent cells can alter the differentiation status of neighboring cells.

#### Epithelial cells

Senescent human and mouse fibroblasts disrupt the differentiation of mammary epithelial cells and inhibit the expression of differentiation markers ([^53], [^80]). This activity is due in large measure to the secretion of MMP-3 by the senescent cells. Furthermore, weakly tumorigenic pancreatic ([^83]) and mammary ([^13]) epithelial cells undergo morphologic changes in culture that resemble an [[Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition|epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition]] in the presence of a senescent conditioned medium. The effect on mammary epithelial cells is attributable to [[IL-6]] and [[IL-8]] ([^13]), as well as to [[HGF]], [[uPA/uPAR|uPAR]], and MMPs ([^53]), all of which can disrupt epithelial cell clusters and stimulate dedifferentiation in culture and in vivo ([^103] – [^104]).

#### Endothelial cells

Strikingly, no angiostatic factors have been reported among SASP constituents (e.g., IFN-γ, TSP-1, MIG, PF4, IP-10, IL-4 and -12, and [[endostatin]]). This contrasts with the largely proangiogenic profile of the SASP (which includes [[IL-8]], [[CCL2|MCP-1]] and -2, GROs, [[Prostaglandin E2|PGE2]], [[VEGF]], EGF, CSFs, u-/tPA, MMPs, [[Fibronectin]], and laminin) ([^105]). Furthermore, there may be an amplifying activation loop because senescent stromal cells secrete MCPs, CSFs, MIPs, GROs, and CXCLs, which in turn recruit inflammatory and immune cells that also secrete proangiogenic factors (VEGFs, [[IL-8]], and MMPs). Thus, senescent cells are poised to support the differentiation of a new vasculature around and within a progressing [[Cancer|tumor]].

### The Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype Affects Leukocyte Infiltration and Tumor Immunology

No anti-inflammatory factors (e.g., IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-3, and IL-5) are significantly secreted by senescent fibroblasts, and some of these factors are even downregulated upon senescence (e.g., IL-2 and -12). Nonetheless, some reports show that massive amounts of either [[CCL2|MCP-1]] or [[IL-8]], which are prominent components of the SASP, lead to tumor destruction ([^106], [^107]). Senescent fibroblasts may influence the macrophage balance in the [[Tumor Microenvironment|tumor environment]]. Molecules that are implicated in the recruitment and differentiation of circulating monocytes to tumor sites also happen to be overexpressed by senescent fibroblasts ([^108]). These molecules can lead to an inadequate immune response within the close proximity of senescent cells. Senescent fibroblasts may affect lymphocytic populations infiltrating the tumor. Specific [[T Lymphocyte|T cell]] populations associated with tumor progression (i.e., Th2 and regulatory T cells) respond to inflammatory cytokines that are commonly present in the fibroblast SASP. Other cells of the specific and innate immune system, such as [[Natural Killer Cells|natural killer cells]], [[Neutrophils|neutrophils]], eosinophils, dendritic cells, and B cells, are also subject to regulation by cytokines that are produced by senescent fibroblasts.

## CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Most insoluble components of the ECM are enzymatic targets of secreted proteases. Therefore, the senescence-associated changes in proteolytic activities could affect the physical properties of the tissue structure around cells. In particular, the accumulation of senescent cells could lessen the supportive role of the ECM, globally diminishing tissue tension and elasticity. In addition, the relaxed tissue structure and higher levels of MMPs may help tumor cells migrate and invade through the ECM, thus enabling [[Metastasis|metastasis]]. The panel of proteases secreted by senescent cells extensively overlaps with those found in malignant tumors. Further, senescence-induced alterations in the secretion of interleukins, chemokines, growth factors, proteases, and associated processing activities tend to establish the SASP as protumorigenic.

> [!warning] The Dark Side of Tumor Suppression
> The SASP represents a fundamental paradox in cancer biology: the same senescence program that initially suppresses tumors by arresting damaged cells can, over time, create a chronically inflamed, pro-tumorigenic microenvironment through the SASP. This has profound implications for [[Therapy-Induced Senescence|cancer therapy]], where treatment-induced senescence may paradoxically promote relapse and progression.

Overall, [[Senescence|senescence]] is a molecular program with a unique phenotypic outcome. How its extracellular molecular signature is activated and maintained and the extent to which it influences the tissue milieu in healthy tissues, aged tissues, and diseased tissues are some of the many questions that remain unanswered. However, even with our currently limited knowledge of the SASP and its potential effects on carcinogenesis, promising new strategies for [[Cancer]] therapies are possible. For example, restoring the activity of tumor-suppressor proteins is an attractive, potentially powerful therapeutic approach. Taking into account our present understanding of the cell-nonautonomous effects of tumor-suppressor genes such as [[p53]], small chemicals that can pharmacologically restore their normal function would help reestablish the proper tissue and cell signals, thereby stimulating cancer regression ([^109] – [^110]). Such approaches could stimulate cancer elimination for two reasons: First, they would limit [[Inflammation|inflammation]] and thus possibly allow proper tissue repair; second, they would directly promote the immune-mediated clearance of cells that drive [[Cancer|cancer progression]].
