# Axonal Degeneration

**Axonal degeneration** is the disintegration of the axon, the long projection of a [[Neuron]], that occurs after mechanical injury, excitotoxic insult, or in chronic [[Neurodegenerative Disease]] such as [[Alzheimer's Disease]] and [[Parkinson's Disease]].

## Molecular Mechanism
Axonal degeneration is an early event in acute cerebral injury and chronic neurodegeneration. It is caused in part by [[Excitotoxicity]], during which neurons undergo strong [[NAD+]] depletion. A key executor is [[SARM1]], an NAD+-depleting enzyme that consumes NAD+ to promote axonal degeneration after injury, thereby decreasing overall NAD+ content. This SARM1-dependent NAD+ loss is a conserved feature of the program historically described as [[Wallerian Degeneration]].

## NAD+ and Neuroprotection
Preserving axonal NAD+ to supply damage-induced SARM1 activation is one of two proposed mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of [[Nicotinamide Riboside]], which protects neurons from excitotoxicity-induced axonal degeneration. By supporting NAD+ salvage and biosynthesis (via [[NAMPT]] and NMNAT), NR maintains the NAD+ pool that opposes SARM1-driven axon loss.

> [!info] NAD+-SARM1 Axis
> SARM1 uses NAD+ as a substrate to execute axonal destruction; agents that maintain axonal NAD+ (e.g., [[Nicotinamide Riboside]]) suppress this cascade, positioning the NAD+–SARM1 axis as a druggable target for axonoprotection.

## Clinical Relevance
Axonopathy contributes to disability in stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegenerative conditions, where axon loss precedes cell body death. Strategies that preserve axonal NAD+ are therefore of broad therapeutic interest in neurology.
