# Dyslipidemia

**Dyslipidemia** refers to abnormal levels of blood lipids, including elevated LDL cholesterol and triglycerides or reduced [[HDL]], and is a major modifiable risk factor for [[Atherosclerosis]] and [[Cardiovascular Disease]].

## NAD+ Precursors and Lipid Metabolism
[[Niacin|Nicotinic acid]] (NA, niacin) lowers blood lipid levels and is used to treat dyslipidemia, although it is implicated with flushing at doses higher than 50 mg/day. In contrast, nicotinamide (NAM) does not affect lipid blood levels, yet can exhibit sirtuin-inhibiting effects at higher doses, while [[Nicotinamide Riboside]] (NR) does not cause flushing because it does not activate GPR109A (the niacin receptor that mediates vasodilatory flushing).

> [!info] Precursor-Specific Pharmacology
> The distinct physiological responses of the [[NAD+]] precursors NA, NAM, and NR, despite all three raising NAD+ and NADP+ levels, are important for selecting the appropriate precursor for specific therapeutic indications. Niacin targets dyslipidemia; NR offers NAD+ elevation without flushing.

## Mechanisms
Niacin's lipid-lowering effects involve reduced hepatic VLDL and LDL production and inhibition of adipose tissue lipolysis via GPR109A, while also raising HDL. By contrast, NR and NAM primarily modulate NAD+-dependent enzymes such as the [[Sirtuins]], linking NAD+ metabolism to metabolic and vascular health.

## Clinical Significance
The choice among NAD+ precursors depends on the intended indication: niacin for classic [[Dyslipidemia]] management, and NR for raising NAD+ to support [[Sirtuins]] and cardiometabolic health without the flushing side effect. Both pathways intersect with lipid metabolism and [[Energy Balance]].
