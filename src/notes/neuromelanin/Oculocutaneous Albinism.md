# Oculocutaneous Albinism

[[Oculocutaneous Albinism]] (OCA) comprises a genetically and phenotypically heterogeneous group of autosomal recessive disorders affecting [[Melanogenesis|melanin biosynthesis]]. Global incidence is estimated at approximately 1:17,000–1:20,000 live births, with higher prevalence in certain populations (e.g., ~1:1,500 in sub-Saharan Africa for OCA2). All forms of OCA result from defects in melanosome biogenesis, tyrosinase function, or downstream melanin synthesis enzymes, leading to variable degrees of hypopigmentation of the skin, hair, and eyes.

## Types and Genetics

OCA is classified into at least seven subtypes (OCA1–7), each arising from biallelic mutations in distinct genes along the melanogenic pathway.

**OCA1** (~40% of OCA worldwide) is caused by mutations in the [[Tyrosinase]] gene (TYR, 11q14.3). Tyrosinase catalyzes the rate-limiting hydroxylation of [[L-Tyrosine]] to [[L-DOPA]] and the subsequent oxidation to dopaquinone. OCA1A represents the most severe form: complete absence of tyrosinase activity results in pure white hair, white skin, and pink/blue irides that remain unchanged throughout life. OCA1B, in contrast, retains partial tyrosinase activity, allowing accumulation of some pigment over time — hair may darken to blond or light brown and skin may tan minimally.

**OCA2** is the most common subtype globally, particularly prevalent in individuals of African descent (incidence up to 1:1,500 in parts of sub-Saharan Africa). It results from mutations in the OCA2 gene (formerly the P gene, 15q12-q13), which encodes the P protein — a melanosomal transmembrane protein essential for melanosome pH regulation and tyrosine transport. Phenotype ranges from minimal pigment to moderate pigmentation that can increase with age.

**OCA3** (rufous/red albinism), due to mutations in TYRP1 (9p23), which encodes [[Tyrosinase-related protein-1]] — a melanosomal enzyme involved in eumelanin synthesis. It is most commonly observed in individuals of African or Puerto Rican descent, presenting with reddish-bronze skin, ginger-red hair, and blue or brown irides.

**OCA4** is caused by mutations in SLC45A2 (5p13.2), encoding a putative melanosomal solute transporter. Common in Japanese and other East Asian populations. Phenotypically similar to OCA2.

**OCA5–7** are rarer subtypes:
- OCA5: mutations in LRMDA (4q24), encoding leucine-rich melanocyte differentiation-associated protein.
- OCA6: mutations in DCT (13q32.2), encoding [[Dopachrome tautomerase]]/TYRP2.
- OCA7: mutations in SLC24A5 (15q21.1), encoding a potassium-dependent sodium/calcium exchanger critical for melanosomal ion homeostasis.

## Clinical Presentation

**Skin and hair:** Hypopigmentation is the hallmark, ranging from complete absence of pigment (OCA1A) to nearly normal skin tone (some OCA2 and OCA4). Affected individuals are exquisitely sensitive to ultraviolet (UV) radiation, with sunburns occurring after minimal sun exposure. Chronic sun damage leads to solar elastosis, lentigines, and actinic keratoses. The lifetime risk of both [[Cutaneous Melanoma]] and non-melanoma skin cancer (basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma) is substantially elevated, necessitating lifelong dermatologic surveillance.

**Ocular manifestations** are present in all OCA subtypes and include:
- **Nystagmus** — involuntary, usually horizontal, pendular or jerk nystagmus that may improve slightly with age.
- **Photophobia** — marked discomfort in bright light due to deficient iris pigmentation and consequent transillumination.
- **Reduced visual acuity** — typically 20/60 to 20/400, resulting from [[Foveal hypoplasia]] (absence of a normal foveal pit and cone-rich specialization).
- **Strabismus** — esotropia or exotropia due to misrouting of retinogeniculostriate projections.
- **Iris transillumination** — iris pigment epithelium deficiency allows light to pass through the iris.
- **Optic nerve fiber misrouting** — an excessive decussation of temporal retinal fibers at the chiasm leads to abnormal binocular visual evoked potentials.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on characteristic cutaneous and ocular findings. Genetic testing via multigene panels or whole-exome sequencing confirms the subtype and enables accurate genetic counseling. Historically, the **hair bulb tyrosinase assay** — incubation of plucked anagen hair bulbs with [[L-Tyrosine]] or [[L-DOPA]] to detect pigment formation — differentiated OCA1A (negative) from OCA1B and other subtypes (positive or partially positive), though it has largely been superseded by molecular testing. Ophthalmic examination should include best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp examination for iris transillumination, fundoscopy for foveal hypoplasia, and visual evoked potentials to detect optic misrouting.

## Management

There is no curative therapy. Management is multidisciplinary and lifelong:

**Photoprotection:** High-SPF (≥50), broad-spectrum sunscreen applied liberally and frequently; sun-protective clothing, wide-brimmed hats, UV-blocking sunglasses; avoidance of peak UV hours; regular self-skin exams.

**Dermatologic surveillance:** Annual or semi-annual full-body skin examinations by a dermatologist to detect skin cancer at the earliest possible stage.

**Ophthalmologic care:** Corrective glasses or contact lenses for refractive errors; low-vision aids (magnifiers, telescopes, screen readers); tinted lenses or photochromic glasses for photophobia; strabismus surgery when indicated; nystagmus surgery (e.g., Kestenbaum procedure) may improve head posture and visual function in select cases.

**Genetic counseling:** Recurrence risk for siblings is 25%; carrier testing and prenatal/preimplantation diagnosis are available for families with known mutations.
