# Cathepsins

Cathepsins are a family of proteases (cysteine, aspartic, or serine proteases) primarily localized to [[Lysosome|lysosomes]], where they mediate bulk protein degradation and amino acid recycling. Eleven human cathepsins have been identified — B, C, D, E, F, G, H, K, L, S, V, W, and X/Z — each with distinct tissue distribution, substrate specificity, and physiological roles. They are optimally active at acidic pH (4.5–5.5), consistent with the lysosomal environment, and are synthesized as inactive proenzymes (zymogens) that require proteolytic removal of their propeptide for activation, typically upon arrival in the lysosome.

## Classification

Cathepsins are grouped by catalytic mechanism. **Cysteine cathepsins** (B, C, F, H, K, L, O, S, V, W, X) constitute the largest and most abundant subset. They share a conserved catalytic Cys-His-Asn triad and exhibit broad endopeptidase or exopeptidase specificity. **Aspartic cathepsins** (D, E) are pepsin-like enzymes that use a catalytic aspartic acid dyad and function optimally at low pH. **Serine cathepsins** (A, G) employ a serine nucleophile in their catalytic mechanism and are structurally distinct from the cysteine and aspartic groups. This mechanistic diversity underlies the wide functional repertoire of the cathepsin family.

## Physiological Function

**Lysosomal Protein Degradation.** The primary function of cathepsins is the non-selective turnover of intracellular and endocytosed proteins within lysosomes, supplying amino acids for recycling and cellular homeostasis.

**Antigen Processing.** Cathepsins B, D, L, and S play central roles in immunity by processing endocytosed antigens for loading onto [[MHC II|MHC class II molecules]] in antigen-presenting cells, thereby enabling CD4+ T-cell activation.

**Bone Remodeling.** [[Cathepsin K]] is abundantly expressed in [[Osteoclasts|osteoclasts]] and is the principal protease responsible for degrading type I [[Collagen|collagen]] during bone resorption. Its activity is essential for normal skeletal remodeling and calcium homeostasis.

**Prohormone Processing.** Cathepsin L participates in the limited proteolysis of prohormones and neuropeptide precursors within secretory vesicles, contributing to endocrine signaling.

**Apoptosis.** During [[Lysosomal membrane permeabilization]] (LMP), [[Cathepsin B]] and [[Cathepsin D]] are released into the cytosol, where they cleave [[Bid]] to generate truncated Bid (tBid), triggering mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and subsequent [[Caspase|caspase]] activation. This lysosomal–mitochondrial apoptotic axis represents a critical cell death pathway under stress conditions.

**Autophagy.** In [[Autophagy|autophagic]] flux, cathepsins mediate the degradation of autophagosomal cargo after autophagosome–lysosome fusion, a step essential for quality control of cytoplasmic components and nutrient recycling.

## Pathology

**Lysosomal Storage Diseases.** Genetic deficiencies in specific cathepsins cause devastating lysosomal storage disorders. For example, cathepsin D deficiency leads to [[Ceroid lipofuscinosis|neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis]], a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by accumulation of autofluorescent lipopigment.

**Cancer.** Cathepsins B, L, and S are frequently upregulated in malignant tumors and facilitate [[Cancer|invasion and metastasis]] by degrading components of the [[Extracellular Matrix]] (ECM). They also remodel the tumor microenvironment, promote angiogenesis, and modulate immune surveillance. Their secretion by cancer cells and tumor-associated macrophages creates pericellular proteolytic hotspots that drive tissue invasion.

**Osteoporosis.** Excessive [[Cathepsin K]] activity in osteoclasts contributes to pathological bone loss. This observation has driven therapeutic efforts to inhibit cathepsin K as a strategy for treating [[Osteoporosis|osteoporosis]].

**Inflammation and Cardiovascular Disease.** [[Cathepsin S]] participates in elastin and collagen degradation within arterial walls, promoting plaque instability in [[Atherosclerosis|atherosclerosis]]. Cathepsin S is also implicated in airway remodeling in [[Asthma|asthma]], and more broadly in [[Inflammation|chronic inflammatory]] conditions through its role in [[MHC II]] antigen presentation.

**Lysosomal Membrane Permeabilization.** Release of cathepsins from lysosomes during LMP switches their role from housekeeping proteases to mediators of programmed cell death. This mechanism is engaged by a wide range of cellular stressors, including reactive oxygen species, lipid peroxidation products, and chemotherapeutic agents.

## Therapeutic Targeting

**Cathepsin K Inhibitors.** [[Odanacatib]], a selective cathepsin K inhibitor, was developed for osteoporosis and demonstrated efficacy in increasing bone mineral density and reducing fracture risk in clinical trials. However, its development was discontinued due to an increased risk of cardiovascular events, including stroke and atrial fibrillation, raising questions about the safety of long-term cathepsin K inhibition.

**Cathepsin S Inhibitors.** Selective inhibitors of cathepsin S have been investigated for autoimmune diseases, given the enzyme's role in [[MHC II]]-restricted antigen presentation. Several compounds have reached early clinical trials for conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and multiple sclerosis, though none have yet received regulatory approval.

**Cathepsin B and L Inhibitors.** Preclinical studies have explored cathepsin B and L inhibitors as anti-metastatic agents, but challenges with selectivity, off-target toxicity, and compensatory protease upregulation have limited clinical translation.
