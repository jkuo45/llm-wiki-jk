# Sodium nitrite

Sodium nitrite (NaNO₂) is an inorganic salt of nitrous acid used widely as a meat-curing and food-preservation agent (inhibiting *Clostridium botulinum* and fixing color), as well as in industrial and chemical synthesis. In the body it is the proximal methemoglobin-forming agent among the nitrate/nitrite family: ingested [[Nitric Oxide|nitrate]] is reduced by oral and gut microbiota to nitrite, which is then absorbed.

Its principal toxicity is acquired [[Methemoglobinemia]]. Nitrite oxidizes [[Hemoglobin]]'s ferrous (Fe²⁺) heme iron to ferric (Fe³⁺), yielding [[Methemoglobin]], a species unable to carry oxygen and one that shifts the [[Oxygen-dissociation curve]] leftward, impairing tissue oxygen release. Large acute ingestions — accidental, intentional, or from contaminated food/water — produce cyanosis, hypoxia, and, in severe cases, methemoglobin levels requiring methylene-blue therapy. Infants are especially vulnerable ("blue baby syndrome") because of immature reductase capacity and higher nitrite exposure from water.

Sodium nitrite sits alongside [[Amyl nitrite]] and [[Isobutyl nitrite]] ("poppers"), [[Aniline dyes]], and [[Nitrobenzene]] as a classic methemoglobinemia cause. The overarching redox theme links it to adrenochrome biology: nitrite imposes an oxidative insult on the heme carrier just as [[Oxidative Stress]] drives catecholamines such as [[Epinephrine]] to autoxidize into [[Adrenochrome]] — two faces of the same ferric/oxidized-redox perturbation.

> [!info] Nitrate–nitrite axis
> Dietary nitrate is converted to nitrite by microbiota; nitrite is the direct methemoglobin former.
