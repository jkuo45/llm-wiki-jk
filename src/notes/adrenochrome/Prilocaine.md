# Prilocaine

Prilocaine is an amino-amide local anesthetic structurally related to [[Lidocaine]] and frequently combined with it (e.g., EMLA cream) for topical analgesia. Like other amide anesthetics it blocks voltage-gated sodium channels to arrest nerve impulse propagation. Its distinct clinical safety issue is a well-characterized dose-dependent capacity to induce acquired [[Methemoglobinemia]].

The mechanism is metabolic: prilocaine is hydrolyzed to *o*-toluidine (and related aromatic amines), which oxidize [[Hemoglobin]]'s ferrous (Fe²⁺) heme iron to ferric (Fe³⁺), generating [[Methemoglobin]]. Unlike [[Benzocaine]], which produces a direct oxidant, prilocaine's effect is mediated by this aromatic amine metabolite, so the onset is somewhat delayed and correlates with systemic exposure. Infants and patients with [[G6PD deficiency]] or preexisting methemoglobinemia are at greater risk; the classic sign is cyanosis refractory to oxygen, with chocolate-brown blood on visual inspection.

Prilocaine thus belongs to the same redox-toxicity family as [[Nitrobenzene]] and aniline dyes, where an aromatic amine drives ferrihemoglobin formation. The shared principle with adrenochrome biology is the oxidation of an endogenous redox carrier: just as *o*-toluidine imposes an oxidative burden on hemoglobin, [[Oxidative Stress]] drives catecholamines such as [[Epinephrine]] to autoxidize into [[Adrenochrome]]. Both underscore how exogenous chemicals can tip a tightly balanced redox equilibrium.

> [!warning] Infant sensitivity
> Prilocaine is generally avoided in neonates because of immature methemoglobin-reductase capacity.
