# Arterial Stiffening

**Arterial stiffening** is the age-related loss of compliance in large elastic arteries (aorta, carotids), reflected by rising pulse-wave velocity. It elevates systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure, increases left-ventricular afterload, and promotes small-vessel damage in the brain and kidney — a major contributor to cardiovascular [[Aging]] and cognitive decline.

A central molecular driver is **glycation**: glucose and dicarbonyls form [[Advanced Glycation End Products|AGEs]] that cross-link arterial [[Collagen]] and elastin, replacing compliant elastin with rigid, non-extensible matrix. Accumulation of stable cross-links such as [[Glucosepane]] is essentially irreversible and compounds with oxidation and inflammation. AGEs also engage the receptor [[RAGE]], sustaining vascular [[Inflammation]] and [[Oxidative Stress]].

> [!tip] Reversibility strategies
> Dietary restriction of AGEs, the breaker [[Alagebrium]], enzymatic glucosepane cleavage ([[BluB-like]]), and autophagy-enhancing agents such as spermidine all target the stiffening axis at different levels.

Clinically, arterial stiffness is a stronger predictor of adverse cardiovascular events than chronological age alone, making it a key geroscience biomarker.
