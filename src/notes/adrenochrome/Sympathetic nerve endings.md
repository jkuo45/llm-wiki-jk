# Sympathetic nerve endings

## Overview

Sympathetic nerve endings are the distal terminals of postganglionic sympathetic neurons. Unlike classical synaptic terminals that form discrete one-to-one junctions, most sympathetic nerve endings exhibit a distinctive anatomical feature: **varicosities** — bead-like swellings spaced along the terminal axon. These varicosities release neurotransmitters "en passant" along the length of the axon rather than at a single postsynaptic density, allowing a single sympathetic fiber to influence a broad field of target cells.

The primary neurotransmitter stored and released at these endings is [[Norepinephrine]], though a subset of fibers (particularly those innervating the heart and certain blood vessels) also co-release [[Epinephrine]], [[ATP]], and [[Neuropeptide Y]] ([[NPY]]). The relative contribution of each cotransmitter depends on the firing frequency and the target organ, with NPY and ATP predominating at higher stimulation frequencies to augment and prolong the vasoconstrictor response.

## Innervation

Sympathetic nerve endings provide widespread innervation to nearly every organ system. Major targets include:

- **Most blood vessels:** Sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers innervate resistance arterioles, regulating blood pressure and regional blood flow. Density varies by vascular bed — cutaneous and splanchnic vessels are densely innervated, while cerebral and coronary vessels receive relatively sparse input.
- **Heart:** Dense innervation of the [[SA Node]], [[AV Node]], and ventricular [[Myocardium]]. Sympathetic activation increases heart rate (positive chronotropy), conduction velocity (positive dromotropy), and contractile force (positive inotropy).
- **Lungs:** Sympathetic fibers innervate [[Bronchial smooth muscle]], inducing relaxation (bronchodilation) via [[β₂-adrenergic receptor]] activation.
- **GI tract:** Sympathetic innervation causes [[Sphincter]] contraction and inhibits peristalsis and secretion via presynaptic inhibition of [[Enteric nervous system]] activity.
- **Sweat glands:** Exceptionally, eccrine sweat glands receive [[Cholinergic]] sympathetic innervation — these fibers release [[Acetylcholine]] rather than [[Norepinephrine]].
- **Salivary glands:** Sympathetic stimulation produces thick, mucinous saliva via [[α-adrenergic receptor]] activation.
- **Kidney:** Sympathetic nerve endings innervate the [[Juxtaglomerular apparatus]], where [[Norepinephrine]] acting on [[β₁-adrenergic receptor]] stimulates [[Renin]] release, contributing to [[Renin-Angiotensin System]] activation.
- **Smooth muscle of all organs:** Sympathetic fibers innervate the smooth muscle layers of the reproductive tract, urinary bladder (trigone and internal urethral sphincter), and piloerector muscles of the skin.

## Neurotransmission

Sympathetic neurotransmission proceeds through a stereotyped sequence:

1. An action potential propagates along the postganglionic axon and invades the varicosity.
2. Depolarization opens voltage-gated [[Calcium]] channels (primarily N-type and P/Q-type), triggering Ca²⁺ influx.
3. Elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ drives fusion of small dense-core vesicles with the plasma membrane, releasing [[Norepinephrine]] (and cotransmitters) into the synaptic cleft.
4. Norepinephrine diffuses across the cleft and binds to postsynaptic [[Adrenergic receptor]] (α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂) or presynaptic α₂-autoreceptors (which provide negative feedback by inhibiting further release).
5. The physiologic effect depends on the receptor subtype expressed by the target tissue: α₁ mediates vasoconstriction, β₁ mediates cardiac stimulation, β₂ mediates bronchodilation and vasodilation.

### Termination of Signal

Signal termination is rapid and highly efficient:
- **[[NET]] (Norepinephrine Transporter):** Approximately 80–90% of released norepinephrine is cleared by high-affinity reuptake into the presynaptic varicosity via [[NET]], where it is either repackaged into vesicles or metabolized.
- **Enzymatic Metabolism:** The remainder is metabolized by [[COMT]] ([[COMT|Catechol-O-methyltransferase]]) and [[MAO]] ([[Monoamine oxidase]]). COMT methylates norepinephrine to normetanephrine, while MAO (primarily [[MAO-A]] in sympathetic neurons) oxidatively deaminates it to [[Dihydroxyphenylglycol]] ([[DHPG]]). The combined action produces [[Vanillylmandelic acid]] ([[VMA]]) as the major urinary metabolite.

## Adrenochrome Relevance

Sympathetic nerve endings represent a significant site of potential [[Adrenochrome]] formation. [[Norepinephrine]] and [[Epinephrine]] stored in varicosities are susceptible to [[Autoxidation]] and metal-catalyzed oxidation (particularly by Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺) to their corresponding [[Aminochrome]] derivatives, including Adrenochrome from epinephrine and [[Noradrenochrome]] from norepinephrine. This process is accelerated under conditions of:

- **[[Oxidative Stress]]:** Elevated local [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] (ROS) promote catecholamine oxidation.
- **High Catecholamine Turnover:** States of chronic [[Sympathetic nervous system]] activation (e.g., [[Heart Failure]], [[Pheochromocytoma]], chronic stress) increase the pool of cytosolic catecholamines vulnerable to oxidation.
- **Reduced Antioxidant Capacity:** Depletion of [[Glutathione]] or [[Ascorbic Acid]] in sympathetic terminals lowers the threshold for aminochrome formation.

Once formed, adrenochrome and related aminochromes can exert local cytotoxic effects through [[Redox Cycling]], mitochondrial impairment, and covalent modification of proteins. This mechanism has been implicated in the progressive cardiac damage observed in [[Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy]] and may contribute to [[Sympathetic nerve terminal dysfunction]] in [[Heart Failure]].
