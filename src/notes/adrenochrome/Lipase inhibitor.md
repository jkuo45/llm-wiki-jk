# Lipase inhibitor

A **lipase inhibitor** is a compound that suppresses the activity of pancreatic and gastric lipases — the enzymes that hydrolyze dietary triglycerides into absorbable monoacylglycerols and free fatty acids. By blocking fat digestion, lipase inhibitors reduce caloric uptake and lower postprandial lipemia.

The best-known agent is [[Orlistat]], a irreversible inhibitor of gastric and pancreatic lipase derived from *Streptomyces toxytricini*. Beyond weight management, orlistat has an intriguing secondary effect relevant to glycation: it reduces the intestinal absorption of dietary [[Advanced Glycation End Products|AGEs]] (and their precursors) by limiting the micellar solubilization and uptake of lipophilic, AGE-rich food fractions. This lowers systemic AGE burden — a mechanistic link between fat digestion and the glycation axis central to vascular and metabolic [[Aging]].

> [!info] Redox/glycation interface
> Because lipophilic AGEs co-travel with dietary fat, lipase inhibition represents a plausible, low-cost lever to curb AGE load alongside dietary restriction of fried/processed foods.

Other lipase inhibitors (e.g., cetilistat) follow the same principle. Chronic use requires fat-soluble vitamin monitoring due to malabsorption.
