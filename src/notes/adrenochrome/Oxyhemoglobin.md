# Oxyhemoglobin

Oxyhemoglobin (HbO₂) is the oxygen-loaded ferrous (Fe²⁺) state of [[Hemoglobin]], in which O₂ is reversibly bound at each heme. It is the principal oxygen-carrying species in erythrocytes and the source of arterial red coloration. Oxyhemoglobin is in constant redox equilibrium with [[Methemoglobin]] through spontaneous *autoxidation*: the bound O₂ abstracts an electron from Fe²⁺, yielding ferric (Fe³⁺) heme and releasing [[Superoxide anion]] (O₂•⁻). Normal autoxidation is slow (~0.5–3% of Hb per day), with methemoglobin kept low by cytochrome b5 reductase and [[Diaphorase|NADPH diaphorase]] systems.

This heme-centered redox process interfaces directly with catecholamine chemistry. The superoxide and downstream H₂O₂ produced from oxyhemoglobin autoxidation can oxidize [[Epinephrine]] and other catecholamines to their quinone/semiquinone intermediates and ultimately to [[Adrenochrome]] and related aminochromes; conversely, reducing species such as [[Leuco-adrenochrome]] can reduce methemoglobin back, forming a coupled redox cycle. Thus erythrocyte heme redox and plasma catecholamine redox are linked through shared radical intermediates.

The adrenochrome relevance is concrete: factors that accelerate oxyhemoglobin autoxidation — oxidative stress, nitrite, certain drugs — raise both methemoglobin and the oxidant burden that drives aminochrome formation. Conversely, preserving the reduced Fe²⁺/catechol state limits [[Adrenochrome]] accumulation, tying hemoglobin redox to the vault's central aminochrome theme.

> [!info] Coupled redox
> Oxyhemoglobin autoxidation and catecholamine oxidation share superoxide as a common intermediate.
