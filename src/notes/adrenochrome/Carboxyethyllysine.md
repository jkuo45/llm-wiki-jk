# Carboxyethyllysine

**N-ε-carboxyethyllysine** (CEL) is a well-characterized, methylglyoxal-derived member of the [[Advanced Glycation End Products]] (AGEs), structurally the two-carbon homologue of [[N-(carboxymethyl)-lysine]].

## Formation

CEL arises from the reaction of the ε-amino group of [[Lysine]] with **methylglyoxal**, the major endogenous dicarbonyl and a toxic intermediate of glycolysis and lipid peroxidation. Methylglyoxal is detoxified by the glyoxalase system; when that capacity is exceeded, it reacts with lysine residues to accumulate as CEL.

The Maillard cascade supplies the same chemistry. Reducing sugars first react with lysine to give Amadori products, which degrade to reactive 1,2-dicarbonyls; glyoxal then gives carboxymethyllysine while methylglyoxal gives CEL. Because the reaction is at lysine side chains, both compounds mark proteins broadly and irreversibly.

> [!info] CEL as a marker of dicarbonyl stress
> CEL is one of the better-established AGE biomarkers in human biofluid. It is measured both free and protein-bound, the free pool reflecting small-molecule generation and the protein-bound pool reflecting cumulative tissue exposure. It accumulates in long-lived proteins — lens crystallins, collagen, and neuronal proteins — which is why it rises with age.

## Clinical Relevance

**Inflammation and endothelial dysfunction in kidney disease.** In individuals with stage 5 chronic kidney disease not on dialysis, higher serum free and protein-bound AGE and dicarbonyl levels were positively correlated with biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction and low-grade inflammation — free CEL with soluble VCAM-1 and soluble thrombomodulin and with hs-CRP, SAA, and IL-6. Adjustment for eGFR attenuated these partial correlations, indicating that kidney function confounds and/or mediates the relationship (Tang et al., *PLoS ONE* 2019; PMID 31408493). This fits the general pattern: the kidney is the principal clearance route for circulating dicarbonyls and AGEs, so renal impairment raises the whole burden.

**Vascular stiffness and atherosclerosis.** Like other AGEs, CEL correlates with arterial stiffness in the elderly and with markers of vascular injury, plausibly through [[RAGE]]-mediated activation of endothelial and inflammatory signaling.

**Dietary exposure.** CEL occurs in cooked food, generated by methylglyoxal in the Maillard reaction, with an estimated dietary intake of roughly 0.02–0.04 mg/kg body weight per day. It is among the most abundant dietary glycation compounds.

> [!warning] The dietary-AGE evidence base is weak
> The German Research Foundation's Senate Commission on Food Safety systematically assessed the literature on dietary glycation compounds and concluded that there is at present **no convincing evidence for a causal association** between dietary intake of glycation compounds and adverse health effects. Human studies were limited by small cohorts, short duration, and confounding; animal evidence for dicarbonyl exposure supporting impaired glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, and renal injury used doses far exceeding estimated human exposure (Hellwig et al., *Crit Rev Toxicol* 2024; PMID 39150724). Endogenous formation means serum biomarkers reflect total exposure, so the dietary share cannot be cleanly separated.
