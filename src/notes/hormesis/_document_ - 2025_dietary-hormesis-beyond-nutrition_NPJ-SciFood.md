# Dietary hormesis: beyond nutrition and energy supply

_Source: 2025_dietary-hormesis-beyond-nutrition_NPJ-SciFood.nxml (JATS full text via EuropePMC, converted auto)_

## Abstract

**Subject terms:** Nutrition, Quality of life

## Main

The primary focus of food science research has traditionally been on understanding the nutritional composition and energy-yielding properties of food, emphasizing energy provision, macronutrient balance and micronutrient adequacy. While these aspects are undeniably crucial, the broader potential of food to influence human health through non-nutritional mechanisms has often been overlooked. One such mechanism is hormesis, a biological phenomenon characterized by beneficial adaptive responses to low doses of stressors.

Hormesis is a biphasic dose-response characterized by low-dose stimulation and high-dose inhibition, resulting in an adaptive response to stressors that enhances an organism’s resilience and overall health<sup>1</sup>. Initially observed in toxicology, where sub-toxic doses of certain chemicals paradoxically improved organismal function, the term “hormesis” was later coined to describe this broadly applicable phenomenon, encompassing cellular, molecular, and organismal levels.

Among all hormetic contexts, dietary hormesis stands out as the safest and most convenient form of hormesis that can be seamlessly integrated into daily life. Unlike other hormetic stressors, such as radiation or chemical exposure, which may carry inherent risks and require careful management, dietary hormesis involves the consumption of natural foods rich in bioactive compounds, such as fruits, vegetables, and whole grains<sup>2–4</sup>. These foods not only provide essential nutrients and energy but also promote beneficial adaptive responses in the body when consumed in moderate amounts. By incorporating a diverse array of hormetic foods into our diets, individuals can harness the health benefits of dietary hormesis without the need for specialized interventions or exposure to potentially harmful substances. This accessibility makes dietary hormesis an appealing strategy for enhancing resilience, preventing chronic diseases, and promoting overall well-being in everyday life.

Within the study of hormesis in nutrition, it is important to make a distinction between the effects of individual food components and the broader phenomenon of dietary hormesis. The former generally refers to the biphasic dose-response effects of single, often isolated, bioactive compounds (hormetins), such as resveratrol or curcumin. These effects are frequently characterized in controlled laboratory settings. In contrast, dietary hormesis represents a more holistic concept. It encompasses the cumulative, and potentially synergistic, adaptive responses to a complex array of hormetins as they are consumed within the matrix of whole foods. The food matrix itself—the intricate assembly of nutrients, fiber, and other constituents—can significantly modulate the bioavailability, metabolism, and ultimate bioactivity of these compounds. Therefore, the health effects of a diet rich in hormetic foods may be greater than, and distinct from, the sum of the effects of its individual chemical parts.

This perspective elucidates the concept of hormesis, its manifestation in food, and its implications for human health. The aim is to initiate a broader discussion within the food science community about the necessity of incorporating dietary hormesis into the functional food research agenda.

### Conceptual Origin: From Empirical Observation to Theoretical Framework

In the 16th century, the Swiss physician Paracelsus established the philosophical foundation for dose-response relationships with his statement, “The dose makes the poison” (*Sola dosis facit venenum*). This was more than a simple statement on toxicity; it was a philosophical foundation suggesting that no substance is inherently a poison or a remedy. Rather, its effect is dictated by its quantity. This insight, which implied that a substance could be harmful at a high dose but beneficial or benign at a low one, directly prefigured the biphasic dose-responses central to hormesis and laid the groundwork for modern toxicology. The first direct empirical evidence for this principle emerged nearly three centuries later from the work of German pharmacologist Hugo Schulz. In the late 19th century, Hugo Schulz observed that low concentrations of disinfectants could stimulate yeast metabolism, a finding initially dismissed as experimental error due to the prevailing scientific paradigm<sup>5</sup>. Consequently, Schulz’s groundbreaking observations of a biphasic, stimulatory-inhibitory response were largely dismissed by his contemporaries as experimental anomalies, delaying the formal recognition of hormesis for decades.

A landmark study in 1943 by Southam et al., published in *Phytopathology*, provided the first robust scientific validation of hormesis<sup>6</sup>. This biphasic effect was subsequently replicated with penicillin and *Staphylococcus*, prompting the scientific community to re-evaluate the fundamental characteristics of biological stress responses. In the 1980s, Luckey expanded the scope of hormesis research from chemical toxins to physical factors by proposing the concept of “radiation hormesis”<sup>5</sup>.

Calabrese and his colleagues have also made significant efforts to refine the definition and classification of hormesis: an adaptive response of biological systems to low-intensity stressors, characterized by a biphasic dose-response curve with a stimulatory zone (typically 0.1-10 times the No-Observed-Adverse-Effect Level, NOAEL) and an inhibitory zone<sup>7</sup>. This established the modern theoretical framework of hormesis. Large-scale data analyses indicate that approximately 40% of chemical substances exhibit hormetic characteristics, with evolutionary conservation across species, from bacteria to mammals<sup>8</sup>.

### Biological Mechanisms: Molecular Pathways of Stress Adaptation

The biological mechanisms underlying hormesis involve a hierarchical activation of cellular stress response pathways. A primary pathway is the Nrf2/ARE antioxidant defense system. Low-dose oxidative stress triggers a conformational change in the Keap1 protein, releasing the transcription factor Nrf2. This process is a key target for dietary hormetins. For instance, electrophilic compounds such as sulforaphane from cruciferous vegetables or the flavonoid quercetin from apples can directly react with critical cysteine residues on the Keap1 protein. This covalent modification disrupts the Keap1-Nrf2 complex, liberating Nrf2 to translocate to the nucleus. Once in the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) and induces the expression of over 500 cytoprotective genes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)<sup>9–11</sup>, thereby enhancing the cell’s resilience against more significant stressors.

This pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining redox homeostasis and regulating antioxidant responses. Dietary compounds such as flavonoids and isothiocyanates can stimulate the Nrf2 pathway, leading to the expression of various antioxidant enzymes and detoxifying proteins<sup>12–14</sup>. This activation not only helps in neutralizing oxidative stress but also promotes cellular resilience against more significant stressors, thereby contributing to cancer chemoprevention and overall health maintenance.

Mitohormesis, or mitochondrial adaptive response, also plays a crucial role. This process is readily triggered by specific dietary compounds. Plant polyphenols, such as resveratrol found in grapes and red wine, can activate key regulatory pathways involving sirtuins and the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), which promote the expression of genes involved in mitochondrial function<sup>15–17</sup>. This activation cascade promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and enhances the efficiency of cellular energy production, contributing to improved metabolic health and resilience.

Finally, epigenetic reprogramming contributes to long-lasting hormetic responses. Specific food components act as potent epigenetic modulators that can dynamically regulate gene expression. For example, sulforaphane from broccoli is a well-characterized inhibitor of histone deacetylase (HDAC) enzymes, while the catechin epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) abundant in green tea is known to inhibit DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs)<sup>18,19</sup>. Besides, mild caloric restriction or the consumption of other phytochemicals can lead to changes in gene expression patterns through epigenetic mechanisms<sup>20–23</sup>. By altering histone acetylation and DNA methylation patterns, these dietary compounds can modify chromatin structure and influence the expression of genes involved in stress resistance, inflammation, and longevity, thereby conferring a durable adaptive advantage.

The biological mechanisms previously described constitute the fundamental framework of the cellular adaptive stress-response. A critical extension of this mechanistic understanding involves identifying the specific dietary agents capable of activating these pathways. Accordingly, the subsequent section transitions from this theoretical basis to a practical examination of foods rich in hormetic compounds. The focus will shift to how particular plant metabolites, fermentation byproducts, and cooking-generated compounds serve as exogenous triggers for these endogenous defense systems, thus elucidating the link between dietary intake and tangible physiological benefits.

### Hormetic Effects in Food

Food represents a rich and diverse source of hormetic compounds, or “hormetins,” capable of inducing beneficial adaptive responses in the body. These compounds, often present in low concentrations in commonly consumed foods like fruits, vegetables, spices, and fermented products<sup>2–4,24</sup>, extend the concept of hormesis beyond synthetic chemicals.

Plant polyphenols, for example, are well-established hormetins<sup>25,26</sup>. Resveratrol in red wine, quercetin in apples, and catechins in green tea activate cellular stress response pathways, notably the Nrf2/ARE pathway, leading to increased production of endogenous antioxidants and detoxification enzymes. This activation contributes to enhanced resistance to oxidative stress, reduced inflammation, and a potentially decreased risk of chronic diseases<sup>27,28</sup>. Sulforaphane, a bioactive isothiocyanate generated from its precursor, glucoraphanin, in broccoli, brussels sprouts, and other cruciferous vegetables, is another well-characterized hormetin. It activates the Nrf2 pathway and upregulates detoxification enzymes, such as glutathione S-transferase. These effects contribute to enhanced detoxification capacity, reduced oxidative damage, and increased cellular protection against environmental toxins<sup>29,30</sup>.

Other plant compounds also play a role in hormesis. Capsaicin, the compound responsible for the spicy flavor in chilli peppers, induces a stimulatory response by activating transient receptor potential (TRP) channels. This activation leads to increased thermogenesis, improved mitochondrial function, and enhanced insulin sensitivity. Regular capsaicin consumption has been associated with a reduced risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease<sup>31</sup>.

Fermented foods, such as kimchi, sauerkraut, and yogurt, provide a source of hormetins through the presence of probiotics and bioactive peptides, but also through a complex array of other microbial metabolites that can act as signaling molecules to modulate host physiology<sup>32</sup>. These compounds can modulate immune function, improve gut health, and enhance systemic antioxidant defenses. Nattokinase in natto, for example exhibits a dose-dependent effect on blood clotting. This dose-dependent effect is related to the concentration gradient of fibrinogen/fibrin<sup>33</sup>. Similarly, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, which are produced by the gut microbial fermentation of dietary fibers (prebiotics), are crucial for health. Butyrate serves as a primary energy source for colonocytes and enhances intestinal barrier integrity by increasing the expression of tight junction proteins like occludin, which is vital for preventing systemic inflammation<sup>34</sup>.

The processing and cooking of food can generate a variety of byproducts, some of which exhibit hormetic properties. The Maillard reaction, a non-enzymatic browning process between amino acids (e.g., lysine) and reducing sugars (e.g., glucose), is ubiquitous in the cooking of many foods—responsible for the characteristic flavors and colors of bread crusts, seared meats, and roasted coffee—and presents a complex interplay of benefits and risks<sup>35</sup>. While high levels of Maillard reaction products can be detrimental, emerging evidence suggests potential hormetic effects of Maillard reaction products (MRPs) at lower doses, primarily stemming from their antioxidant, antimicrobial, and prebiotic properties. Certain MRPs have shown promise in contributing to the prevention of cancer and may positively influence glucose metabolism and cardiovascular health by potentially reducing cholesterol and triglyceride levels, inhibiting ACE activity, and exhibiting antithrombotic effects<sup>36,37</sup>. Furthermore, the strategic use of the Maillard reaction, such as in the formation of protein-polysaccharide conjugates, can enhance the functional properties of food components, including their solubility, emulsifying capacity, and antioxidant activity<sup>38,39</sup>. Beyond this, other cooking processes warrant consideration. Caramelization, the browning of sugar by heat, produces a mixture of compounds like caramel, which at low concentrations have been shown to possess antioxidant capabilities, fitting a hormetic model<sup>40</sup>.

These examples collectively highlight the pervasive, yet often overlooked, presence of hormetic effects within the food we consume, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of dose-response relationships in the context of food and health.

### Life processes and health benefits influenced by dietary hormesis

Dietary hormesis operates through multiple interconnected pathways, impacting metabolism, immune function, and the gut microbiota.

A primary mechanism by which dietary hormesis promotes health is through the enhancement of antioxidant defense systems. Hormetins activate the Nrf2 pathway, upregulating the expression of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes, such as glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase. This enhanced antioxidant capacity helps neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduce oxidative damage. Chronic inflammation is a key driver of many chronic diseases. Hormetins can modulate inflammatory pathways by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and activating anti-inflammatory signaling. For example, the dietary compound curcumin inhibits the NF-κB pathway; however, due to its low bioavailability and concentration in turmeric (approx. 3% by weight), its in vivo anti-inflammatory efficacy often relies on high doses or formulation strategies, such as co-administration with piperine from black pepper<sup>41,42</sup>.

Dietary hormesis also involves metabolic reprogramming, where low doses of certain dietary compounds can shift metabolic pathways to favor health-promoting processes. For instance, isothiocyanates not only modulate the tumor microenvironment but also rearrange energy metabolism pathways, enhancing cellular energy production and utilization<sup>14,43</sup>. This reprogramming can lead to improved metabolic health, reduced inflammation, and enhanced resistance to diseases such as cancer<sup>12</sup>. The ability of these compounds to influence metabolic pathways underscores the importance of dosage and timing in achieving beneficial effects while minimizing potential toxicity.

Finally, dietary hormesis significantly influences microbiota-host co-evolution. For example, seaweed-derived components can modulate gut microbiota by altering the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria like *Bacteroides* and *Lactobacillus*<sup>44,45</sup>. Flavonoids are metabolized by gut bacteria into phenolic acids, which can then exert protective effects on the host<sup>46–48</sup>. Besides, the fermentation of dietary fibers by gut microbiota produces SCFAs, which have been shown to exert hormetic effects by influencing glucose and lipid metabolism and reducing oxidative stress<sup>49,50</sup>. These examples illustrate the intricate and dose-sensitive ways in which dietary components can modulate the gut microbiome and, consequently, host physiology.

Crucially, the principle of hormesis dictates that the benefits of hormetins are confined to a low-dose window; at higher concentrations, these same compounds exhibit inhibitory or overtly toxic effects. This biphasic nature is fundamental to the concept. For many phytochemicals, the shift from benefit to harm occurs when their role changes from a mild stressor that triggers adaptive signaling to a direct participant in cellular disruption. For example, while low-dose polyphenols stimulate endogenous antioxidant defenses, high concentrations, such as those found in concentrated supplements, can act as potent pro-oxidants, leading to increased oxidative stress and cellular damage<sup>51</sup>. Furthermore, excessive intake can lead to specific toxicities, such as the potential for liver damage from high-dose green tea catechins or gastrointestinal distress from concentrated capsaicin<sup>52,53</sup>. High doses of certain hormetins can also interfere with the absorption of essential nutrients or interact dangerously with pharmaceuticals. This underscores the importance of obtaining hormetins from whole foods—where they exist in naturally moderate concentrations—rather than from high-dose supplements, and it highlights why a deep understanding of the full dose-response curve is essential for both food safety and functional food design.

### Dietary Hormesis Beyond Nutrition and Energy: A Paradigm Shift in Food Science Research

The traditional paradigm in food science, centered on nutrient provision and the avoidance of toxicity, falls short in explaining the health benefits conferred by certain dietary components that act through the induction of mild biological stress responses. While accumulating evidence supports the significant health advantages of dietary hormesis, several critical research gaps remain.

Firstly, a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying hormetic responses, especially within the complex context of whole foods and food matrices, is lacking. Secondly, the identification of hormetic substances in food is currently limited, often focusing on a few well-characterized compounds, leading to a likely underestimation of the full spectrum of beneficial food-derived hormetins. Thirdly, optimal doses and frequencies of consumption for maximizing the health benefits of hormetic substances remain largely undefined.

To address these limitations, this paper proposes several concrete research frameworks. Firstly, we advocate for a novel, multi-layered classification system for “dietary bio-stressors”. This system would categorize compounds by their primary mechanism of action (a classification often established through in vitro research). Critically, to bridge the gap to real-world effects, the system would also incorporate a dynamic tier for in vivo evidence, distinguishing between effects validated in animal models versus human trials and noting known bioavailability limitations. Such a framework would create a clear roadmap for research by differentiating established hormetins from those requiring further translational study. Secondly, we propose a three-dimensional ‘dose-time-individual’ evaluation model to guide future research and personalized nutrition. This framework would be implemented by considering three key components. The dose component would move beyond simple quantity (mg) to focus on bioavailability from the food matrix and defining the precise biphasic dose-response curve to identify the optimal hormetic zone. The time element would investigate the impact of exposure frequency (daily vs. intermittent), duration, and the timing of consumption relative to other factors like meals or exercise. Crucially, the individual component would incorporate key personal variables such as genetic polymorphisms (e.g., in Nrf2 or metabolic enzyme genes), gut microbiome composition (which metabolizes many hormetins), age, and baseline health status.

Finally, we call for the development of quantitative prediction algorithms, leveraging machine learning to model how food processing and cooking parameters affect the generation of these hormetins. Together, these frameworks would provide the structured approach needed to translate the concept of dietary hormesis into practical health strategies.

Mechanism research is crucial, involving the investigation of the molecular and cellular mechanisms of hormesis within the context of whole foods and food matrices. This necessitates studying the intricate interactions between hormetins and other food components, as well as their bioavailability and bioactivity within the body.

Discovery of novel hormetins is a necessary step, requiring an expansion of the search for hormetic substances beyond those already well-understood. To achieve this, advanced analytical techniques such as metabolomics and bioinformatics can be effectively employed to identify and thoroughly characterize previously unknown hormetins present in various foods.

Dose-response studies are essential for gaining a comprehensive understanding of hormetic effects, as precise control over dosage is paramount. These studies are needed to accurately determine the optimal intake levels of hormetic compounds for promoting health, including careful consideration of the effects of low-dose exposure over extended periods and the identification of relevant biomarkers that can indicate hormetic responses.

Translational research is critically needed to effectively bridge the existing gap between laboratory findings and practical real-world applications of hormesis. This involves the development of targeted food-based interventions that contain specific hormetic agents, followed by rigorous testing of their efficacy and safety in human populations through well-designed clinical trials. Furthermore, such studies should carefully consider factors like age, sex, genetic background, and pre-existing health conditions, as these variables can significantly influence individual responses to hormetins.

Thorough exploration of the public health implications is critical for translating dietary hormesis into actionable recommendations and dietary guidelines. This includes the crucial task of identifying specific populations that may derive the greatest benefit from hormetic interventions, both for disease prevention in at-risk individuals (such as those with metabolic syndrome) and as an adjunct therapeutic strategy for managing established chronic diseases, and subsequently developing effective strategies to promote the consumption of foods naturally rich in hormetic compounds. Besides, this also involves moving beyond broad advice to develop concrete, hormesis-informed strategies. For instance, food-based dietary guidelines could be updated to include specific, mechanism-aware advice, such as recommending “one serving of cruciferous vegetables three to four times per week” to leverage Nrf2 activation, or promoting the inclusion of fermented foods like yogurt or kimchi to support gut health via microbial metabolites. Public health campaigns could use simple, memorable heuristics like “aim for a colorful plate,” which naturally encourages the consumption of a wide variety of phytochemicals that act as hormetins. Educational materials could also be developed to reframe consumer perceptions, teaching people to appreciate bitter or pungent foods (e.g., bitter greens, turmeric, chili peppers) not as unpleasant, but as sources of beneficial, low-dose biological stressors. While identifying and targeting populations at high risk of chronic disease is important, the overarching message should remain focused on variety and moderation. This approach avoids the “superfood” trap and empowers consumers to build resilient dietary patterns based on sound hormetic principles. Future work should also explore the significant practical applications and the broader socio-economic and policy implications of fully adopting a hormesis-based framework in the food sector.

## Conclusion

Food science research has traditionally focused on the nutritional and energy-providing properties of food, often overlooking the significant health benefits conferred by hormesis. By incorporating the concept of dietary hormesis into the research agenda, the food science community can achieve a more complete understanding of how food influences human health, extending its role as merely a source of calories and essential nutrients. This shift in perspective has the potential to revolutionize our approach to food and nutrition, paving the way for innovative strategies to promote health, prevent disease, and potentially enhance longevity.

The molecular mechanisms of dietary hormesis are complex and multifaceted, involving a network of cellular pathways, including oxidative stress response, autophagy, DNA repair, and inflammation modulation. These pathways are often interconnected, and their coordinated activation enhances cellular resilience, reduces oxidative damage, and promotes overall health. Identifying and characterizing the diverse array of hormetic components in food, along with a deeper understanding of their mechanisms of action and long-term health benefits, is crucial for fully realizing the potential of dietary hormesis.

As research in this field progresses, it is essential to adopt a multidisciplinary approach, integrating insights from nutrition, toxicology, molecular biology, epidemiology, agricultural science and public health. This collaborative effort will unlock new opportunities to improve human health and well-being through the strategic and informed consumption of food. By embracing a more holistic view of food’s role in health, we can move beyond the traditional, somewhat limited, focus on nutrition and energy supply and embrace a new paradigm that recognizes food as a potent source of adaptive, hormetic responses—a key to promoting resilience and optimal health throughout the lifespan (Fig. 1). This paper serves as a call to action for the food science community to embrace this paradigm shift and dedicate resources to further explore the vast, and largely untapped, potential of dietary hormesis.
