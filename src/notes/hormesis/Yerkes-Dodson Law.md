# Yerkes-Dodson Law

The **Yerkes-Dodson law** states that performance on a task improves with
arousal up to an optimum and then deteriorates as arousal rises further — an
inverted-U, or bell-shaped, relationship. It was proposed in 1908 by Robert M.
Yerkes and John Dillingham Dodson from experiments on mice and rats
discriminating visual cues under electric shock, at intensities spanning a wide
range.

> [!info] The original result was task-difficulty dependent from the start
> Dodson and Yerkes reported that the optimal arousal level was lower for
> difficult discriminations than for easy ones — the second, less famous half of
> the finding. The law therefore always had two claims: an inverted-U, and a
> leftward (lower) shift of the peak with task difficulty. Quoting only the
> inverted-U misrepresents the original work.

## Form of the Relationship

Three arousal zones are conventionally described:

- **Under-arousal** — insufficient drive to focus attention; performance
  impaired.
- **Optimal arousal** — peak performance, with the location of the peak set by
  task difficulty and by the organism's baseline state.
- **Over-arousal** — attentional narrowing, working-memory failure, and
  performance impairment that is typically *worse* than the under-aroused
  state.

Two methodological criticisms are important and often omitted:

1. **Ceiling effects in the mouse data.** The original dose-response analyses
   were retrospective fits to grouped data, and the apparent asymmetry of the
   curve has been argued to be partly an artifact of how the low-intensity
   conditions were constructed.
2. **The inverted-U is not universal.** Performance on some tasks, and some
   individuals' data, show monotonic or J-shaped relationships with arousal.
   Modern models treat arousal as a multidimensional construct (arousal,
   valence, activation, effort) rather than one axis, which is why the
   single-curve version has largely been abandoned in the cognitive literature.

## Relation to Hormesis

The Yerkes-Dodson law is the psychological and historical relative of the
[[Hormesis]] dose-response: both describe a response variable that turns over,
being beneficial at low exposure and harmful at high exposure, with a peak
inside a tolerated range.

> [!important] Structural similarity, not the same phenomenon
> The two curves have the same *shape* for different reasons, and treating them
> as one has caused repeated confusion. The Yerkes-Dodson peak reflects
> allocation of attention under sympathetic drive; a hormetic peak reflects
> adaptive repair capacity being overwhelmed past a dose threshold. The dose
> axis is also different in kind: arousal intensity in a task is not a chemical
> concentration.

Where they genuinely converge is in the **eustress / distress distinction**: the
[[Eustress]] zone is the left limb of the inverted-U, and distress is the right
limb. That framing is why psychosocial stress research imports the term —
most prominently in work tracing stress biology and hormesis back to the
Yerkes-Dodson foundations.

The same turnover also appears in the [[Hormetic Window]] concept used for
interventions such as [[Exercise]], [[Caloric Restriction]], and heat or cold
exposure, where the useful dose is bounded on both sides.

## Modern Status

The law retains real utility as a teaching device and as a hypothesis about the
shape of psychomotor performance under acute stress, and the 2008-ish revival
of arousal research explicitly frames its questions in these terms. It is
reproduced qualitatively in applied human-factors settings (aircraft handling
under increasing workload, marksmanship under increasing threat) and in sport,
where the practical form of the law is a coaching heuristic.

It should not be cited as an established general law of performance. The
reproducibility of the inverted-U itself is limited, individual differences in
peak arousal are large and partly trait-like, and there is no single accepted
mechanism. The defensible statement is narrower: moderate sympathetic arousal
tends to improve performance on simple or well-practised tasks and to impair it
on complex or novel ones.

## Connections
- [[Hormesis]] — the biological statement of the same biphasic shape: beneficial at low dose, harmful at high dose, with the troughs determined by the same adaptive capacity logic.
- [[Eustress]] — the positive limb of the curve, and the term the stress literature imported from arousal research; distress is its right-limb counterpart.
- [[Hormetic Window]] — the bounded dose range in which an intervention produces benefit, which is the practical translation of the inverted-U into dosing language.
- [[Exercise]] — the canonical hormetic stressor whose dose-response is biphasic in exactly this sense: too little under-adapts, too much causes injury and net harm.
- [[Caloric Restriction]] — a second biphasic intervention where the benefit is bounded above by malnutrition and below by no signal at all.
- [[Cortisol]] — the stress hormone that provides the biological substrate for the arousal axis and is itself released in a biphasic, stress-dose-dependent pattern.
- [[Sympathetic Nervous System]] — the physiological system whose intensity is the arousal variable in the law, and which hormesis theory treats as the trigger channel for many hormetins.
- [[Mitohormesis]] — the mitochondrial-specific form of the same hormetic logic, frequently used as the mechanistic justification for inverted-U thinking about ROS signalling.
- [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] — the signal whose dose-response is genuinely biphasic (hormetic) in cells, which is the closest biological analogue to the inverted-U and the most cited mechanistic bridge.
- [[Homeostasis]] — the set-point concept that gives a reason for a turnover point: perturbation from baseline is tolerated in both directions before function fails.
- [[Mitochondria]] — the organelle whose ROS output, and whose damage threshold, make the biphasic relationship concrete at the biochemical level.
- [[Adrenochrome]] — a case where the same inverted-U logic is used rhetorically to rationalise a stress-adaptation story; worth linking precisely so the analogy's limits stay visible.
