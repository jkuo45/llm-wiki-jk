## Overview

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are monoclonal antibodies that block inhibitory receptors and their ligands — most notably PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 — thereby releasing brakes on anti-tumor T-cell responses. They have transformed the treatment of many solid and hematologic malignancies.

## Mechanism of Action

Immune checkpoints are physiological brakes on lymphocyte responses that cancers hijack to avoid immune destruction. Checkpoint inhibitors are antibodies that release the brakes on [[T Cell|T cells]] by blocking PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4 interactions, restoring effector function and enabling immune-mediated tumor rejection. The directionality runs from the inhibitor toward the [[Immune Checkpoint]], making the inhibitor the active agent and the checkpoint the recipient of the effect.

> [!info] Direction of action
> Clinically, immune checkpoint inhibitors position themselves as a therapeutic modality against the checkpoint; efficacy is shaped by biomarker selection and the eventual emergence of resistance.

## Clinical Applications & Biomarkers

PD-1/PD-L1 blockade (e.g., [[Pembrolizumab]], nivolumab) and CTLA-4 blockade (ipilimumab) are approved across melanoma, lung, renal, bladder, and other cancers. Responses are enriched in tumors with high PD-L1 expression, high tumor mutational burden, and deficient mismatch repair, but primary and acquired resistance remain common.

## Challenges & Resistance

Resistance mechanisms include loss of antigen presentation, upregulation of alternative checkpoints, and immunosuppressive infiltration by cells such as [[Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells]]. Combination strategies with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted agents aim to convert immunologically cold tumors into responsive ones.
