# Vinca Alkaloids

**Vinca alkaloids** are a class of plant-derived antimitotic agents isolated from the Madagascar periwinkle (*Catharanthus roseus*, also *Vinca rosea*). [[Vincristine]] and [[Vinblastine]] are the two agents in clinical use; the semisynthetic analogs vinorelbine and vinorelbine-era compounds are less widely used. They were among the first chemotherapies to demonstrate that a plant secondary metabolite could be converted into a selective anticancer agent, and they remain standard therapy for lymphomas, leukemias, and several solid tumors roughly six decades after their introduction.

> [!info] Why a plant alkaloid became chemotherapy
> The Madagascar periwinkle entered pharmacological use through folk medicine rather than screening. Its antineoplastic activity was identified clinically in the 1950s and the active alkaloids were later isolated, refined, and formulated. Vinblastine (Velban) and vincristine were the first plant-derived agents to enter routine cancer chemotherapy, a distinction usually attributed to vinblastine.

## Mechanism of Action

Vinca alkaloids bind the **"vinca site"** on β-tubulin — a hydrophobic pocket on the microtubule lattice distinct from the colchicine site (on tubulin, not microtubules) and the taxane site (the lumen-facing surface). The functional consequences are:

- They **stabilize the microtubule polymer**, preventing the depolymerization that is normally required to shorten a mitotic spindle.
- The resulting microtubules remain **dynamically dead**: they persist but cannot dynamically reconfigure.
- Spindle microtubule dynamics are required for kinetochore attachment, congression, and the spindle assembly checkpoint response. Because dynamics fail, cells arrest in **metaphase**, unable to satisfy the checkpoint even though the chromosomes appear attached.
- Prolonged arrest triggers the intrinsic apoptotic program via the [[MOMP]] pathway — cytochrome c release, [[Apoptosome]] assembly, and caspase activation. In some settings mitotic slippage and eventual exit occur instead, producing aneuploid cells.

> [!important] Antimitotic versus mitotic arrest are different
> Vinca alkaloids are described interchangeably as "mitotic inhibitors," "antimitotics," and "mitotic arrest agents," but the distinction matters. Vinca alkaloids inhibit **microtubule dynamics**, which produces the arrest; they do not directly inhibit a mitotic kinase. Colchicine, by contrast, prevents polymerization outright. This difference underlies their distinct toxicity spectra and explains why vinca-site agents remain active against tumor cells that have become resistant to taxanes.

## Pharmacodynamics & Resistance

- Vinblastine binds more potently than vincristine but causes less neurotoxicity; vincristine is more neurotoxic and is dosed accordingly.
- Vinca alkaloids are large, lipophilic, and strongly protein-bound, so they have large volumes of distribution and long tissue half-lives.
- Principal resistance mechanism is upregulation of class III β-tubulin isotypes (βIII and βV), which reduce vinca-site binding; P-glycoprotein efflux (see [[P-glycoprotein]]) is the other major mechanism and accounts for much of the multidrug-resistance phenotype of cancer cells.
- Epigenetic silencing of β-tubulin genes and tubulin post-translational modifications have also been implicated, and tubulin mutations are rare contributors.

## Clinical Uses

- **[[Vincristine]]** — ALL and lymphoma regimens, including vincristine–doxorubicin–cyclophosphamide-type combinations; also used in regimens for childhood and adult CNS tumors and neuroblastoma.
- **[[Vinblastine]]** — Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, testicular cancer, gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, and bladder cancer.
- Vinorelbine — non-small-cell lung cancer and breast cancer.
- Given by intermittent bolus rather than infusion; continuous infusion is used in some regimens to exploit the greater potency of prolonged exposure.

## Adverse Effects

- **Peripheral/autonomic neuropathy.** Dose-limiting and cumulative for vincristine; the reason vincristine is dosed on a weekly-to-monthly cycle rather than continuously. Causes numbness, paresthesias, areflexia, and **autonomic** bowel dysfunction (constipation or ileus). Recovery is slow and can be incomplete; concurrent vincristine with other neurotoxins is avoided where possible.
- **Myelosuppression.** Vinblastine is more myelosuppressive than vincristine.
- **Alopecia** and mucositis.
- **Extravasation injury** — both agents are vesicants and cause severe local tissue damage.
- **SIADH and hyponatremia** with vinblastine in children; **phototoxicity** and jaw pain noted for some agents.

> [!warning] Neurotoxicity and combination selection
> Peripheral neuropathy from vincristine is the practical constraint that governs many regimen choices, particularly in older and chemotherapy-pretreated patients, and it interacts additively with other neurotoxic agents (including platinum agents, taxanes, and [[Doxorubicin]]-related cumulative toxicity). Antifungal azole coadministration has been reported to potentiate vinca neurotoxicity (PMID: 41630284) — a drug interaction worth checking rather than assuming.
