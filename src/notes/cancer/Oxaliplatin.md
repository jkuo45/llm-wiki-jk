# Oxaliplatin

## Chemical Properties & Classification

**Oxaliplatin** (marketed as Eloxatin; also developed as SAP) is a third-generation platinum chemotherapeutic and the only diaminocyclohexane platinum compound in clinical use. Its structure pairs a platinum(II) centre with two ammine ligands and a chelating (1R,2R)-cyclohexane-1,2-diamine (DACH) group, which is far less reactive than the ethylenediamine ligands of [[Cisplatin]] and substantially less nephro-, ototoxic and myelosuppressive.

The oxalate leaving group is displaced in the low-chloride intracellular environment to form the aqua species that binds nucleophilic sites on DNA. The bulky DACH ring makes the resulting adducts stereochemically distinct from cisplatin-type 1,2-intrastrand crosslinks and generates a higher proportion of 1,3-intrastrand and interstrand crosslinks.

## Pharmacodynamics & Pharmacokinetics

- **Mechanism**: Platinum adducts bend and distort DNA, stalling replication and transcription and triggering the Fanconi anaemia pathway, ATR/CHK1 checkpoint activation, and ultimately [[Apoptosis]] or senescence. The interstrand crosslinks are the most lethal lesion. Damage is amplified by thiol-rich proteins and by the sequestration of platinum by [[Glutathione]] and metallothioneins.
- **Kinetics**: Oxaliplatin is eliminated by renal excretion with a terminal half-life of ~350–400 hours, reflecting extensive and slow covalent binding to plasma proteins and red cells — the reason for its prolonged post-infusion exposure. Unlike cisplatin, it is not deactivated by high chloride concentrations and does not require hepatic detoxification, so it is less affected by renal or hepatic dysfunction.
- **Schedule dependence**: Efficacy depends on dose intensity and on the duration of infusion, with prolonged (6-hour) infusions reducing neurotoxicity relative to short (2-hour) boluses in some protocols.

## Applications

- **Colorectal cancer**: Oxaliplatin is a standard component of FOLFOX and CAPOX regimens in advanced [[Colorectal Cancer]] and [[Rectal Cancer]], both in the adjuvant setting (stage II–III) and in combination with biologic agents such as anti-EGFR or anti-VEGF antibodies. It has no meaningful single-agent activity in breast or lung cancer, unlike cisplatin and carboplatin.
- **Neurotoxicity**: Cumulative, dose-dependent, and often irreversible peripheral sensory neuropathy is the signature toxicity, presenting as cold-triggered dysesthesia in the hands and feet and as a dose-limiting cumulative effect that frequently requires dose reduction or discontinuation. Chronic pain and autonomic and cognitive sequelae ("chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy") are increasingly recognised.
- **Other toxicities**: Myelosuppression (neutropenia), diarrhoea, nausea, and, uniquely among platins, no substantial nephrotoxicity or ototoxicity. Oxaliplatin can trigger a rare acute cold-triggered dysesthesia syndrome during infusion, and there is a small, debated risk of tumour lysis in high-bulk disease.
- **Hypersensitivity**: Platinum salts, including oxaliplatin, can cause immediate-type hypersensitivity reactions; desensitisation protocols are used for rechallenge.
- **Resistance**: Effective resistance mechanisms include increased drug efflux via P-glycoprotein ([[P-glycoprotein]]), enhanced glutathione and metallothionein sequestration, DNA repair capacity, and tumour-intrinsic detoxification.
