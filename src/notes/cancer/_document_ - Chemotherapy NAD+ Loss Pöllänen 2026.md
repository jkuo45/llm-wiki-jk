# Chemotherapy Induces Tissue NAD+ Loss and Nrk2 Downregulation Marks Muscle Wasting

**Pöllänen et al. (2026)** — bioRxiv preprint

> [!info] Key Insight
> Chemotherapy alone — independent of tumor burden — can disrupt tissue [[NAD+]] metabolism. Skeletal muscle NAD+ loss accompanies [[Cachexia|muscle atrophy]], and the NAD+ biosynthetic enzyme [[NRK2|Nrk2]] is an early, robust marker of muscle mass changes across species.

## Abstract

Aberrant [[NAD+]] metabolism has been implicated in the pathogenesis of [[Cachexia|cancer cachexia]], highlighting this pathway as a potential therapeutic target to mitigate [[Skeletal Muscle|skeletal muscle]] wasting. This study investigated the effects of commonly used [[Chemotherapy]] regimens on NAD+ metabolism in skeletal muscle and liver of healthy mice.

> [!important] Methods
> Healthy mice were treated with 2-week regimens of [[FOLFIRI]] or [[Cisplatin]], or 5-week regimens of FOLFIRI or [[FOLFOX]], with vehicle-treated mice as controls. Given the consistent downregulation of Nrk2 in cachectic chemotherapy-treated mice, the authors examined skeletal muscle _Nrk2_/_NRK2_ expression across published murine and human cachexia datasets and in additional models of muscle wasting and hypertrophy.

## Key Findings

### Skeletal Muscle NAD+ Loss Associates with Chemotherapy-Induced Atrophy

- [[Cisplatin]] (2-week treatment) reduced muscle [[NAD+]] by −14% (p=0.047) alongside declines in [[NADH]] and [[NADPH]], consistent with severe body and muscle weight loss
- [[FOLFIRI]] (5-week treatment) elicited a near-significant decrease of −18% (p=0.069) in muscle NAD+ levels
- [[FOLFOX]] (5-week treatment) did not alter NAD+ metabolites, consistent with the absence of overt muscle atrophy
- The [[NAD+]]/[[NADH]] ratio remained unchanged with cisplatin (proportional depletion), while the [[NADPH]]/NADPH ratio increased, suggesting a shift toward a more oxidized cellular state

> [!tip] Clinical Relevance
> The loss of skeletal muscle NAD+ associates with the development and severity of chemotherapy-induced muscle atrophy — not merely with chemotherapy exposure itself.

### Nrk2: An Early, Chemotherapy-Responsive NAD+ Biosynthetic Gene

- [[NRK2|Nrk2]] (nicotinamide riboside kinase 2, also known as _Nmrk2_, _Itgb1bp3_, or _Mibp_) was the most responsive NAD+ biosynthetic enzyme, showing consistent downregulation across all chemotherapy models with ongoing or developing muscle loss:
  - [[Cisplatin]]: −93% (p<0.001)
  - [[FOLFIRI]]: −84% (p<0.001)
  - [[FOLFOX]]: −92% (p<0.001)
- Nrk2 downregulation was detectable as early as 24 hours after FOLFIRI treatment, preceding any measurable muscle loss
- In contrast, [[NAMPT]] (the primary salvage pathway enzyme in muscle) was not consistently altered
- [[FOLFOX]] reduced muscle Nrk2 despite preserved NAD+ levels, accompanied by upregulation of the ubiquitously expressed isoform [[NRK2|Nrk1]], suggesting Nrk2 repression may precede NAD+ depletion or become functionally uncoupled from NAD+ biosynthesis

### Hepatic NAD+ Metabolism: Prolonged Chemotherapy Effects

- Neither FOLFIRI nor [[Cisplatin]] impacted hepatic NAD+ levels at 2 weeks
- At 5 weeks, both FOLFIRI and FOLFOX induced modest but significant decreases in hepatic NAD+ levels (−15 to −20%)
- Treatment-specific reorganization of hepatic NAD+ biosynthesis was observed, with distinct alterations in the [[Preiss-Handler pathway]] and salvage pathway gene expression
- Hepatic NAD+ consumption (via [[PARP]] enzymes) was not increased, suggesting altered biosynthetic capacity rather than increased consumption drives hepatic NAD+ loss

### Cross-Species Nrk2 Expression Analysis

> [!info] Meta-Analysis Evidence
> A recent meta-analysis by Zhao et al. encompassing 13 studies across multiple mouse models (C26, KPC, LLC, KPP, KPC GEMM) identified [[NRK2|Nrk2]] as consistently downregulated in skeletal muscle across all cancer cachexia models.

- **Rodent cancer cachexia models**: Nrk2 downregulation was early and consistent across C26, LLC, KPC, KPC GEMM, and KPP GEMM models
- **Cachexia-targeted therapies**: Interventions that increase muscle mass (activin receptor ligand blockade, IL-6 deficiency, FoxO inhibition, GDF15 neutralization) restored Nrk2 expression
- **Human inactivity**: Skeletal muscle _NRK2_ expression was strongly decreased upon bed rest or limb immobilization
- **Human resistance training**: Significantly upregulated muscle _NRK2_ expression
- **Human cachexia**: _NRK2_ ranked among the top 20 most differentially expressed transcripts in cachectic pancreatic cancer patients
- **Sarcopenia**: Reduced _NRK2_ expression in elderly sarcopenic patients
- **Critical illness myopathy**: Reduced _NRK2_ expression compared to healthy counterparts

> [!important] Nrk2 as a Biomarker
> Muscle _Nrk2_ expression dynamically tracks changes in muscle mass, reflecting transitions between catabolic and anabolic muscle states. It may serve as a predictor of muscle mass or atrophy, with potential utility for monitoring treatment responses over time.

## Mechanistic Framework

Chemotherapy-induced muscle [[NAD+]] loss is primarily driven by impaired NAD+ biosynthesis rather than increased consumption. The consistent transcriptional response across chemotherapy treatments was the downregulation of muscle [[NRK2|Nrk2]]. Potential upstream regulators include:

- **[[PGC-1α]]/[[FOXO]] signaling**: PPARα is a known transactivator of Nrk2
- **Cytokine-driven [[Inflammation|inflammation]]**: via [[TNF-alpha|TNF-α]], [[IL-6]]
- **Activin signaling**: chemotherapy-induced activin signaling (reversed by activin inhibition)
- **[[Sirtuins|Sirtuin]]-dependent pathways**: NAD+ depletion compromises SIRT1/SIRT3 activity

> [!warning] Study Limitations
> Relatively small sample sizes, lack of detailed temporal analysis, and sex-specific differences (short-term experiments in males, long-term in females) should be acknowledged. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal designs and balanced sex representation.

## Conclusions

Chemotherapy-induced cachexia shares a core NAD+ metabolic signature with cancer cachexia, characterized by skeletal muscle NAD+ loss and [[NRK2|Nrk2]] downregulation. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting NAD+ metabolism to combat muscle loss during chemotherapy and identify muscle Nrk2 expression as a conserved and responsive marker of muscle mass changes.
