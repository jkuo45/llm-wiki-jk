## Overview

Acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL) is a distinct subtype of acute myeloid leukemia defined by the t(15;17) translocation that fuses the promyelocytic leukemia (PML) gene to the retinoic acid receptor alpha (RARA). The PML-RARA fusion blocks myeloid differentiation, producing an accumulation of promyelocytes. APL is highly responsive to differentiation therapy with all-trans retinoic acid and arsenic trioxide, which degrade the fusion protein.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

The PML-RARA oncoprotein acts as a dominant-negative transcriptional repressor of retinoic acid target genes, arresting differentiation at the promyelocyte stage. The balance between survival and differentiation of leukemic blasts is influenced by epigenetic and signaling regulators, among them the sirtuin deacetylases.

## SIRT2 and Therapeutic Potential

Directionally, [[SIRT2]] activity sits upstream of leukaemia cell survival and differentiation programs. Pharmacological modulation of [[SIRT2]] is being explored as a therapeutic strategy, potentially by restoring differentiation or promoting death of leukemic blasts. Because sirtuin inhibitors can shift the balance away from survival, [[SIRT2]] has been proposed as a promising drug target in APL.

> [!info] Mechanistic position
> SIRT2 is upstream of the survival/differentiation switch in APL blasts, so its inhibition may complement established PML-RARA-targeted therapy by pushing blasts toward differentiation or death.

## Clinical Presentation & Therapeutics

APL presents with cytopenias and a high risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation; prompt recognition is critical because early hemorrhagic mortality is high. First-line therapy combines all-trans retinoic acid with arsenic trioxide. The emerging [[Sirtuin Inhibitors]] directed at [[SIRT2]] represent a research-stage adjunct whose role in combination regimens remains to be defined.
