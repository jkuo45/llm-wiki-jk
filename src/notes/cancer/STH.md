# STH

**Soil-transmitted helminths (STH)** are a group of parasitic nematodes that infect the human intestine and complete part of their life cycle in soil. The main species are the roundworm *Ascaris lumbricoides*, the whipworm *Trichuris trichiura*, and the human hookworms *Ancylostoma duodenale* and *Necator americanus* (with *Ancylostoma ceylanicum* increasingly recognised). *Strongyloides stercoralis* shares the same faecal–soil transmission route but is handled separately by WHO because its diagnosis and treatment differ.

> [!info] A neglected tropical disease by definition and by burden
> STH affect roughly **1.5 billion people, about 24% of the world's population**, concentrated in warm, moist, low-sanitation settings in sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and Central and South America. Over 880 million children required preventive chemotherapy in 2024, of whom about 507 million (57.4%) received it. WHO classifies STH among the neglected tropical diseases.

## Transmission

Adult worms live in the intestine and release thousands of eggs daily into the environment. Transmission requires a break in sanitation:

- eggs contaminating soil and being ingested with unwashed raw vegetables or contaminated water;
- children playing in contaminated soil and then eating without washing;
- eggs reaching the mouth from contaminated hands or fomites;
- for hookworm specifically, **skin penetration** by filariform larvae when walking barefoot, or ingestion of larvae in *A. duodenale*.

*Ascaris* and *Trichuris* eggs must embryonate in soil before they are infective, and neither multiplies inside the human host — re-infection requires repeated environmental exposure. *Strongyloides* is the exception: it can autoinfect and multiply within the host, which is why reduced host immunity can trigger hyperinfection or dissemination, a syndrome that is invariably fatal if untreated.

## Pathology and clinical features

Morbidity scales with worm burden. Light infection is usually asymptomatic; heavy infection produces:

- **Intestinal and nutritional.** Diarrhoea, abdominal pain, and impaired nutrient absorption, contributing to malnutrition, stunting, and impaired physical and cognitive development in children. *Trichuris* characteristically causes colonic infection and rectal prolapse. *Ascaris* in heavy burden can obstruct the intestine, a surgical emergency.
- **Haematological.** Hookworms attach to the small-intestinal mucosa and feed on blood, causing chronic intestinal blood loss, anaemia, and protein loss — with the worst growth and cognitive effects in children.
- **Immunological.** Tissue-invasive larvae migrating through tissues provoke marked [[Eosinophilia]], which is often the laboratory signature of STH infection.

> [!warning] The hookworm anaemia mechanism is specific
> Blood loss from hookworm is chronic and quantifiable, so iron deficiency and not eosinophilia is the dominant pathology in chronic hookworm disease. In *Ascaris* and *Trichuris*, by contrast, nutritional impairment and eosinophilia predominate. The species, not the label, determines the clinical picture.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by stool microscopy for characteristic eggs, conventionally the Kato-Katz thick smear, which is quantitative and therefore allows egg-per-gram counts to grade intensity. In endemic settings, especially during mass drug administration, treatment is frequently empirical without individual diagnosis.

## Treatment and control

Single-dose [[Albendazole]] 400 mg or mebendazole 500 mg are the WHO-recommended medicines; both are inexpensive, oral, and deliverable by non-medical personnel such as teachers. Regimen efficacy differs by species — single doses work best against *Ascaris*, while *Trichuris* responds poorly to single doses and needs longer courses or combination therapy.

The control strategy is morbidity control by periodic mass administration to at-risk groups: preschool children, school-age children, women of reproductive age including pregnant and lactating women, and adults in high-risk occupations. Sanitation, safe water, and health education are equally necessary, since treatment alone does not interrupt the transmission cycle that reinfects treated individuals.

> [!important] Immunomodulatory relevance
> Chronic helminth infection drives a T helper 2 / regulatory T cell immunomodulatory phenotype. This is the mechanistic basis of the hygiene hypothesis — the observation that childhood helminth exposure associates with reduced allergic and autoimmune disease — and it means deworming trials have been read as immune-modifying interventions as much as antiparasitic ones. Whether mass deworming raises or lowers allergy risk remains contested.
