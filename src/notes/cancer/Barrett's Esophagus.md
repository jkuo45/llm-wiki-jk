# Barrett's Esophagus

**Barrett's esophagus** is a premalignant condition in which the normal stratified squamous epithelium of the distal esophagus is replaced by metaplastic, intestinalised columnar epithelium in response to chronic gastroesophageal reflux. It is the only identifiable precursor lesion of esophageal adenocarcinoma, and its detection defines a surveillance obligation.

> [!info] Definition requires both endoscopy and histology
> Diagnosis needs two things: salmon-coloured columnar mucosa extending at least 1 cm above the gastroesophageal junction on endoscopy, **and** biopsy confirmation of intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells. Length and circumferential extent are graded by the Prague C&M classification. Merely finding cardia-type intestinal metaplasia at the gastroesophageal junction does not qualify.

## Pathogenesis

Chronic exposure of the distal esophageal mucosa to acid, pepsin, and bile salts produces injury that the squamous epithelium cannot sustain. Repeated wounding and repair drives a metaplastic programme: stem-cell progenitors at the squamous-columnar junction re-specify toward an intestinal phenotype, producing goblet cells and absorptive columnar cells. These cells are intrinsically more acid-resistant, which is why the change is protective in the short term and carcinogenic in the long one.

Chronic inflammation is not incidental. Neutrophilic infiltration generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, including 8-nitroguanine, one of the most mutagenic DNA lesions known. Patients with Barrett's esophagus show markedly elevated 8-nitroguanine in esophageal epithelium, linking nitrative stress to the neoplastic cascade (see [[Nitrative Stress]]).

## Natural history

Progression is conventionally modelled as a stepwise sequence: reflux → metaplasia → low-grade dysplasia → high-grade dysplasia → intramucosal carcinoma → invasive adenocarcinoma. Histologic grading runs nondysplastic → indefinite for dysplasia → low-grade dysplasia → high-grade dysplasia → intramucosal carcinoma.

Annual risk of progression to cancer, by baseline histology:

| Baseline histology | Progression to esophageal cancer per year |
|---|---|
| No dysplasia | 0.1–0.33% |
| Low-grade dysplasia | 0.5% |
| High-grade dysplasia | 7% |

> [!warning] Most patients never progress
> The absolute majority of patients with Barrett's esophagus do not develop cancer. Reported progression rates vary widely with study design, definition of Barrett's, and whether neoplasia found in the first surveillance year is counted; surveillance detects earlier-stage cancer but has not consistently lowered all-cause mortality after lead-time adjustment. Overdiagnosis is a genuine concern, and there is no expert consensus on an age to stop surveying.

## Risk factors

Male sex, smoking, increasing segment length, and confirmed low-grade dysplasia are the strongest predictors. Central obesity is an independent risk factor acting through raised intra-abdominal pressure and reflux promotion. *Helicobacter pylori* infection appears in some analyses to be protective, plausibly via reduced acid secretion.

## Management

**Acid suppression.** Proton pump inhibitor therapy controls reflux symptoms and is associated with reduced risk of progression, though it does not eliminate surveillance need.

**Surveillance endoscopy.** Non-dysplastic Barrett's with two negative examinations can be spaced to three-year intervals; confirmed low-grade dysplasia warrants closer follow-up or endoscopic therapy. Surveillance typically uses high-definition white-light endoscopy combined with chromoendoscopy or narrow-band imaging, with four-quadrant biopsies (Seattle protocol). The AGA 2025 guideline recommends against surveillance for short-segment columnar lining under 1 cm with intestinal metaplasia alone.

**Ablation for dysplasia.** Endoscopic ablation reduces the absolute risk of progression to high-grade dysplasia or cancer in low-grade dysplasia by about 11% (NNT ≈ 10). High-grade dysplasia carries 30–40% cancer risk and was historically managed with esophagectomy; endoscopic eradication therapy has largely replaced surgery for patients fit for endoscopic treatment.
