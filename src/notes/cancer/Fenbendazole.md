# Fenbendazole

[[Fenbendazole]] is a broad-spectrum [[Benzimidazole]] anthelmintic agent used to treat gastrointestinal parasites in animals, including giardia, roundworms, hookworms, whipworms, and certain tapeworms. It is being investigated as a candidate for [[Drug Repositioning]] in [[Cancer]] therapy due to its ability to interfere with [[Microtubule]] polymerization and modulate cellular energy metabolism.

## Overview

Fenbendazole (methyl N-(6-phenylsulfanyl-1H-benzimidazol-2-yl)carbamate) is not FDA/EMA-approved for human use; its human pharmacokinetics are inferred from animal studies and from its major metabolite [[Oxfendazole]], which has completed a phase I trial. Its low cost, oral availability, and unique anti-proliferative activities make it the preferred benzimidazole for cancer repurposing over [[Albendazole]] and [[Mebendazole]] (which is used human antiparasitically).

## Mechanism of Action in Cancer

Fenbendazole exerts multiple, partially overlapping anticancer effects:

- **Microtubule destabilization:** Acts as a moderate [[Microtubule|microtubule destabilizing agent]], similar in class to [[Vincristine]] but with lower toxicity, inducing G2/M arrest (4, 10, 35).
- **Glycolysis / glucose starvation:** Suppresses [[Glycolysis]] by down-regulating [[Glucose uptake]] (via [[GLUT1]]) and inhibiting [[Hexokinase 2]]; reduces lactate and reverses drug resistance (4, 7, 13-17).
- **p53 activation:** Increases [[p53]] activity by down-regulating [[MDM2]] and [[MdmX]], driving mitochondrial p53-p21 signaling that suppresses GLUT expression (4, 35).
- **Oxidative stress / MAPK:** Induces [[Oxidative Stress]] and activates the MEK3/6-[[p38 MAPK]] pathway, inhibiting proliferation and enhancing [[Apoptosis]] (37).
- **Proteasome impairment:** Inhibits proteasomal function, contributing to apoptotic cancer-cell death (58).

> [!info] Source: [[Oral Fenbendazole for Cancer Therapy in Humans and Animals]]
> In colorectal cancer cells, fenbendazole triggers [[Apoptosis]] through mitochondrial injury and the [[Caspase-3]]-[[PARP]] pathway. In wild-type CRC it activates p53-mediated apoptosis; in 5-FU-resistant CRC it triggers apoptosis without changing p53, likely via p53-independent [[Ferroptosis]]-augmented apoptosis (5). It also induces [[Necrosis]] and [[Autophagy]].

> [!info] Source: [[Oral Fenbendazole for Cancer Therapy in Humans and Animals]]
> Compared to albendazole, fenbendazole was more effective against 5-FU-resistant colorectal cells, likely because of its intervention in [[Glycolysis]] (5); it also showed activity against [[Paclitaxel]]- and [[Docetaxel]]-resistant cells (5, 8, 9).

## Pharmacokinetics & Metabolism

Orally administered fenbendazole has very low water solubility (~0.3 μ g/ml) and permeability, with only ~5% released in 15 min and ~81% in 1 h (11, 12); in male rats (10 mg/kg p.o.) Cmax ≈ 0.32 μ g/ml, Tmax 0.25 h, T1/2 ≈ 8.2 h (10). It is metabolized in the liver by [[Flavin-containing Monooxygenase|FMO]] and [[CYP3A4]] to the active sulfoxide [[Oxfendazole]], by [[CYP2J2]]/[[CYP2C19]] to [[Hydroxyfenbendazole]], and further to [[Fenbendazole sulfone]] (38-43). Excretion is mainly fecal (cattle: 36% recovered in feces) (46).

## Solubility & Bioavailability Vehicles

Because poor solubility limits systemic exposure, formulations are explored (Table III): [[Methyl-beta-cyclodextrin]] (1:1 complex → 20.21 mg/ml solubility, 75% release in 15 min) (11), [[Dimethyl Sulfoxide]]/DNTC (also inhibits 2C19/3A4, prolonging exposure) (51-53), [[Soluplus]] micelles (lower clearance) (60), [[Salicylic acid]] cocrystals (100% release in <1 h) (12), and [[PLGA]] nanoparticles (enhanced ovarian-cancer activity) (8).

## Safety, Toxicity & Human Use

In animals fenbendazole shows a wide safety margin (rodent LD50 > 10 g/kg; no carcinogenesis or reproductive toxicity, though hepatocellular hypertrophy/hyperplasia observed) (47, 48). [[Oxfendazole]] phase I data in humans showed acceptable tolerability (49, 43). Self-administration case reports describe tumor reduction (e.g., [[Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma]], bladder urothelial carcinoma) but also reversible drug-induced liver injury when combined with [[Pembrolizumab]] or other agents (54-57). Common anecdotal regimen: 1 g orally daily × 3 days, then 4 days off. Patients with liver compromise should use caution.

## Therapeutic Potential

Studied across skin, lung ([[non-small-cell lung cancer]], [[Small-cell lung cancer]]), colorectal, breast, ovarian, prostate, bladder, and lymphoma models, plus radiation-enhancement contexts. Also shows activity against [[Bleomycin]]-induced [[Pulmonary Fibrosis]] (36).

> [!warning] Conflicting evidence: EMT6 model
> In the EMT6 mouse mammary tumor model, fenbendazole showed in vitro cytotoxicity at high concentrations (1-3 μM, 24 h) with modest preferential toxicity under [[Hypoxia]], but **did not alter tumor growth or radiation response in vivo** when given as three daily i.p. injections (50 mg/kg/day) or in diet (150 ppm). Toxicity with [[Docetaxel]] was strictly additive, with no synergism observed. The authors concluded there was no evidence to support further testing of fenbendazole for cancer therapy, though they noted related compounds (e.g., [[Flubendazole]]) merit investigation (Duan et al. 2013).
