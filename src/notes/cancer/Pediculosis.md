# Pediculosis

Pediculosis is an infestation by ectoparasitic lice. It comprises three clinical forms: **head lice** (*Pediculus humanus capitis*), **body lice** (*Pediculus humanus corporis*), and **pubic lice** (crabs, *Pthirus pubis*). Head louse infestation is the most prevalent, affecting an estimated 6–12 million children per year in the United States alone. Body lice are associated with homelessness, overcrowding, and poor sanitation, and serve as vectors for several serious bacterial pathogens. Pubic lice are typically sexually transmitted.

## Biology

Adult lice are small, flattened, wingless insects 2–4 mm in length with six legs equipped with claws adapted for grasping hair shafts or clothing fibers. They undergo complete metamorphosis: eggs (nits) are glued to the base of hair shafts or clothing seams, hatch into nymphs, and develop into mature adults over 2–3 weeks. Lice feed exclusively on human blood every 2–6 hours and cannot survive more than 24–48 hours away from a host. Nits appear as small, oval, white-to-yellow specks firmly cemented to the hair shaft and are not easily dislodged, distinguishing them from dandruff or hair debris.

## Body Lice as Vectors

Body lice are of particular public health concern because they transmit three major pathogens: *Rickettsia prowazekii* (epidemic typhus), *Bartonella quintana* (trench fever), and *Borrelia recurrentis* (relapsing fever). These louse-borne diseases flourish under conditions of war, natural disaster, refugee displacement, and chronic homelessness. Epidemic typhus remains a re-emerging threat in impoverished and conflict-affected regions worldwide.

## Clinical Presentation

The hallmark symptom of pediculosis is intense pruritus (itching) driven by a hypersensitivity reaction to louse saliva. Persistent scratching leads to excoriations, secondary bacterial impetiginization, and cervical or occipital lymphadenopathy (particularly with head lice). Nits and crawling lice may be visible on examination of the scalp, hair, and body. In body louse infestation, lice and nits are most often found in the seams of clothing rather than directly on the skin. Pubic lice may also involve the eyelashes (pediculosis palpebrarum) in children, eyebrows, beard, axillary, and perianal hair.

## Treatment

**Head lice**: First-line therapy is 1% permethrin cream rinse applied to damp hair and left for 10 minutes. Alternative agents include spinosad (Natroba), topical [[Ivermectin]] lotion 0.5% (Sklice), and malathion lotion 0.5%. Manual nit removal with a fine-toothed comb is recommended to remove residual nits. Resistance to permethrin is increasingly reported, necessitating rotation of agents.

**Body lice**: Treatment focuses on improving hygiene and laundering clothing and bedding in hot water. In institutional or outbreak settings, 5% permethrin powder can be applied to clothing. Antibiotic therapy is required if secondary infection or louse-borne disease is present (e.g., doxycycline for typhus or trench fever).

**Pubic lice**: Treated with 1% permethrin cream rinse or oral [[Ivermectin]] 200 µg/kg repeated in 7–10 days. Sexual partners and close household contacts should be treated simultaneously. Clothing and bedding should be washed in hot water. Excoriations and secondary infection reflect local [[Inflammation]].
