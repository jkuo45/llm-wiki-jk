# Tumor Lysis Syndrome

**Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS)** is an acute, potentially fatal metabolic emergency that occurs when a large burden of malignant cells is destroyed — usually within hours to a few days of starting cytotoxic or targeted therapy, but sometimes spontaneously. The released intracellular contents overwhelm physiological excretory capacity, producing the biochemical triad of **hyperuricaemia, hyperkalaemia and hyperphosphataemia**, with secondary **hypocalcaemia** and **acute kidney injury**.

## Pathophysiology

- **Nucleic acids → urate.** Tumour cells have high nucleotide turnover and, after lysis, release far more nucleic acid than normal cell turnover. Purines are oxidised by [[Xanthine Oxidase|xanthine oxidase]] to [[Uric Acid|uric acid]], which is poorly soluble and precipitates in the renal tubules, causing obstructive and crystal nephropathy. Nucleic acid breakdown also consumes bicarbonate, worsening metabolic acidosis.
- **Potassium release.** Cell lysis liberates intracellular [[Potassium|K+]] faster than the kidney can excrete it; hyperkalaemia is the immediate killer, causing fatal arrhythmias.
- **Phosphate release.** Hyperphosphataemia develops early and is *causally upstream* of hypocalcaemia: calcium is deposited as calcium phosphate in tissues and vessels, suppressing parathyroid hormone release. Early hypocalcaemia is a hallmark of **established laboratory TLS**.
- **Calcium and the [[NLRP3 Inflammasome]].** Uric acid released as monosodium urate crystals can trigger innate immune activation; the resulting inflammation further impairs renal clearance.

> [!important] Laboratory versus clinical TLS
> Modified **Cairo-Bishop** criteria define laboratory TLS by meeting two or more abnormalities (uric acid ≥8 mg/dL or a 25% rise; potassium ≥6 mmol/L or a 25% rise; phosphate ≥4.5 mg/dL in adults; calcium ≤7 mg/dL or a 25% fall — within 3 days before or 7 days after treatment initiation). **Clinical TLS** additionally requires one of: acute kidney injury, cardiac arrhythmia, cardiac failure or seizure. Risk stratification therefore precedes the first dose, because prophylaxis is the intervention that matters.

## Risk Factors

Highest risk: high tumour burden and high proliferation rate — acute leukaemias, high-grade and bulky [[Lymphoma]]. Modifying factors: large white cell count, elevated LDH, elevated baseline urate, pre-existing renal impairment, dehydration, and the agent itself. Modern culprits include fludarabine combinations, high-dose cytarabine, dasatinib, venetoclax (with or without rituximab) and dual-drug immunomodulation. **Spontaneous TLS** occurs in untreated high-burden CLL and aggressive lymphoma, and **solid-tumour TLS** is increasingly reported with targeted and immune checkpoint therapies in high-burden, liver-metastatic disease.

## Management

- **Aggressive IV hydration** (2.5–3 L/m²/day) is the mainstay, maintaining urine output above 100 mL/h.
- **Rasburicase** — recombinant urate oxidase, preferred for high-risk and established TLS because it degrades uric acid directly rather than preventing its formation. Avoid alkaline urine, since alkaline pH promotes calcium phosphate precipitation.
- **Allopurinol** — xanthenine oxidase inhibitor for prophylaxis in moderate-risk patients; it does not reduce existing urate.
- **Electrolyte management** — treat hyperkalaemia and hypocalcaemia urgently; do not correct asymptomatic hypocalcaemia (may worsen calcium phosphate deposition).
- **Renal replacement therapy** for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload or anuria.

## Connections

- [[Uric Acid]] — Hyperuricaemia is the initiating event of TLS and the target of both prophylaxis and therapy; urate precipitation in the tubule is the main mechanism of the associated kidney injury.
- [[Allopurinol]] — Allopurinol is standard prophylaxis in moderate-risk patients, given before and for several days after chemotherapy, but it prevents rather than clears urate.
- [[Rasburicase]] — Rasburicase is the treatment of choice for high-risk or established TLS and is far faster than allopurinol at lowering urate.
- [[Acute Kidney Injury]] — AKI is the most common organ injury in TLS, both cause and consequence of urate nephropathy, and the key clinical criterion separating clinical from laboratory TLS.
- [[Venetoclax]] — Venetoclax produces unusually high rates of laboratory TLS in CLL, so the label requires tumour-burden reduction, dose ramp-up, and rasburicase prophylaxis — a template for managing at-risk targeted agents.
- [[Lymphoma]] — Bulky, high-grade lymphoma is the classic high-risk setting; its histology (LDH, white cell count, bulk) determines the prophylaxis strategy.
- [[Potassium]] — Hyperkalaemia released from lysed cells is the most rapidly lethal component of TLS and the reason continuous ECG monitoring accompanies the metabolic derangement.
