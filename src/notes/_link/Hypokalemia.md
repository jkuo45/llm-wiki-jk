# Hypokalemia

**Hypokalemia** is a serum [[Potassium]] concentration below 3.5 mmol/L, or below 3.0 mmol/L when ECG changes are present. It is the most common electrolyte abnormality encountered in hospital practice and is a marker of both catabolic illness and of impaired renal or gastrointestinal handling of potassium.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

**Gastrointestinal losses (shift from intracellular to extracellular pool):**

- Vomiting and nasogastric suction produce volume depletion with secondary hyperaldosteronism, plus loss of gastric HCl.
- Diarrhoea and ileostomy or colostomy cause large fecal potassium losses, usually with metabolic acidosis.
- Laxative overuse and bowel obstruction contribute.

**Renal losses:**

- **Aldosterone excess:** primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome), secondary hyperaldosteronism from renal artery stenosis, congestive heart failure, cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome; renin-producing tumours; Liddle syndrome; apparent mineralocorticoid excess (cortisol or 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase deficiency).
- **Renal tubular losses:** Bartter and Gitelman syndromes (loop and distal tubule defects), Gordon syndrome (proximal tubule), renal tubular acidosis type 1 and 2, and nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.
- **Drugs:** loop diuretics (furosemide), thiazides, osmotic diuretics, amphotericin B, aminoglycosides, penicillins, cisplatin, calcineurin inhibitors, beta-agonists (shift, not loss), and −Na/K-ATPase agonists.

**Transcellular shifts (redistribution):**

- **Metabolic alkalosis** — the classic setting; potassium shifts into cells as H+ shifts out, and alkalosis increases renal K+ and bicarbonate loss.
- Insulin therapy, beta-2 agonists (salbutamol), catecholamines, and respiratory or metabolic acidosis treated with bicarbonate.
- **Hypothermia** and digitalis excess.

**Reduced intake and malabsorption:** anorexia nervosa, prolonged fasting, parenteral nutrition without adequate potassium, and malabsorption.

**Magnesium depletion is mechanistically central:** intracellular magnesium blocks ROMK potassium channels; when magnesium is depleted this block is lifted and renal potassium wasting accelerates. Conversely, hypokalemia itself causes renal magnesium wasting. The two are therefore tightly interdependent, and potassium repletion fails until magnesium is also corrected.

**Total body potassium is always depleted in true hypokalemia**, since serum potassium is only ~2% of total body potassium; a small change in serum concentration reflects a large change in total body stores.

## Clinical Presentation & Biomarkers

- **Neuromuscular:** weakness, fatigue, cramps, hyporeflexia, and in severe cases paralysis, rhabdomyolysis and respiratory muscle weakness.
- **Cardiac:** the most dangerous consequence. Hypokalemia prolongs the repolarization phase, increases automaticity and shortens the QT interval, predisposing to premature atrial and ventricular beats, atrioventricular block, torsades de pointes and ventricular fibrillation. It also increases digitalis toxicity and myocardial ischaemia risk. Arrhythmia risk rises with rapid repletion.
- **Endocrine and metabolic:** impaired insulin release causing hyperglycaemia, and alkalosis shifting potassium intracellularly (pseudohypokalemia).
- **Chronicity:** sustained hypokalemia causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and, in severe or prolonged cases, hypokalaemic nephropathy with tubulointerstitial fibrosis.
- **Biomarkers:** serum potassium with ECG; urine potassium to distinguish renal from extrarenal loss (high urine K+ in renal loss, low in GI loss and shifts); plasma renin and aldosterone; serum magnesium; acid–base status.

## Therapeutic Landscape

- **Treat the cause:** stop offending drugs, replace volume, treat vomiting/diarrhoea, manage heart failure and ascites, and address primary hyperaldosteronism (aldosterone antagonists or adrenalectomy).
- **Repletion:** oral potassium chloride (preferred for routine use), or intravenous potassium for severe hypokalemia, arrhythmia, or when oral intake is impossible. Replacement is guided by ECG and frequent electrolytes. Insulin and glucose (with or without dextrose) drive potassium intracellularly and are useful when rapid correction is needed.
- **Magnesium:** replete magnesium first or concurrently; magnesium sulfate is the usual route when there is deficiency.
- **Caution:** correction is usually limited to about 20–25 mmol/L per 24 hours (lower in severe renal failure), because over-rapid repletion risks rebound hyperkalaemia and, in the presence of underlying ischaemia, cardiac complications.
- **Monitoring:** ECG monitoring during IV repletion, repeat electrolytes, and re-check potassium after any correction of alkalosis.
