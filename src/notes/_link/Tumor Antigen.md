## Overview

Tumor antigens are the molecular signatures by which the immune system distinguishes malignant cells from healthy tissue. They fall into several classes: tumor-specific mutated neo-antigens (highest immunogenicity), overexpressed or lineage differentiation antigens, oncofetal antigens, and viral oncoproteins. Presentation on **[[Major Histocompatibility Complex]]** molecules makes them visible to cytotoxic T cells; loss or down-regulation of antigen/MHC is a key route to **[[Immune Evasion]]**.

## Classes and Examples

- **Neo-antigens**: Arise from somatic mutations and are patient-specific; they drive response to checkpoint inhibitors and are targets for personalized vaccines.
- **Shared/tumor-associated antigens**: Overexpressed self-proteins (e.g., differentiation or oncofetal antigens) targeted by engineered T cells and antibodies.
- **Viral antigens**: Oncogenic virus-encoded proteins (e.g., HPV E6/E7, EBV) presented on MHC.

## Role in Immunity and Therapy

Tumor-antigen recognition initiates T-cell killing of **[[Cancer]]** cells and supports immunosurveillance. Therapeutic exploitation includes checkpoint blockade (which liberates pre-existing anti-tumor T-cell responses), adoptive cell therapy, and therapeutic cancer vaccines. Escape occurs via antigen loss, MHC down-regulation, or inhibitory checkpoint up-regulation.

> [!warning] Antigen heterogeneity and MHC loss variants permit immune-edited relapse; combinatorial targeting of multiple antigens reduces outgrowth of antigen-negative clones.
