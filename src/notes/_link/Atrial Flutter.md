# Atrial Flutter

**Atrial flutter** is a supraventricular re-entrant [[Arrhythmias|arrhythmia]] in which an impulse circulates around a fixed anatomical circuit in the atrium, producing a rapid, *regular* atrial rate — classically about 300 bpm — with a variable AV block determining the ventricular rate. It is distinct from [[Atrial Fibrillation]] by its organised macroreentrant mechanism, which is also why it is far more curable.

> [!info] Flutter is the "easy" atrial arrhythmia
> Where AF is a chaotic, self-sustaining, multi-circuit arrhythmia requiring lifelong rate/rhythm control and anticoagulation, typical flutter is usually a single circuit in a fixed anatomical isthmus. A single catheter lesion across that isthmus achieves high acute and durable success. Flutter also carries a lower thromboembolic risk than AF, though the distinction is one of degree — anticoagulation decisions follow AF guidance in most guidelines.

## Classification

**Typical (isthmus-dependent) flutter** is the commonest form by a wide margin. It is a counterclockwise macro-reentrant circuit in the right atrium that runs down the lateral wall, across the **cavotricuspid isthmus** (between the tricuspid annulus and the inferior vena cava), up the septum, and back. A clockwise variant is mirror-image. ECG signature: continuous, regular "sawtooth" negative flutter waves in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF), flat atrial deflections in I and aVL, and typically 2:1 conduction giving a ventricular rate near 150 bpm.

**Atypical (non-isthmus-dependent) flutter** arises around other barriers — pulmonary veins, surgical scars, suture lines, the mitral annulus, crista terminalis, coronary sinus ostium — in either atrium. Because flutter-wave morphology depends on activation *outside* the circuit, an atypical circuit can occasionally produce a typical-looking ECG; confirmation requires electrophysiological mapping, and success rates are lower with higher recurrence.

> [!warning] Post-ablation AF is common after flutter ablation
> Ablating the isthmus removes the circuit but leaves the underlying atrial substrate — stretch, fibrosis, hypertension, age — that supports atrial arrhythmia generally. Concomitant or subsequent AF is frequent, so "success" at flutter ablation does not eliminate the need for ongoing surveillance, and many patients eventually need AF-directed management.

## Mechanism

Re-entry requires a substrate plus a trigger. The substrate is the electrically remodelled atrium: fibrosis and slow conduction create slow-conduction pathways bordered by anatomical barriers (tricuspid annulus, venae cavae, crista). The trigger is an ectopic beat that finds a wavefront able to travel around the loop while the tissue ahead of it has recovered excitability. Purkinje or pulmonary-vein ectopic discharges supply the premature beat.

Maintenance depends on the circuit wavelength — conduction velocity × refractory period. Any intervention that shortens the refractory period (e.g. atrial stretch, thyrotoxicosis, catecholamines) shrinks the wavelength below the path length of the circuit and makes re-entry impossible.

## Clinical Presentation and Management

- **Symptoms** — palpitations, fatigue, dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance; some patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally.
- **Risk factors** — advancing [[Aging|age]], hypertension, valvular disease, ischaemic heart disease, obesity, [[Diabetes]], sleep apnoea, and post-cardiac-surgery states. Atrial remodelling increases with age, so prevalence rises steeply in older cohorts, and flutter and AF coexist in the same patients far more often than they occur apart.
- **Rate control** — beta-blockers or rate-limiting calcium channel blockers slow AV conduction; often insufficient alone in typical flutter because the circuit rate is so high.
- **Cardioversion** — low-energy synchronised cardioversion reliably terminates typical flutter, unlike AF where repeated shocks are often needed.
- **Ablation** — cavotricuspid isthmus ablation is first-line for recurrent symptomatic typical flutter, with success rates in the high nineties and low complication rates. Ablation performed during sinus rhythm versus during the flutter itself shows comparable long-term freedom from typical flutter recurrence.
- **Anticoagulation** — guided by AF-type risk scores; reduced relative to AF but not zero.

> [!important] Flutter needs structural heart assessment
> Flutter occurring in the setting of recent cardiac surgery, prior ablation, or in a young patient without conventional risk factors should prompt a search for the scar or congenital substrate sustaining it, because that changes the ablation strategy from isthmus-based to circuit-based mapping.

## Connections

- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] — the other common atrial arrhythmia; flutter is the organised relative of AF, shares substrate and risk factors, and frequently coexists or interconverts with it after ablation.
- [[Arrhythmias]] — the parent electrophysiological category; the vault's Arrhythmias note is one of the notes that names flutter as a distinct entity.
- [[Heart Failure]] — a common consequence of tachycardiopathy: sustained rapid atrial rate reduces ventricular filling and, over months, produces dilated cardiomyopathy that may not fully reverse after conversion.
- [[Cardiac Fibrosis]] — the fibrotic substrate in which the re-entrant circuit is anatomically fixed, and the reason atrial substrate persists after a successful isthmus lesion.
- [[Calcium Signaling]] — action potential duration and calcium handling underlie the atrial action potential and its susceptibility to early afterdepolarisation-driven arrhythmia.
- [[Aging]] — atrial remodelling, fibrosis and reduced compliance accumulate with age, which is why atrial arrhythmia prevalence rises steeply in the oldest cohorts.
