# Caffeine

**Caffeine** (1,3,7-trimethylxanthine, MW 194.19) is a purine alkaloid of the
methylxanthine class and the most widely consumed psychoactive substance in the world.
It is produced by *Coffea*, *Camellia sinensis*, *Theobroma cacao*, *Cola nitida* and
*Paullinia cupana*, where it functions as an allelopathic chemical — inhibiting
germination of competing seeds and deterring herbivory.

## Mechanism

> [!info] Adenosine receptor antagonism is the real mechanism
> Caffeine is a close structural relative of [[Adenosine]] and acts principally as a
> **competitive antagonist at adenosine A1, A2A and A2B receptors**. Because adenosine
> is a pervasive inhibitory metabolite whose concentration rises with wakefulness and
> falls with sleep, blocking its receptors produces the alerting, reduced-fatigue and
> enhanced-vigilance effects. The A2A receptor in particular has become a major target
> in Parkinson's disease research, and A2A antagonists are in clinical development
> as non-dopaminergic anti-parkinsonian agents.
>
> The other classic mechanisms — phosphodiesterase inhibition raising cyclic AMP,
> intracellular calcium release, and GABA receptor antagonism — are real but occur at
> concentrations well above those reached by ordinary consumption, and their contribution
> to the ordinary pharmacological effect of a cup of coffee is small. This is worth
> stating plainly because the "PDE inhibitor" framing dominates older popular accounts.

Peripheral effects include [[Beta-Adrenergic Receptor|beta-adrenergic]]-like
sympathomimetic actions (increased catecholamine release, mild diuresis via renal
adenosine antagonism), bronchodilation, and increased gastric acid secretion.

## Pharmacokinetics

Absorption is rapid and near-complete, with peak plasma concentration at 30–75 minutes.
Caffeine is almost entirely metabolised by hepatic [[Cytochrome P450|CYP1A2]] to
paraxanthine (most abundant), theobromine and theophylline, then excreted in urine with
a half-life of roughly 4–6 hours — but substantially longer in pregnancy (up to ~15–20
hours) and in oral contraceptive users, because of [[CYP1A2]] inhibition. The
half-life shift is the single most pharmacologically important fact about caffeine and
underlies the recommendation to limit intake in pregnancy.

## Physiological and clinical effects

- **Central nervous system** — reduced fatigue and drowsiness, improved reaction time,
  vigilance and, at higher doses, reduced accuracy and impaired fine motor control.
  Caffeine does not actually increase baseline energy expenditure; it only offsets the
  withdrawal-related dip.
- **Cardiovascular** — a modest rise in systolic blood pressure and heart rate, largely
  offset by increased vascular tone. Chronic intake produces partial tolerance.
- **Arrhythmias** — see the caveat below.
- **Parkinson's disease** — a modest protective association in prospective cohorts;
  this holds even in filtered-coffee studies where the association persists with
  decaf, indicating the mechanism is not decaf-specific.
- **Cancer** — no convincing association; the residual signal seen in some cohorts is
  attributable to confounding by smoking, and coffee and tea separately do not raise
  cancer risk.
- **Respiratory** — caffeine is a treatment for apnea of prematurity and is used in
  bronchopulmonary dysplasia of prematurity, where it improves weight gain and reduces
  the incidence of cerebral palsy.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat — cardiac
> Caffeine has a biphasic relationship with cardiac electrophysiology. A single large
> dose can prolong the QT interval and increase the risk of ventricular ectopy and
> short runs of ventricular tachycardia in susceptible individuals, and coffee intake
> has been epidemiologically associated with new-onset [[Arrhythmias|atrial fibrillation]]
> and with more frequent [[Ventricular arrhythmias|ventricular ectopy]] — the latter
> mainly in habitual consumers at doses above roughly 400 mg/day, where the relationship
> is U-shaped. However, in healthy volunteers, moderate habitual intake does not
> increase [[Atrial Fibrillation]] risk, and cohort data on hard cardiovascular
> endpoints are reassuring. Clinically the actionable points are: avoid large bolus
> doses, be cautious in patients with long QT or ventricular arrhythmia, and note that
> the electrocardiographic effects are largely offset by the diuresis and potassium
  loss. Patients with [[Arrhythmias]] are generally advised to keep intake moderate
  rather than abstain entirely, but individual tolerance varies widely and abstinence
  from a high baseline can itself provoke withdrawal tachyarrhythmia.

## Tolerance and withdrawal

Regular use produces tolerance to blood-pressure, heart-rate, nervousness and
alerting effects, with upregulation of adenosine receptors — habitual users operate
from a lowered arousal baseline, so the same dose produces a smaller subjective
effect. Cessation after daily use causes withdrawal in 25–50% of consumers: headache,
sleepiness, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and, in high-dependent
individuals, transient [[Arrhythmias]]. Symptoms peak at 12–24 hours and last 2–9
days.

> [!warning] Safety limits
> The FDA classifies caffeine as generally recognised as safe at normal consumption
> levels. The European Food Safety Authority found no safety concern up to 400 mg/day
> (about 5.7 mg/kg) for non-pregnant adults and up to 200 mg/day for pregnant and
> lactating women. Lethal doses exceed 10 g/day for an adult — roughly 50–100 cups of
> coffee. The practical risk is not the coffee but the pure powdered caffeine sold as a
> supplement, where tablespoon quantities are fatal and accidental paediatric
> ingestion has been reported. A 6 oz cup of coffee typically contains 50–175 mg.

## Connections

- [[Arrhythmias]] — Caffeine acutely prolongs the QT interval and increases ventricular ectopy, and high habitual consumption is associated with more frequent ventricular arrhythmias and new-onset atrial fibrillation. The relationship is biphasic and threshold-dependent, so the clinical advice for an arrhythmia patient is to keep intake moderate and stable rather than to avoid it entirely.
- [[Adenosine]] — Adenosine is caffeine's principal target: a competitive A1/A2A/A2B antagonist. This single interaction explains the alerting effects, the diuresis, and why methylxanthines such as theophylline antagonise administered adenosine in clinical use.
- [[Parkinson's Disease]] — Prospective cohorts consistently show a modest inverse association between habitual coffee intake and Parkinson's disease risk, and A2A receptor antagonists are being developed as non-dopaminergic therapy. The mechanism is not established.
- [[Sleep]] — Caffeine's half-life exceeds the typical sleep period, so an afternoon dose measurably reduces deep sleep even when the subject does not perceive it, and reduces total sleep time.
- [[Neurotransmitters]] — Antagonising adenosine receptors disinhibits central neurons and increases acetylcholine release, which is the proximal route to the classic psychostimulant effect.
- [[Cyclophosphamide]] — not mechanistically related, but a recurring point of interest: caffeine has been tested as an adjuvant to reduce cyclophosphamide-induced haemorrhagic cystitis by promoting bladder mucosal repair.
- [[Doxorubicin]] — Caffeine is a well-characterised modulator of the DNA damage response, inhibits the ATM/ATR checkpoint, and has been studied both as a chemosensitiser and as a cardiotoxicity amplifier; the checkpoint-inhibition rationale is the reason it appears in this vault's cancer notes.
- [[NAD+]] — a minor, non-clinical observation: caffeine has been reported to raise hepatic NAD+ content in rodents. The effect is not established in humans and should not be used to justify caffeine consumption as an NAD+-raising intervention.
