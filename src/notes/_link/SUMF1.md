# SUMF1

**SUMF1** (sulfatase-modifying factor 1) encodes the human **C-alpha-formylglycine-generating enzyme (FGE)**, an ER-lumen dioxygenase (EC 1.8.3.7) that performs an unusual post-translational modification. FGE converts a specific cysteine residue in the active site of every human sulfatase into **C-alpha-formylglycine (FGly)**, the nucleophile that all eukaryotic sulfatases require to hydrolyze sulfate esters. SUMF1 is therefore an obligatory and limiting cofactor for the entire sulfatase family.

> [!info] Mechanism
> FGE is a copper-containing non-heme iron/ascorbate-dependent dioxygenase that oxidizes the target cysteine in three steps through a **sulfenic acid (Cys-SOH)** and a **double sulfonic acid (Cys-SO3H)** intermediate, then desulfurizes the latter to the FGly aldehyde. The aldehyde is a far stronger electrophile than the parent thiol, which is what lets sulfatases attack sulfate esters that ordinary cysteines cannot. FGE acts on the nascent polypeptide in the ER, transiently forming a covalent enzyme–substrate intermediate, and a conserved binding mechanism lets one FGE act on all human sulfatases rather than requiring a dedicated enzyme per substrate.

## What sulfatases do and why they need FGE

Sulfatases hydrolyze sulfate esters in [[Glycosaminoglycan|glycosaminoglycans]], sulfolipids, and steroid sulfates. In lysosomes, these include arylsulfatase A (degradation of cerebroside sulfatide), iduronate-2-sulfatase, and others; in the Golgi, steroid sulfatase (STS) desulfates steroid precursors. Because FGly is required for the catalytic mechanism itself, no sulfatase can function before FGE has processed it, and a defect in FGE disables the whole class at once. This is the structural reason multiple sulfatase deficiency presents as simultaneous dysfunction of many unrelated enzymes rather than of one.

## Multiple sulfatase deficiency

Biallelic loss-of-function mutations in SUMF1 cause **[[Multiple Sulfatase Deficiency]] (MSD, MIM 272200)**, a severe [[Lysosomal Storage Disease]]. Two independent 2003 papers in *Cell* — Dierks et al. and Cosma et al. — established that MSD is caused by mutations in the gene encoding human FGE and that the protein is an essential and limiting factor for sulfatase activity. Clinically, MSD presents in infancy with the storage features of individually described sulfatase deficiencies combined: coarse facial features, skeletal dysplasia (dysostosis multiplex), neurodevelopmental regression, and early death. Later work showed a range of hypomorphic SUMF1 variants producing attenuated, later-onset forms, and that FGE is not merely a passive maturase — the degree of residual FGE activity correlates with residual sulfatase activity across five sulfatase deficiencies.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> There is currently no approved therapy for MSD; management is supportive. The severity phenotype is difficult to predict from genotype alone, so a detected SUMF1 variant should be interpreted alongside measured residual sulfatase activity rather than treated as a deterministic prognosis.

## Connections

- [[Multiple Sulfatase Deficiency]] — MSD is caused by SUMF1 mutations. The disease is the direct phenotypic readout of loss of FGly generation, and its multi-enzyme character is fully explained by the shared maturation requirement.
- [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]] — FGE is an ER-lumen enzyme and acts co-translationally on nascent sulfatase polypeptides; the modification happens before the substrate reaches the lysosome or Golgi.
- [[Glycosaminoglycan]] — glycosaminoglycan sulfation is a major function of sulfatases, and their failure to be matured by FGE underlies the storage pathology in MSD.
- [[Post-translational Modification]] — FGly generation is an unusual case: a covalent, irreversible conversion of a standard residue into a non-proteinogenic one, without which the mature enzyme is catalytically dead.
- [[SUMOylation]] — a useful contrast case within the vault's PTM set. SUMOylation is a reversible, proteasome-independent modification that tunes activity, whereas FGly generation is an irreversible conversion that permanently enables catalysis; the two illustrate opposite solutions to the same problem of regulating enzyme function.
