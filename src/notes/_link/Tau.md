# Tau

Tau (microtubule-associated protein tau, MAPT) is a 352–441 amino acid intrinsically disordered protein encoded by the *MAPT* gene on chromosome 17q21.31. It is predominantly expressed in neurons, where it binds and stabilizes [[Microtubule|microtubules]] in axons. Six isoforms are produced by alternative splicing of exons 2, 3, and 10. [[Tau|Tau hyperphosphorylation]] and aggregation into [[Neurofibrillary tangles|neurofibrillary tangles]] (NFTs) is a pathological hallmark of [[Alzheimer's Disease]] and other tauopathies. Outside the nervous system, low-level Tau expression has been detected in kidneys, lungs, and hematopoietic cells, though its function in these tissues remains poorly characterized.

## Structure & Domains

Tau is classified as an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP): it lacks a stable three-dimensional structure under physiological conditions, existing instead as a dynamic ensemble of conformations. This disorder is functionally significant — it enables Tau to interact with multiple binding partners and adopt structure upon binding to [[Microtubule|microtubules]] or upon pathological aggregation into β-sheet-rich conformers.

**N-terminal projection domain (exons 0, 2, 3):** The N-terminus projects outward from the microtubule surface and mediates interactions with the plasma membrane and other cytoskeletal components. Alternative splicing of exons 2 and 3 generates three variants: 0N (no inserts), 1N (one insert), and 2N (two inserts). The N-terminal domain also contains a phosphatase-activating domain (PAD) that, when exposed by hyperphosphorylation, activates protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) and contributes to microtubule destabilization.

**Proline-rich region (PRR):** Located between the N-terminal domain and the microtubule-binding region, the PRR contains multiple PXXP motifs that serve as docking sites for SH3 domain-containing proteins, including the tyrosine kinase [[Fyn]], phospholipase C-γ (PLγ), and the growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (Grb2). These interactions link Tau signaling to receptor tyrosine kinase pathways and cytoskeletal remodeling.

**Microtubule-binding repeats (MTBR):** The central region contains three or four imperfect 31–32 residue repeats (R1–R4) that directly bind the luminal surface of microtubules via electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. Each repeat contains a conserved hexapeptide motif (PHF6* in R2 and R3) that is critical for both microtubule binding and pathological aggregation. Alternative splicing of exon 10 determines whether three repeats (3R, exon 10 excluded) or four repeats (4R, exon 10 included) are produced. The six canonical isoforms are generated by combinatorial splicing: 3R:0N, 3R:1N, 3R:2N, 4R:0N, 4R:1N, and 4R:2N. In the adult human brain, the 3R and 4R isoforms are expressed in approximately equal ratios; imbalances in this ratio are pathological — [[Pick's disease]] involves 3R-predominant inclusions, while [[Progressive supranuclear palsy]] and [[Corticobasal degeneration]] involve 4R-predominant inclusions.

**C-terminal domain:** The C-terminal tail contains the VQIVYK motif (residues 306–312), which is critical for the nucleation of β-sheet structure during fibril formation. This motif is buried in the hydrophobic core of Tau fibrils and is a key determinant of the conformational switch from disordered monomer to ordered aggregate.

## Mechanism of Action & Pathways

**Normal function — microtubule stabilization:** Tau binds microtubules via its MTBR repeats, promoting tubulin polymerization and stabilizing microtubule dynamics. This function is essential for axonal transport, where microtubules serve as tracks for kinesin (anterograde) and dynein (retrograde) motor proteins. Tau also regulates microtubule spacing and cross-linking with other cytoskeletal elements, including [[Actin]] filaments.

**Phosphorylation regulation:** Tau is one of the most heavily phosphorylated proteins in the human brain, with 85 potential serine/threonine phosphorylation sites identified. In a healthy neuron, approximately 2–3 moles of phosphate per mole of Tau are occupied. In [[Alzheimer's Disease]] brain, this increases to approximately 7–8 moles — a state termed hyperphosphorylation.

Key kinases responsible for Tau phosphorylation include:
- [[GSK3]] (glycogen synthase kinase-3β): the principal Tau kinase, phosphorylates residues in the MTBR and PRR
- [[CDK5]] (cyclin-dependent kinase 5): activated by p35/p25, targets disease-relevant epitopes
- [[DYRK1A]] (dual-specificity tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1A): upregulated in [[Down syndrome]], phosphorylates Tau at multiple AD-relevant sites
- Casein kinase 1 (CK1): implicated in priming phosphorylation that enables subsequent GSK-3β action
- AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase): activated by energy stress, phosphorylates Tau at Ser396 and Ser404
- [[p38 MAPK]] and [[JNK]] (c-Jun N-terminal kinase): stress-activated kinases that target Tau under oxidative conditions

The primary Tau phosphatase is PP2A, which accounts for approximately 70% of Tau dephosphorylation activity. PP1 and PP5 contribute to a lesser extent. Oxidative stress, inflammation, and amyloid-β oligomers can inhibit PP2A activity, tipping the kinase–phosphatase balance toward hyperphosphorylation.

**Aggregation cascade:** Hyperphosphorylated Tau detaches from microtubules, exposing the PHF6* hexapeptides and the VQIVYK motif. The free monomers misfold and self-associate through a seeded, prion-like mechanism:
1. **Monomer → oligomer:** Soluble oligomers are considered the most neurotoxic species, disrupting synaptic function and calcium homeostasis
2. **Oligomer → protofibril:** Intermediate aggregates that template further recruitment of native Tau
3. **Protofibril → paired helical filament (PHF):** The classic ultrastructural hallmark of Tau pathology, consisting of stacked β-sheets with a helical twist
4. **PHF → neurofibrillary tangle (NFT):** Dense intracellular inclusions that correlate with neuronal loss and cognitive decline

Tau aggregates propagate between neurons in a stereotypical anatomical pattern described by the Braak staging system (stages I–VI), progressing from the transentorhinal cortex to the hippocampus, limbic structures, and ultimately the neocortex. This propagation is thought to occur via exosomal release, tunneling nanotubes, or direct synaptic transmission of misfolded Tau seeds.

## Physiological Function

Beyond microtubule stabilization, Tau participates in several additional physiological processes:

- **Axonal transport:** By stabilizing microtubule tracks, Tau supports the long-range transport of mitochondria, synaptic vesicle precursors, and signaling endosomes along axons. Dysregulation of this function leads to synaptic energy deficits and neurodegeneration.
- **Neuroplasticity:** Activity-dependent Tau release from synapses has been demonstrated in vitro and in vivo. Released Tau may function as a trans-cellular signal that modulates synaptic plasticity in a manner analogous to [[BDNF]] signaling, though this remains under investigation.
- **DNA damage response:** Tau localizes to sites of DNA double-strand breaks in neurons, where it facilitates the recruitment of DNA repair factors including PARP1 and XRCC4. This non-canonical function may link Tau pathology to age-associated genomic instability.
- **Iron homeostasis:** Tau binds iron via histidine residues within the MTBR repeats. Pathological Tau aggregation may sequester iron, contributing to oxidative stress and ferroptotic cell death in tauopathies.
- **Signaling scaffold:** The N-terminal and proline-rich regions of Tau interact with signaling molecules including [[Fyn]], PLCγ, and Shc, positioning Tau as a scaffold that couples receptor tyrosine kinase signaling to cytoskeletal dynamics.

Tau is constitutively released from neurons into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and, at lower concentrations, into blood. The physiological significance of extracellular Tau is not fully understood, but it may serve as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that activates microglial [[Toll-like receptors]] and neuroinflammatory pathways.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

**Tauopathies:** A heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative diseases characterized by pathological Tau aggregation:

- [[Alzheimer's Disease]]: Hyperphosphorylated Tau in NFTs; Braak staging I–VI correlates with cognitive decline; Tau pathology follows a predictable anatomical progression from transentorhinal cortex to neocortex
- [[Pick's disease]]: Pick bodies composed of 3R Tau; frontotemporal dementia with circumscribed atrophy
- [[Progressive supranuclear palsy]] (PSP): 4R Tau inclusions in basal ganglia and brainstem; associated with the *MAPT* H1 haplotype; vertical gaze palsy and early falls
- [[Corticobasal degeneration]] (CBD): 4R Tau in cortical and subcortical regions; asymmetric apraxia and alien limb phenomenon
- Frontotemporal dementia with parkinsonism linked to chromosome 17 (FTDP-17): Caused by *MAPT* mutations; presents with behavioral variant FTD or parkinsonism
- [[Chronic traumatic encephalopathy]] (CTE): Tau-positive astrocytic tangles at cortical sulci; associated with repetitive head trauma
- Down syndrome: Accelerated AD pathology due to triplication of the *APP* gene on chromosome 21, combined with Tau pathology
- Niemann-Pick type C: Accumulation of unesterified cholesterol leads to Tau hyperphosphorylation via GSK-3β activation
- Argyrophilic grain disease: Small Tau-positive grains in limbic regions; common in elderly brains at autopsy

**MAPT mutations:** Over 50 pathogenic mutations have been identified, including missense mutations (e.g., P301L, V337M, R406W), splice site mutations (e.g., IVS10+16), and exon deletions. Most cause FTDP-17 or PSP/CBD-like phenotypes. The P301L mutation dramatically accelerates aggregation kinetics and is widely used in transgenic mouse models of tauopathy.

**Genetic risk:** The *MAPT* locus exhibits a common haplotype polymorphism. The H1 haplotype (which includes the H1c sub-haplotype) is associated with increased risk of PSP, CBD, and [[Alzheimer's Disease]], while the H2 haplotype is protective. GWAS studies have identified additional risk loci in *ABCA7*, *TREM2*, and *APOE* that interact with Tau pathology.

**Therapeutic strategies:**
- Anti-Tau antibodies: Semorinemab (targeting N-terminal Tau), zagotenemab (targeting MTBR), and E2814 (targeting the mid-region) are in clinical trials. These antibodies aim to neutralize extracellular Tau seeds and block cell-to-cell propagation.
- Tau aggregation inhibitors: Methylthioninium chloride (methylene blue) and its reduced form LMTM (leuco-methylthioninium) were tested in the TRx-237-005 and MODERATIS trials, with mixed results. The compound inhibits the assembly of Tau into PHFs.
- Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs): MAPT-targeting ASOs (e.g., BIIB080) reduce Tau production by degrading *MAPT* mRNA. Phase I data showed dose-dependent reductions in CSF Tau levels.
- Kinase inhibitors: GSK-3β inhibitors (tideglusib, lithium) and CDK5 inhibitors aim to reduce Tau hyperphosphorylation. Lithium, despite being the first Tau kinase inhibitor tested, showed equivocal results in trials, possibly due to insufficient brain exposure.
- Active Tau vaccines: AADvac1 (targeting the PHF6* epitope) and ACI-35 (targeting phosphorylated S396/S404) aim to elicit anti-Tau immune responses. AADvac1 completed a Phase II safety trial.
- Small molecule degraders: PROTACs and molecular glues targeting Tau for proteasomal or lysosomal degradation are in preclinical development.
