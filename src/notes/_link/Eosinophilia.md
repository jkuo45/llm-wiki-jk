# Eosinophilia

**Eosinophilia** is an elevated absolute eosinophil count (AEC) in peripheral blood above the upper limit of normal, 0.35–0.5 × 10⁹/L (equivalently more than ~6% of total leukocytes). It is graded by AEC:

| Grade | AEC |
|---|---|
| Mild | 0.5–1.49 × 10⁹/L |
| Moderate | 1.5–5.0 × 10⁹/L |
| Severe | >5.0 × 10⁹/L |

> [!important] A finding, not a diagnosis
> Eosinophilia names a laboratory abnormality. The clinical question is always *why*, and in most cases the answer is secondary and reactive rather than clonal. Because [[Eosinophils]] infiltrate tissue, degranulate, and release prothrombotic and inflammatory mediators, a raised count can precede or cause organ damage — but the count alone does not establish that damage has occurred.

## Hypereosinophilia versus hypereosinophilic syndrome

These terms are related but not interchangeable:

- **Hypereosinophilia (HE)** is the elevated count (or tissue infiltrate) itself.
- **Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES)** requires HE **plus** organ dysfunction attributable to the eosinophilic infiltrate, with secondary causes excluded. "Syndrome" denotes damage, not magnitude.

For certain myeloid neoplasms — chronic myeloid leukaemia and some acute myeloid leukaemia variants — HE is defined specifically as persistent blood eosinophilia ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L together with ≥10% eosinophils on the differential.

## Causes

**Secondary (reactive) eosinophilia** accounts for the large majority of cases:

- **Parasitic infection**, especially tissue-invasive helminthiasis. [[Gnathostomiasis]] and [[Trichuriasis]] both produce florid eosinophilia, frequently exceeding 20% of total leukocytes, and CSF eosinophilia in eosinophilic meningitis. Diagnosis should include *Strongyloides* IgG, stool ova and parasite examination, and travel history. Notably, absence of marked eosinophilia does *not* exclude parasitic disease.
- **Allergy and hypersensitivity** — asthma, atopic dermatitis, allergic fungal disease.
- **Drug reactions**, including antibiotics, and the DRESS syndrome (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), a type IVb hypersensitivity reaction with HLA-dependent risk.
- **Autoimmune and connective-tissue disease**, including the vasculitides.
- **Malignancy** — both haematological (lymphomas) and solid tumours, via tumour-derived cytokines.
- **Endocrinopathies** and adrenal insufficiency.

**Primary (clonal) eosinophilia** arises from a neoplastic eosinophil precursor: myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions (notably the *FIP1L1::PDGFRA* 4q12 deletion, which is [[Imatinib]]-responsive and often presents with near-total [[Eosinophilia]] and a high mast-cell burden), and the lymphocytic variant driven by clonal T cells secreting IL-5.

**Idiopathic eosinophilia** is the residual category once the above are excluded; even in HES, most cases remain unexplained.

## Clinical significance

Eosinophilic infiltration, degranulation, and mediator release cause organ injury in a characteristic order: skin, heart, lungs, and the central and peripheral nervous systems are involved in over half of HES cases. Cardiac involvement — eosinophilic myocarditis and valvular injury — carries the highest mortality and is the reason HES remains dangerous rather than merely unusual. Coagulation abnormalities, including thrombosis, accompany HES and are partly driven by eosinophil granule proteins.

Where eosinophilia is the only finding and AEC is under 1.5 × 10⁹/L, a watch-and-wait approach with close follow-up is often appropriate, since counts fluctuate.

## Management

Treatment follows the cause, not the count:

- **Parasitic infection** — anthelmintic therapy ([[Albendazole]], mebendazole, praziquantel, ivermectin as indicated). Empiric antihelminthic treatment is reasonable for unexplained eosinophilia below 1.5 × 10⁹/L once contraindications are excluded.
- **Drug reaction** — withdraw the offending agent; corticosteroids for DRESS.
- **Clonal disease** — genotype-directed therapy; imatinib for *FIP1L1::PDGFRA*.
- **Hypereosinophilic syndrome** — corticosteroids first line, with IL-5–directed biological agents (mepolizumab, benralizumab) for refractory or steroid-sparing control.

> [!warning] Steroids obscure the diagnosis
> Corticosteroid therapy suppresses eosinophil counts and impairs immunity, which both erases the diagnostic clue and can precipitate hyperinfection in parasitic disease. Treat the underlying cause, and avoid empiric steroids in unexplained eosinophilia with a plausible helminth exposure until parasitology is worked up.
