# Cyclooxygenase

**Cyclooxygenase** (COX, officially **prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase**, gene symbols **PTGS1**/PTGS2) is a family of isozymes (EC 1.14.99.1) that catalyse the first committed steps of prostanoid synthesis. Arachidonic acid, released from membrane phospholipids by [[Phospholipase A2]], is converted to the short-lived **prostaglandin G2** intermediate and then to **prostaglandin H2** — the common precursor of [[Prostaglandins]], [[Prostacyclin]], and thromboxane.

The same enzyme is a member of the animal-type heme peroxidase family, so COX carries out two chemistries in one active site: a cyclooxygenase reaction (bisdioxygenation of arachidonic acid) and a peroxidase reaction (reduction of the hydroperoxide). The duality is why COX inhibition blocks both inflammation and platelet function, and why the same drug class is simultaneously analgesic and dangerous to the stomach.

> [!info] Structure
> COX-1 and COX-2 are of similar molecular weight (~70 and ~72 kDa) with 65% sequence homology and near-identical catalytic sites. Each has three domains: an N-terminal EGF-like domain, a small four-helical membrane anchor, and a core heme-peroxidase catalytic domain. Both are homodimers. The monotopic membrane anchor pins them to the cytosolic face of the endoplasmic reticulum, which is why the arachidonic-acid source and the enzyme are spatially co-located.

## Isoforms

**COX-1 (PTGS1, chr. 9q32)** is constitutively expressed in most tissues and generates the "housekeeping" prostaglandins that protect the gastric mucosa, support renal perfusion, and maintain the endothelialprostacyclin–platelet-thromboxane balance. Its platelet thromboxane A2 output is irreversible because platelets have no nucleus to resynthesise the enzyme.

**COX-2 (PTGS2, chr. 1q25)** is the isoform discovered in 1991 as an inducible gene. It is low at baseline and strongly induced by inflammatory cytokines and growth factors; it is also constitutive in kidney, brain endothelium, and reproductive tissue. Its discovery reframed the pharmacology of analgesia, because selective inhibition could in principle spare gastric COX-1.

The single most consequential structural difference is **Ile523 in COX-1 versus Val523 in COX-2**. The smaller valine opens a hydrophobic side pocket that the bulkier isoleucine sterically blocks. The coxib class (celecoxib, etoricoxib, rofecoxib) exploits exactly this pocket, which is the structural basis of COX-2 selectivity.

> [!info] Substrate competition
> Acetaminophen (paracetamol) acts mainly through its metabolite **AM404**, which inhibits COX centrally after being formed by fatty acid amide hydrolase conjugating acetaminophen with arachidonic acid. This is why acetaminophen is analgesic and antipyretic but has minimal peripheral anti-inflammatory activity and negligible effect on platelets.

## Pharmacology

**Classical NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac) inhibit both isoforms. Loss of COX-1 removes the gastric protective prostaglandins and the platelet thromboxane; the acid pKa of many of these drugs adds direct topical erosive injury. The result is the classic pairing of analgesia and gastrointestinal ulceration.

**COX-2 selective inhibitors** spare gastric COX-1, which lowers ulcer risk. But suppressing endothelial prostacyclin without suppressing platelet thromboxane tilts the haemostatic balance toward thrombosis. A 2006 meta-analysis of 139 randomised trials (~150,000 participants) found a roughly twofold increase in myocardial infarction risk with selective COX-2 inhibitors, and a similar signal for high-dose diclofenac and ibuprofen but **not** naproxen. Rofecoxib was withdrawn in 2004 on this basis.

> [!warning] The cardiovascular risk is contested
> The 2016 PRECISION trial (24,081 participants) reported *lower* cardiovascular mortality with celecoxib than with either naproxen or ibuprofen, directly contradicting the earlier meta-analysis. The honest reading is that class-wide blanket claims are unreliable, that risk is drug- and dose-specific rather than class-wide, and that COX-2 selectivity has not delivered a uniformly safe drug.

**Aspirin** irreversibly acetylates a serine in the COX active site, permanently disabling the platelet enzyme for the platelet's ~7–10 day lifespan. This is why low-dose aspirin is antithrombotic — and the same mechanism explains the GI bleeding risk at higher doses.

**Beyond the NSAIDs:** fish oils substitute alternative fatty acids that COX routes toward resolvins rather than inflammatory prostaglandins; several flavonoids inhibit COX-2; hyperforin inhibits COX-1 with a lower IC50 than aspirin; and calcitriol inhibits COX-2 gene expression. The clinical significance of most of these remains minor next to the NSAIDs.

## Connections

- [[Arachidonic acid]] — the immediate substrate; released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2 and then committed irreversibly to either the prostanoid or the leukotriene branch.
- [[Phospholipase A2]] — controls substrate availability, and is the reason glucocorticoids suppress prostaglandin synthesis upstream of COX entirely.
- [[Prostaglandins]] — the direct downstream products; COX activity determines their rate of production, which is the mechanistic basis of NSAID analgesia and of gastric ulceration.
- [[Prostacyclin]] — the endothelial anti-aggregatory prostanoid; its suppression without corresponding thromboxane suppression is the thrombotic liability of coxibs.
- [[Aspirin]] — irreversible covalent acetylator of the COX active site; the shared mechanism behind both its cardiovascular benefit and its bleeding risk.
- [[Inflammation]] — the pro-inflammatory context in which COX-2 is induced and in which NSAID inhibition produces the therapeutic effect.
- [[Myocardial infarction]] — the outcome that shaped regulatory policy for the entire COX-2 selective class.
