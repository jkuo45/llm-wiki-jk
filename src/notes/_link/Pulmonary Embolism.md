# Pulmonary Embolism

**Pulmonary embolism** is the obstruction of one or more pulmonary arteries by thrombus,
most commonly originating in the deep veins of the leg or pelvis. It is the most
common cause of preventable in-hospital death, and the reason it is dangerous is
biological rather than anatomical: the embolus itself is often small, and the lethal
event is the abrupt rise in pulmonary vascular resistance that a modest obstruction
produces in a patient with limited right ventricular reserve.

## Pathophysiology

Three things happen, in sequence:

1. **Ventilation-perfusion mismatch.** Embolised vessels are ventilated but not
   perfused, creating dead space; the remaining lung is over-perfused, and blood
   diverted to unoccluded vessels causes V/Q mismatch there. The net result is
   hypoxaemia, a widened alveolar-arterial gradient, and hypocapnia from hyperventilation.
2. **Neurohumoral mediators.** Activated platelets in the thrombus release serotonin,
   thromboxane, adenosine and thrombin, which drive pulmonary vasoconstriction —
   counterbalanced by nitric oxide and prostacyclin. The vasoconstrictor contribution
   is well established (the Rise and Fall of the Rise of Lung Serotonin: platelet
   serotonin was shown to drive pulmonary hypertension, and 5-HT₂A blockade reduces
   the serotonin-induced rise in pulmonary vascular resistance in canine
   experiments), though its precise weight in human PE is harder to pin down.
3. **Right ventricular strain.** The thin-walled right ventricle cannot generate more
   than ~40 mmHg systolic pressure acutely. Acute pressure overload causes RV
   dilatation, rising wall stress, tricuspid annular dilatation, septal flattening
   into the left ventricle, reduced LV preload, and falling cardiac output — the
   pathway to obstructive shock.

> [!info] Clinical caveat
> The corollary is that prognosis is determined by RV reserve, not by clot burden. A
> patient with normal RV function and a saddle embolus may be stable, while a patient
> with an embolus in a single segment and a failing RV may be in shock. This is why
> imaging-based clot load and clinical severity deliberately dissociate in the
> risk-stratification schemes.

## Clinical presentation

Presentation is highly variable and the classic triad of dyspnoea, pleuritic chest
pain and haemoptysis appears in a minority. Sudden unexplained dyspnoea, syncope,
tachycardia, hypoxaemia, and a clear chest radiograph is the more useful pattern.
[[Cardiac arrest]] is a recognised presenting mode, particularly in the
normotensive-but-decompensating patient and in malignancy.

## Diagnosis

The diagnostic algorithm is structured around **pretest probability** rather than
starting with imaging:

- **Low pretest probability.** The **PERC** rule (all of: age <50, HR <100, SpO₂ ≥95%,
  no unilateral leg swelling, no haemoptysis, no history of VTE, no recent surgery or
  trauma, no exogenous oestrogen, PE not the most likely diagnosis) has a sensitivity
  above 97% for PE in a low-risk population, and *no testing at all* — not even
  D-dimer — is required. PERC is **not** validated in pregnancy, malignancy, morbid
  obesity, or chronic hypoxaemia.
- **Low or intermediate probability.** A high-sensitivity [[D-dimer]] rules out. The
  age-adjusted threshold (age in years × 10 µg/L for >50) performs better than a fixed
  500 µg/L, as shown in the 2019 PEGEd trial, which found a 0.3% three-month VTE rate
  in patients below threshold.
- **High probability, or positive D-dimer.** **CT pulmonary angiography** is the
  diagnostic standard. Nuclear ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scanning is
  preferred in pregnancy and where contrast or radiation must be avoided; SPECT/CT
  allows binary read-out and is well validated. Compression ultrasonography of the legs
  supports treatment even when chest imaging is negative or unavailable, since a
  proximal DVT is a VTE.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> CTPA is heavily over-utilised, and the diagnostic yield falls when it is ordered
> for patients whose pretest probability is low — where the false-positive rate
  exceeds the true-positive rate. Elevated D-dimer is also nonspecific in the
  conditions PE patients are most often to have (postoperative state, malignancy,
  sepsis, pregnancy, ageing), which is precisely why D-dimer only has a role in the
  low-probability pathway.

## Risk stratification and treatment

The **2026 AHA/ACC multi-society guideline** introduces five clinical categories (A–E)
with subcategories, replacing the earlier high/intermediate/low and
massive/submassive/non-massive terminology:

- **Anticoagulation is the default and the foundation.** Unfractionated heparin for
  patients who may need thrombolysis or procedural intervention because it is rapidly
  reversible; LMWH or fondaparinux; or a direct oral anticoagulant. The 2026 guideline
  recommends a DOAC over a vitamin K antagonist when oral anticoagulation is needed in
  the low-risk categories and for extended treatment.
- **Reperfusion for the unstable.** Systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed
  thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, or surgical embolectomy, guided by a
  Pulmonary Embolism Response Team (PERT) for the intermediate and high-risk
  categories.
- **Lower-dose thrombolysis** is a considered option where bleeding risk is
  acceptable, to reduce major bleeding and intracranial haemorrhage.
- **Systemic thrombolysis is not recommended in preference to anticoagulation alone**
  in the low-risk categories, where the bleeding risk exceeds the benefit.
- **Vasopressor and mechanical support.** Norepinephrine, and veno-arterial
  extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for refractory obstructive shock, since
  RV unloading rather than more inotropy is the therapeutic target.

For intermediate-risk patients with both RV dysfunction and positive troponin, close
monitoring is standard; routine primary reperfusion has not improved outcomes in that
group, and the 2026 guideline rates the evidence for systemic thrombolysis there as
uncertain.

## Complications

- **Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension** (CTEPH) — persistent
  pulmonary hypertension from organised non-resolving thrombus, in roughly 2–4% of
  PE survivors. Defined as mean PA pressure ≥25 mmHg with a wedge pressure <15 mmHg.
  The only definitive treatment is pulmonary thromboendarterectomy, which is
  potentially curative; the guideline separates CTEPH and residual pulmonary vascular
  obstruction as distinct follow-up categories.
- **Post-PE impairment** — persistent exercise intolerance, dyspnoea and reduced
  exercise capacity, present in a large minority even with normal resting haemodynamics.

## Connections

- [[Thromboembolism]] — PE is the arterial endpoint of venous thromboembolism, and
  essentially always begins as a DVT; the same [[Anticoagulant]] classes prevent both.

  class in which the vault's [[Warfarin]] and [[Heparin]] notes sit.
- [[Platelet aggregation]] — the observation that platelet-derived serotonin drives
  pulmonary vasoconstriction in PE makes platelet biology mechanistically relevant
  upstream of the obstruction, and thrombin generated at the clot is the most potent
  platelet activator.
- [[D-dimer]] — the rule-out test that gates the whole diagnostic pathway, and the

- [[Cardiac arrest]] — PE is one of the few potentially reversible causes of arrest, and
  one of the few that can be diagnosed at the point of resuscitation.
- [[Thromboembolism]] — the underlying mechanism; the vault's thrombotic disease notes give
  the platelet and coagulation arms that generate the embolus.
- [[Platelet]] — the serotonin and thrombin released by activated platelets contribute
  to the vasoconstriction that makes a given obstruction haemodynamically severe.
- [[Pulmonary Hypertension]] — acute RV pressure overload is the acute presentation and
  CTEPH the chronic sequel; both sit within the same vascular bed.
- [[Antithrombin III]] — the heparin cofactor; its consumption in severe thromboembolism
  and its hereditary deficiency are both clinically relevant to PE.
- [[Inflammation]] — thrombo-inflammatory coupling means PE is not a purely mechanical
  event, and inflammatory markers contribute to prognostication.
