# Gene

A **gene** is a stretch of genomic DNA whose information is transcribed into a functional RNA — either a protein-coding [[Messenger RNA|mRNA]] or a non-coding RNA that functions in its own right. It is simultaneously the basic unit of inheritance and the physical unit of heredity: variants in genes are what is passed from parents to offspring, and genes are what most [[Mutations]] and variants of clinical interest fall in.

> [!info] Definition
> A gene is a sequence of DNA that is transcribed into an RNA molecule which is then either translated into protein (mRNA) or performs a structural, catalytic or regulatory function as RNA itself (rRNA, tRNA, microRNA, lncRNA).

## Structure

- **Regulatory region** — promoter and enhancer elements that determine *when* and *how much* a gene is transcribed, together with insulators and CpG-island methylation states.
- **Transcribed region** — exons (retained in mature RNA) separated by introns (spliced out), transcribed into a pre-mRNA.
- **Terminator** — the 3' boundary, where cleavage and polyadenylation signals end the transcript.

Human genes are discontinuous and often span tens to hundreds of kilobases, encoding only a small fraction of their own length in exons. Genes may also be nested, overlapping in either strand, or read in alternative frames.

## Information Flow and Regulation

A gene's information flows DNA → RNA → protein via [[Transcription]] and [[Translation]], and output is set at several separable steps:

- **Transcriptional control** — [[Transcription Factor]]s and [[Chromatin]]/[[Epigenetics|epigenetic]] state set expression level; this is where most age- and disease-relevant regulation happens (see [[Gene Expression]], [[Gene Silencing]], [[Epigenetic Aging]]).
- **Post-transcriptional control** — [[Alternative Splicing]] lets one gene encode multiple protein isoforms; RNA stability, miRNA and siRNA decay ([[RNA Interference]]) tune output further.
- **Post-translational control** — protein abundance and activity are mostly regulated after translation.

> [!important] One gene is not one protein
> Alternative splicing, alternative start and polyadenylation sites, and post-translational modification all break the naive one-gene-one-protein equation. This is why the vault treats gene symbols (e.g. *PIK3CA*) as upstream of, but distinct from, the protein and pathway notes.

## Categories and Nomenclature

Human genes are given approved symbols by the HGNC (e.g. *LMNA*, *PEX6*, *TP53*); locus-specific names follow conventions such as *TP53* for tumour protein 53. Genes are either protein-coding (~19–20,000) or non-coding RNA genes, the latter including rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, snoRNA, microRNA, lncRNA and the increasingly large set of uncharacterised transcripts.

## Clinical Relevance

Genes are the substrate of genotyping and pharmacogenomics, of gene-targeted therapy, and of the entire hereditary-disease framework. Categories of disease-causing variant:

- **Loss-of-function** — haploinsufficiency and recessive null alleles (e.g. PEX nulls in Zellweger spectrum disease).
- **Gain-of-function** — activating [[Oncogene]] lesions such as [[PIK3CA]] mutations; such variants often cluster in specific residues (catalytic, degradation motifs) or in protein domains.
- **Dominant-negative** — mutant protein interferes with the wild-type product, as in *LMNA*/[[Lamin A]] disorders.
- **Repeat expansion** — e.g. Huntington's disease.

In the vault's ageing context, gene activity is modulated rather than mutated by [[Epigenetic Aging]]: [[DNA Methylation]] clocks such as the [[Horvath Clock]] and [[DunedinPACE]] read gene-position-specific methylation to estimate biological age.

## Connections

- [[Gene Expression]] — The process by which a gene's information becomes a functional product; this note defines the substrate of that process.
- [[Epigenetics]] — Regulates which genes are transcribed without changing the [[DNA]] sequence, making it the primary lever on gene output in ageing and disease.
- [[Oncogene]] — An activated [[Gene]] whose gain-of-function promotes [[Cancer]]; oncogenes and [[Tumor Suppressor Gene|tumour suppressor genes]] are the two gene classes that dominate cancer genomics.
- [[Tumor Suppressor Gene]] — The counterpart to oncogenes: loss of function removes a brake on proliferation, as in [[p53]] or [[BRCA1]] pathway lesions.
- [[DNA Methylation]] — The best-characterised epigenetic mark; its pattern across a genome is read out as epigenetic clocks.
- [[Alternative Splicing]] — Enables one gene to produce multiple isoforms, decoupling gene count from proteome complexity.
- [[Mutations]] — Sequence changes in genes are the primary mechanism of inherited and somatic disease.
- [[Non-coding RNA]] — Most of the transcriptome, and by transcript count most of the genome's output, is non-coding; genes are not only protein recipes.
