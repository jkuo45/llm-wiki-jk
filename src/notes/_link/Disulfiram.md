# Disulfiram

**Disulfiram** (brand name Antabuse) is an FDA-approved drug originally used as an alcohol-aversion therapy. It was repurposed as the first-in-class **[[SARM1]] inhibitor**.

## Mechanism

Disulfiram covalently modifies a cysteine residue in the [[SARM1]] TIR domain, irreversibly blocking its [[NAD+]] glycohydrolase (NADase) activity. By preventing SARM1-mediated NAD⁺ depletion, it protects axons from Wallerian degeneration in preclinical injury models.

## Therapeutic Context

- Repurposed from its established alcohol-aversion indication due to the conserved TIR-domain cysteine targeted by disulfiram.
- **Limitation:** Disulfiram is pharmacologically promiscuous (it inhibits multiple cysteine-dependent enzymes, including aldehyde dehydrogenase), which restricts its utility as a selective SARM1 inhibitor and drives the development of more selective next-generation molecules ([[DSRM-3716]], [[GSK-428]], [[NB-4746]]).
- Shares the **adduct-forming uncompetitive inhibitor** mechanism class with the [[CD38]] inhibitor 78c — a conserved NAD-dependent strategy across NAD hydrolases (Bratkowski et al. 2022, _Neuron_).

## Connections

- [[SARM1]] — covalent TIR-domain inhibitor; blocks NADase, prevents axonal NAD⁺ catastrophe
- [[CD38]] — parallel NAD⁺ glycohydrolase; shares adduct-forming inhibitor logic (78c)
- [[NAD+]] — substrate whose hydrolysis is blocked
