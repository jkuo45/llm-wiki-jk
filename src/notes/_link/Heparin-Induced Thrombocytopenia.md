# Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia

**Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)** is an iatrogenic, immune-mediated, profoundly prothrombotic disorder triggered by exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH) or, less commonly, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH). It is the most frequent drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia. The name is misleading in one respect: although the platelet count falls, the dominant clinical risk is **thrombosis, not bleeding**. One-third to one-half of cases are complicated by thrombosis — venous, arterial or microvascular — with mortality attributable to HIT of roughly 5–10% and limb amputation in 1–3%.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

- **The antigen.** Heparin binds the tetrameric platelet protein **platelet factor 4 (PF4/CXCL4)** released from platelet α-granules, forming multimolecular, highly negatively charged PF4-heparin complexes. PF4 alone is weakly immunogenic; complex formation with heparin is what breaks tolerance. Immunogenicity scales with heparin chain length and degree of sulphation, which is the standard explanation for why UFH is more immunogenic than LMWH and why fondaparinux (a synthetic pentasaccharide, too short to form tetramers) carries essentially no risk.
- **The antibody.** IgG — and sometimes IgA or IgM — forms against PF4/heparin. Prior bacterial infection, and the biophysical properties of circulating PF4/heparin complexes, are both implicated in why only a minority of exposed patients seroconvert.
- **Effector phase.** Immune complexes cross-link **FcγRIIa** on platelets, triggering activation, aggregation and release of more PF4 — a positive-feedback loop. Monocyte FcγRIIa engagement induces tissue factor expression; neutrophils contribute NETosis and neutrophil–platelet–histone-DNA thrombi, and depletion of neutrophils abolishes thrombus formation in mouse and microfluidic models. Endothelial PF4/glycosaminoglycan complexes are also targeted. The net result is a hypercoagulable state with thrombin generation and procoagulant platelet microparticles.
- **Two types.** **Type I** is a non-immune, benign, self-limiting fall in platelet count in the first days of therapy. **Type II** is the immune, delayed, prothrombotic entity that this note describes and that causes essentially all serious HIT.
- **Five phases** (per ASH): suspected → acute HIT (until platelet recovery) → subacute HIT A (platelet recovered, functional assay still positive) → subacute HIT B (functional assay negative, immunoassay positive) → remote HIT (antibody undetectable by immunoassay).

> [!info] Timing is diagnostic
> Naive patients develop thrombocytopenia at **5–14 days** after heparin starts, because that is the time needed to raise new IgG. A fall **≤1 day** after exposure indicates preformed antibodies persisting from heparin within the past 100 days (median onset 10.5 hours). "Delayed-onset HIT" presents days to weeks *after* discontinuation. Recovery of platelet counts occurs within 7 days in ~90% of cases after heparin is stopped; functional assays turn negative at a median of 50 days and immunoassay at a median of 85 days.

## Clinical Presentation & Diagnosis

- **4Ts score** — magnitude of **T**hrombocytopenia, **T**iming of fall, presence of **T**hrombosis, absence of o**T**her explanation; 0–8 points, scored ≤3 low, 4–5 intermediate, 6–8 high. A low score has a negative predictive value of **0.998 (95% CI 0.970–1.000)** and should end the workup. Positive predictive value is poor (0.14 for intermediate, 0.64 for high), so a positive 4Ts mandates testing rather than diagnosis. The HEP score is an alternative that performs comparably and may do better in trainees and ICU patients.
- **Immunoassays** (ELISA, particle gel, latex, rapid assays) detect anti-PF4/heparin antibody: sensitivity ~95%, specificity ~82%; IgG-specific ELISAs are more specific than polyspecific. Optical density adds information, and a high 4Ts with strongly positive ELISA (OD > 2.0) approaches certainty.
- **Functional assays** (serotonin release assay, HIPA, P-selectin expression assay, PF4-enhanced variants) detect heparin-dependent platelet activation: specific but resource-intensive, usually send-out. PF4-enhanced assays detect some SRA-negative HIT.

> [!warning] Two common pitfalls
> 1. **Bleeding is not the presentation.** Clinically significant bleeding at presentation is uncommon; do not anchor on bleeding as the expected feature.
> 2. **Low 4Ts in post-cardiopulmonary-bypass or ECMO patients may not exclude.** PPV fell to 0.56 and 0.25 respectively in these populations, and inter-observer variability in 4Ts scoring is substantial — consider joint scoring with an experienced haematologist.

## Therapeutic Landscape

- **Acute HIT:** stop all heparin including flushes, and start a non-heparin anticoagulant at therapeutic intensity. Accepted agents: **argatroban**, **bivalirudin**, **danaparoid**, **fondaparinux**, or a direct oral anticoagulant (rivaroxaban or apixaban preferred orally once the platelet count has normalised).
- **Never start a vitamin K antagonist early.** Warfarin before platelet recovery is contraindicated — strong recommendation against initiation prior to platelets ≥150 × 10⁹/L — because of limb-venous-gangrene risk from a transient hypercoagulable state on warfarin with heparin withdrawn.
- **Duration:** at least 4 weeks or until platelet recovery for isolated HIT; **3–6 months** if HIT is complicated by thrombosis. Routine platelet transfusion is not recommended.
- **Refractory disease:** IVIG (1 g/kg/day × 2 days) is suggested for severe/refractory thrombocytopenia or thrombosis, on the theory that it competitively blocks FcγRIIa engagement; plasma exchange reduces antibody burden in refractory cases, though this is not a categorised ASFA indication.
- **Pregnancy:** LMWH is preferred.
- **Prevention:** limiting UFH use system-wide reduces incidence; 12-lipoxygenase inhibition is under trial as an agent that could block PF4-dependent platelet activation before diagnosis.

> [!info] Related but distinct entities
> **Autoimmune HIT / spontaneous HIT / VITT-like** disease occurs without prior heparin exposure; PF4 autoantibodies are heparin-independent and often IgG subclass-restricted, and IVIG or plasma exchange is more often needed. Type I HIT, by contrast, is not immune-mediated at all.

## Connections
- [[Heparin]] — the trigger; UFH is the most immunogenic form because long, highly sulphated chains form the PF4 tetramers that render the complex antigenic, which is the structural basis for the UFH > LMWH > fondaparinux risk gradient.
- [[Thrombocytopenia]] — the presenting haematological finding and the trigger for scoring; the count falls because immune-complex-coated platelets are cleared by the reticuloendothelial system.
- [[Platelets]] — the effector cell; FcγRIIa cross-linking by PF4/heparin IgG drives activation, aggregation, PF4 release and microparticle generation.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — the therapeutic class that must replace heparin; the whole management problem is anticoagulating a hypercoagulable, thrombocytopenic patient without heparin.
- [[Warfarin]] — the vitamin K antagonist that must be delayed until platelet recovery, since early initiation in acute HIT causes venous limb gangrene.
- [[Deep Vein Thrombosis]] — the most common thrombotic complication; HIT-associated thrombosis is venous as well as arterial and is itself a component of the 4Ts score.
- [[Pulmonary Embolism]] — a frequent thrombotic manifestation of HIT, as elsewhere in venous thromboembolism.
- [[Complement System]] — complement activation accompanies immune-complex formation and platelet activation, adding to the prothrombotic and inflammatory response.
- [[Vascular Inflammation]] — the endothelium is an active target of anti-PF4/heparin antibodies via surface PF4-glycosaminoglycan complexes, so HIT is an endothelial as well as a platelet disease.
- [[Myocardial infarction]] — arterial thrombosis in HIT can present as coronary thrombosis or graft occlusion, not merely venous clot.
