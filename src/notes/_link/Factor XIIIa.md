# Factor XIIIa

Factor XIIIa is the active transglutaminase form of coagulation factor XIII
(FXIII), the last enzyme of the coagulation cascade and the only one that
introduces covalent rather than non-covalent bonds into a forming clot. Its
substrate is the fibrin network that [[Thrombin]] has just polymerised; its
product is a clot that no longer dissolves.

## Zymogen, activation, and structure

FXIII circulates as a **zymogen**, unlike every other coagulation factor, which
circulate as active enzymes. It is a tetramer: two A chains (the catalytic
transglutaminase) and two B chains (the carrier, held by non-covalent
interactions and released at activation). The A chain contains the
transglutaminase domain, which is homologous to tissue transglutaminase 2 and
to E-cadherin - all three share the Cys-His-Asp catalytic triad.

Activation is unique in the cascade and is a **thrombin-dependent, Ca2+-dependent
proteolytic** event that can be followed by a non-proteolytic
dissociation-dependent step:

1. Thrombin cleaves the activation peptide from the B chain.
2. This exposes the N-terminal of the B chain, which binds the transglutaminase
   activation loop of the A chain and stabilises the active conformation
   non-proteolytically. This is the only non-proteolytic activation step in
   coagulation.

Because XIIIa is the only factor that does not act on soluble substrates, the
system is designed so it can only be activated **after** fibrin has been laid
down - it is chemically inert until a clot is in the right place.

> [!info] The fxIII activation paradox
> That same design produces a rare bleeding disorder. Congenital factor XIII
> deficiency (autosomal recessive) presents with delayed bleeding from the
> umbilical stump, a bleeding tendency out of proportion to any coagulation
> screen - PT, aPTT, and thrombin time are all normal - and, characteristically,
> with **spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage** in neonates and recurrent
> pregnancy loss in women. The diagnosis is made by mixing study or factor
> activity assay, not by a screening test. Acquired factor XIII autoantibodies
> cause a similar picture in adults and are associated with pregnancy and with
> multiple myeloma.

## Chemistry of the cross-link

The catalytic cysteine attacks the gamma-carboxamide group of a glutamine
residue on one fibrin chain, forming an acyl enzyme intermediate and releasing
ammonia. The enzyme-thioester then attacks the epsilon-amino group of a lysine
on a second chain, forming an isopeptide bond. Net result: covalent cross-link,
one glutamine consumed, one ammonia released.

> [!info] The two isopeptide bonds
> Fibrin has two named cross-link sites. The **a** bond links the alpha chains
> at residues 95 and 328, and is the first and dominant cross-link. The **b**
> bond links adjacent alpha chains in the D-domain at residues 366 and 539.
> The a: b ratio is a mechanobiological readout: stiffer clots have higher
> a-bond content, and higher a:b ratios associate with thrombosis. Tissue
> transglutaminase 2 has recently been shown to contribute beta-chain
> cross-linking at the clot, and these are not fully interchangeable enzymes.

## Physiological and pathological significance

- **Clot mechanics**: FXIIIa cross-linking raises clot stiffness and
  tensile strength substantially, and it is the principal determinant of clot
  mechanical stability rather than fibrin polymerisation itself.
- **Resistance to [[Fibrinolysis]]**: cross-linking introduces binding sites
  for [[Plasminogen]] and for alpha2-antiplasmin. Incorporating alpha2-antiplasmin
  into the clot is a major mechanism by which a clot locally suppresses its own
  breakdown.
- **Vessel wall**: cross-links also form in fibrin deposited on the injured
  vessel wall, and factor XIII is atheroprotective in mouse atherosclerosis
  models independently of fibrin - via binding to VLDL and altered lesion
  composition. Reductions in FXIII in [[Myocardial infarction]] patients
  predict worse outcome.
- **Wound healing and tissue repair**: FXIIIa is a key facilitator of cell
  adhesion and migration through integrin cross-linking, and of macrophage
  recruitment.
- **Angiogenesis and pregnancy**: FXIIIa binds to endothelial cell surfaces and
  is required for normal vessel formation; in the placenta, XIIIa is required
  for trophoblast fusion and fibrin deposition in the maternal-fetal interface.
- **Inflammation**: FXIIIa has proinflammatory and antifibrinolytic effects
  that are separable from its cross-linking activity, and platelet-derived
  FXIII has an intracellular platelet-retention function independent of
  degranulation.

## Clinical use and pharmacology

Factor XIII concentrates and recombinant FXIII are approved for congenital
factor XIII deficiency, and FXIII activity is being explored as a
**biomarker** in septic shock (low FXIII predicts poor outcome, a partly
unresolved finding) and in complicated [[Stroke]]. FXIII deficiency is
distinguished from [[Antithrombin III]] deficiency by the normality of routine
screening tests. There is no FXIII inhibitor in clinical use, but the
transglutaminase class is targeted in other indications and the pocket is
chemically tractable.

## Connections

- [[Fibrinogen]] — the precursor substrate: thrombin cleaves it to fibrin, and
  FXIIIa then cross-links the fibrin; congenital afibrinogenemia and factor
  XIII deficiency can be difficult to separate on clinical grounds.
- [[Thrombin]] — the enzyme that both polymerises fibrin and activates FXIII;
  the direct coupling of clot formation to clot cross-linking.
- [[Plasminogen]] — binds to the cross-linked clot surface, which is how FXIIIa
  reduces fibrinolysis; the fibrinolytic counterweight to this enzyme.
- [[Platelet]] — the major cellular reservoir of factor XIII, released during
  activation, including a non-secreted platelet-retention form.
- [[Myocardial infarction]] — clinical context in which reduced factor XIII
  activity predicts worse outcome.
- [[Stroke]] — intracerebral haemorrhage in factor XIII deficiency is the
  classic and most characteristic presentation of the inherited disorder.
- [[Anticoagulant]] and [[Heparin]] — the therapeutic contrast: factor XIII is
  the one coagulation enzyme deliberately *not* targeted by the mainstream
  anticoagulants, since its only clinical deficiency state is a bleeding
  diathesis.
- [[Inflammation]] — FXIIIa has proinflammatory actions separable from its
  transglutaminase activity, which is why it appears in the
  coagulation-inflammation interface.
- [[Integrins]] — FXIIIa cross-links integrins, which is how it affects cell
  adhesion and migration independently of fibrin.
- [[Atherosclerosis]] — factor XIII deficiency is protective in mouse
  atherosclerosis models, an observation not obviously predicted from its role
  in clot stabilisation.
