# Potassium

**Potassium** (K⁺, Z = 19) is the most abundant intracellular cation in the human
body. Total body potassium is 3,500–4,000 mmol, of which roughly 98% sits inside cells
— predominantly skeletal muscle — leaving only 55–65 mmol (about 2%) in the
extracellular compartment, where the concentration is held at 3.5–5.0 mmol/L with
remarkable tightness. That 35:1 gradient is the single determinant of the resting
membrane potential in every excitable cell, and most of the clinical significance of
potassium follows from how little slack there is in the extracellular pool.

A typical Western diet supplies about 70 mmol/day against an AI of 2,500–3,500 mg
(~64–89 mmol) for adults, so the kidney, not the diet, sets the balance.

## Distribution: two independent problems

Potassium balance has two separable components, and conflating them is the usual source
of confusion in teaching:

- **Internal distribution** (fast, minutes–hours) — controlled by [[Insulin]] and
  β-adrenergic (β2) signalling, both of which stimulate the [[Sodium Potassium ATPase]] and drive K⁺ into cells. Because the intracellular store is enormous, a
  transcellular shift of a few hundred mmol changes plasma K⁺ substantially. This is
  the lever used therapeutically: insulin-with-glucose is first-line treatment for
  acute hyperkalaemia for exactly this reason.
- **Renal excretion** (slow, hours–days) — controlled by distal tubular transport and
  by the volume status that drives aldosterone secretion.

Insulin is the acute defence: without it, a large meal could double extracellular K⁺
into the lethal range while the kidney is still hours behind.

## Renal handling

Filtered potassium is reabsorbed almost completely in the proximal tubule; the
decisive exchange happens in the connecting tubule and cortical collecting duct:

- **Principal cells** secrete K⁺ through apical ROMK, driven by the lumen-negative
  potential that apical ENaC sodium reabsorption creates, with basolateral uptake via
  the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Aldosterone (secreted from the adrenal cortex's zona glomerulosa)
  acts on all three steps: more basolateral pump, more ENaC, and greater luminal K⁺
  permeability.
- **Intercalated cells** reabsorb K⁺ via an apical H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which is
  up-regulated in depletion.

Depletion is sensed by basolateral Kir4.1 potassium channels in distal convoluted
tubule cells, which act as sensors and activate the thiazide-sensitive NaCl
cotransporter (NCC) to reclaim sodium — and with it chloride — while sparing
potassium.

> [!info] Mechanism
> Aldosterone's effect on NCC has been contested. Poulsen and colleagues found that
> blocking aldosterone-induced K⁺ loss with amiloride abolished the rise in NCC and
> phosphorylated NCC, while ENaC was still up-regulated — arguing that the NCC
> response is secondary to the hypokalaemia that aldosterone-induced ENaC activity
> produces, not a direct mineralocorticoid effect. Aldosterone clearly regulates ENaC
> directly; the directness of its action on NCC is less settled.

## Hypokalaemia

Causes fall into three buckets: loss (renal and GI), shift into cells, and poor intake.
Clinically, the commonest is **loop- and thiazide-diuretic use**. Effects include
weakness, cramps, ileus, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, and — the part that kills —
arrhythmia. At the cellular level hypokalaemia hyperpolarises the resting potential
*and* suppresses multiple K⁺ conductances, so repolarisation reserve falls even though
the cell is more polarised; it also inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, raising intracellular Na⁺
and Ca²⁺.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Hypokalaemia gives a prolonged QT and torsades de pointes; hyperkalaemia gives
> peaked T waves, PR prolongation, loss of P waves and sine-wave pre-excitation.
> Neither shows symptoms until the ECG changes, so the potassium is a lab value that
> has to be looked for rather than waited for. The commonest contributors to
> severe hyperkalaemia are reduced renal excretion plus the loss of the insulin and
> aldosterone defence — hence the usual presentation in diabetic or
> hypoaldosteronism patients, and in those on RAAS or mineralocorticoid-receptor
> blockers, where the compensatory aldosterone rise is prevented.

## Other biology

Potassium is a required cofactor for enzymes including the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase itself, and
adequate intake matters for blood pressure — the DASH and potassium-enrichment trial
data support higher dietary potassium in hypertension. Repletion matters in
[[Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease]] and heart failure, where diuretics and
disease both deplete. There is no established role for routine potassium
supplementation in the general population beyond correcting deficiency.

## Connections

- [[Sodium Potassium ATPase]] — consumes ATP to pump 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, and is the
  common endpoint on which insulin, β2-adrenergic signalling and aldosterone all act
  to shift potassium intracellularly; its inhibition in hypokalaemia is part of why
  that state is arrhythmogenic.
- [[Insulin]] — the fastest and most clinically used lever on plasma potassium, via
  Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activation in muscle and fat; the basis of insulin-plus-glucose
  treatment for hyperkalaemia and the reason diabetic patients tolerate potassium loads
  poorly.
- [[Adrenal gland]] — aldosterone secretion from the zona glomerulosa drives the whole
  distal K⁺ secretory apparatus, so hypoaldosteronism (Addisonian, or drug-induced)
  produces hyperkalaemia and hyperaldosteronism produces hypokalaemia.
- [[Calcium Signaling]] — the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is an electrogenic pump, so its activity
  contributes directly to the membrane potential that calcium channel gating and
  excitability depend on.
- [[Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease]] — a common setting for both hypokalaemia
  (loop diuretics, corticosteroids, poor intake) and acid–base disturbance, where
  acute metabolic acidosis drives potassium out of cells.
- [[Insulin Resistance]] — skeletal muscle becomes resistant to potassium uptake
  before it becomes resistant to glucose uptake, so impaired internal disposal
  precedes any rise in plasma potassium.
