# Antidotum Mithridaticum

**Overview:** [[Antidotum Mithridaticum]] (Mithridatium) was a celebrated ancient "universal antidote" attributed to King [[Mithridates VI]] of Pontus (c. 134–63 BCE). It was a complex polypharmic electuary compounded from dozens of ingredients—roots, seeds, resins, and small amounts of reputedly toxic substances (e.g., opium, viper flesh, certain metals and botanicals).

**Historical Principle — Mithridatism:** The legend holds that Mithridates ingested sub-lethal doses of poisons daily to build tolerance, a practice later termed [[Mithridatism]]. This is an early intuition of [[Hormesis]]: that low-dose exposure to a stressor can induce adaptive protective responses that increase resistance to higher doses.

**Composition and Evolution:** Recipes varied across centuries and cultures; later Galenic and Renaissance versions (e.g., "theriac") blended scores of agents in honey or wine. The rationale was both pharmacological (opiate analgesia, antimicrobial resins) and immunological (gradual desensitization). The formulations were never standardized and their efficacy was largely unverified by modern standards.

**Relevance to Modern Biology:** Mithridatism prefigures contemporary concepts of preconditioning and mitohormesis—low-level oxidative, thermal, or caloric stress that activates cytoprotective pathways (e.g., [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] antioxidant defenses, heat-shock proteins, autophagy). In this sense the Antidotum is a historical antecedent to deliberate hormetic interventions studied for [[Longevity]] and resilience.

**Caveats:** Modern toxicology does not endorse self-dosing with poisons; the historical practice is of interest primarily as an early conceptual model of dose-dependent bidirectional (hormetic) responses.
