# Amphotericin B

**Amphotericin B** is a heptaene macrolide polyene produced by *Streptomyces nodosus*. It
remains the "gold standard" agent for most systemic fungal infections, and it is the
first-line treatment for visceral [[Leishmaniasis]] — an indication often forgotten because
the drug is filed under antiparasitics as well as antifungals.

## Chemistry and mechanism

The molecule is a mycosamine-containing macrolide with seven conjugated double bonds
(the heptaene chromophore). The amphipathic structure — a rigid hydrophobic face and a
flexible mycosamine hydroxyl face — drives it into the plasma membrane, where it binds
**ergosterol**, the fungal sterol, with far higher affinity than it binds mammalian
[[Cholesterol]] (roughly a 20-fold selectivity, which is the whole basis of its
therapeutic index). The bound drug self-assembles into **ion channels / pore-like
aggregates** in the membrane, and a second mechanism — interfacial oxidation of
membrane sterols by the drug–peroxide complex — adds oxidative membrane injury. Result:
leakage of monovalent ions and small solutes, depletion of ATP, and, in fungi, cell
death.

Candidiasis and cryptococcosis are the classic indications; the drug also covers
histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, talaromycosis and mucormycosis, though
azole agents are now often preferred for several of these.

> [!info] Why the toxicity profile looks like it does
> The drug is not intrinsically lethal to the host at therapeutic concentration, but its
> high plasma protein binding, low tissue penetration into certain compartments, and slow
> renal clearance mean that accumulation produces dose-limiting toxicity:
> **infusion-related reactions** (fever, rigors, hypotension — largely an
> infusion-rate phenomenon, mitigated by slow infusion and premedication) and
> **dose-limiting nephrotoxicity** (azotemia, tubular injury, hypokalaemia and
> hypomagnesaemia from distal tubular effects, potentiated by concurrent
> nephrotoxic drugs). The nephrotoxicity is partly mitigated by lipid formulation and by
> pre/post-hydration and sodium loading, and is often reversible on discontinuation.

## Formulations

This is the drug's real pharmacologic story. Because the deoxycholate micellar
formulation is toxic and must be infused slowly, two lipid formulations were developed
and are now standard:

- **Liposomal amphotericin B** — amphotericin B encapsulated in ~80 nm
  [[Liposomes|liposomes]] of phospholipid, which sequesters the free drug and drastically
  reduces nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions. The box has a test-tube-retention
  requirement to confirm intact liposomes.
- **Amphotericin B lipid complex (ABLC)** and **amphotericin B colloidal dispersion** —
  detergent-lipid complexes and colloidal particles respectively, with the same
  toxicity advantage over deoxycholate.

All lipid formulations are markedly more expensive, and cost-effectiveness in
resource-limited settings remains an active equity issue — relevant because most
fungal disease burden is in exactly those settings.

## Non-fungal indications

- **Visceral leishmaniasis.** Amphotericin B deoxycholate remains first-line; the
  liposomal formulation is preferred and has become the backbone of treatment in
  HIV–Leishmania coinfection, where miltefosine, [[Pentamidine]] and antimonials are
  less well tolerated or less available.
- **Leishmaniasis relapse** after antimonial failure.
- Historically used for fungal infections in neutropenic patients and for suspected
  endocarditis.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> The "ampho-terrible" reputation is real and dose-related. Because deoxycholate
> amphotericin B is substantially more toxic than the lipid formulations, the practical
> question at the bedside is not whether the drug works but which formulation the
> patient can tolerate for long enough to complete a course.

## Connections

- [[Leishmaniasis]] — the antiparasitic indication: liposomal amphotericin B is now
  first-line for visceral leishmaniasis, including in HIV coinfection where
  miltefosine and antimonials underperform.
- [[Miltefosine]] — the main oral alternative for visceral leishmaniasis; miltefosine
  avoids amphotericin B's parenteral route and nephrotoxicity but has its own
  teratogenicity and GI and nephrotoxicity profile, and relapse rates are higher.
- [[Cholesterol]] — the structural basis of selectivity: the drug binds fungal
  ergosterol preferentially over mammalian cholesterol, and cholesterol depletion
  partly explains the tubulopathy.
- [[Liposomes]] — lipid encapsulation is what converted this drug from nearly unusable
  to tolerable; the pharmacologic improvement is a delivery achievement rather than a
  change in target.
- [[Pentamidine]] — an alternative agent in leishmaniasis and antifungal regimens,
  with a different toxicity profile (hypoglycaemia, hypotension, nephrotoxicity).
- [[Acute Kidney Injury]] — the clinically decisive adverse effect; monitoring of
  creatinine, potassium and magnesium is standard practice.
- [[Lymphoma]] — indirectly: prolonged amphotericin exposure, or co-administration with
  other nephrotoxic drugs, contributes to renal injury in the haematologic
  transplant setting.
