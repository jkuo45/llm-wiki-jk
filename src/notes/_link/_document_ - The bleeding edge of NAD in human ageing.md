## Standfirst

Whole-blood nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) levels do not consistently decline with age, challenging the notion that lower systemic NAD levels cause age-related diseases. However, blood NAD levels provide an incomplete view of systemic NAD metabolism, failing to capture the heterogeneity of NAD metabolism across blood and solid tissues.

## Figure 1

**Fundamental differences in NAD metabolism between red blood cells and solid tissues can explain the disparate effect of ageing on NAD levels.**

Both nicotinamide and nicotinic acid are used as precursors for the synthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD). In red blood cells, nicotinic acid is preferentially used to synthesize NAD, whereas in most cells that comprise solid tissues, nicotinamide is preferentially used. Another key distinction is that solid tissue cells, but not red blood cells, possess enzymes including CD38, PARPs, sirtuins and others that enzymatically degrade NAD, generating nicotinamide and other products. These fundamental differences between red blood cells and solid tissue cells undermine the assumption that whole-blood NAD levels reliably reflect NAD homeostasis in other tissues.
