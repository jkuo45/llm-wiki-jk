# Antiphospholipid Syndrome

**Antiphospholipid syndrome** (APS) is an autoimmune disorder in which antiphospholipid antibodies — typically against β2-glycoprotein I, prothrombin, cardiolipin, or phospholipid-binding proteins — drive recurrent arterial or venous thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity, with laboratory positivity persisting on repeat testing ≥12 weeks apart.

> [!info]
> Source: [[_document_ - Current understanding of eryptosis mechanisms, physiological functions, role in disease, pharmacological applications, and nomenclature recommendations|Tkachenko et al. 2025 eryptosis consensus review]]
> Autoantibodies from APS patients — unlike those from asymptomatic antiphospholipid-positive carriers — induce [[Eryptosis]] in healthy donor erythrocytes in vitro. Because eryptotic cells externalize [[Phosphatidylserine]], which is procoagulant and increases thrombin generation, this offers a candidate mechanism linking the antibody repertoire to the prothrombotic state and to the anemia seen in APS.

## Clinical framing

- Thrombotic APS requires antibodies plus a qualifying clinical event; obstetric APS has its own criteria. "Triple-positive" (lupus anticoagulant, anti-β2-glycoprotein I, anticardiolipin) carries the highest risk.
- The hypercoagulable state is paradoxical against the thrombocytopenia common in APS — the usual explanation (antibody-mediated platelet activation and complement) does not account for red-cell data, which is why erythrocyte death phenotypes are of interest here.
- Management is anticoagulation (usually warfarin or heparin) plus low-dose aspirin in selected patients; immunosuppression only for a defined indication.
