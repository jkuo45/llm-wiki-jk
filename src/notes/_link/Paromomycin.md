# Paromomycin

**Paromomycin** is an aminoglycoside derived from *Streptomyces griseolus* and chemically related to the aminoglycoside neomycin. It is administered as the sulfate salt. Its defining pharmacological property is that it is **essentially not absorbed from the gut**, which confines most of its use to luminal protozoal infection while leaving room for the separate, systemic indication in visceral leishmaniasis.

## Mechanism of Action

Like other aminoglycosides, paromomycin binds the 16S rRNA decoding centre of the **30S ribosomal subunit** and blocks the initiation complex, causing misreading of mRNA. Aminoglycoside uptake into bacteria depends on an oxygen-dependent transport system, which is why these drugs are ineffective against anaerobes and why activity falls in low-oxygen tissues.

> [!info] Distinct leishmanicidal mechanism
> In *Leishmania* the dominant mechanism is different from the antibacterial one. Atomic-resolution ribosome structures (Yonath lab, 2017) show paromomycin occupying a site on the small subunit that blocks the translocation step and additionally induces **mistranslation of specific mRNAs**, including those encoding glycosylation machinery. The resulting defects in post-translational glycosylation of surface proteins (particularly the mannose-containing glycoconjugates that anchor amastigotes in the macrophage) are more lethal to the parasite than the general inhibition of protein synthesis itself. This is unusual for an aminoglycoside and explains why the drug works against an organism whose ribosome is not its primary ancestral target.

## Clinical Uses

- **Intestinal protozoa** — the dominant indication. Paromomycin is used for intestinal amebiasis (with a lumen-active amoebicide such as diloxanide furoate, since it does not reach tissue amoebae), and for giardiasis, cryptosporidiosis, and dientamoebiasis. Oral dosing, three times daily, in an unabsorbed form.
- **Visceral leishmaniasis** — historically the standard parenteral treatment, particularly where miltefosine and liposomal amphotericin are unavailable. In combination with liposomal amphotericin B, paromomycin shortens the duration of the intensive phase. It has been largely displaced in high-resource settings but remains first-line in several endemic programmes.
- **Topical** — investigated for cutaneous leishmaniasis and for mycetoma.

> [!warning] Resistance and withdrawal risk
> The single most important practical property of paromomycin is the **absence of a useful oral systemic option for prolonged courses**. Extended paromomycin is used for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, and long courses carry a real risk of irreversible vestibular ototoxicity. This has driven the search for better delivery (liposomal, nano-formulated, inhaled liposomal) rather than new scaffolds.

## Pharmacokinetics and Toxicity

Oral bioavailability is under 1%, and absorbed drug is largely renally excreted unchanged. Toxicity is the class-standard aminoglycoside spectrum:

> [!warning] Ototoxicity
> Cochlear and vestibular damage from aminoglycosides is **commonly irreversible**, and paromomycin is among the drugs on WHO's list of essential medicines with no reliable antidote. Risk rises with cumulative dose and renal impairment — the latter matters because the drug is eliminated by the kidney, so an AKI patient accumulates drug. There is no accepted ototoxicity-protective co-therapy, and therapeutic drug monitoring is not routine in the endemic settings where the drug is most used.

Other class risks: nephrotoxicity (largely reversible), neuromuscular blockade (theoretical risk with concurrent anaesthetics or neuromuscular blockers, well described in case reports), and rare allergic reactions. Aminoglycosides are inactivated by some penicillins administered concurrently, another reason the combination is historically avoided.

## Connections

- [[Leishmaniasis]] — the main systemic indication. Paromomycin is one of the very few drugs active against intracellular amastigotes in macrophages, because it penetrates eukaryotic cells far better than most of its class.
- [[Leishmania]] — the parasite for which paromomycin's glycosylation-disrupting mechanism, rather than its antibacterial one, is the operative mechanism.
- [[Cholesterol]] — paromomycin is a known inhibitor of intestinal NPC1L1-independent cholesterol absorption and has been used experimentally to lower cholesterol; it is an aminoglycoside antibiotic whose mechanism against eukaryotes is not the one it was selected for.
- [[Multidrug Resistance]] — paromomycin is one of the agents used to construct aminoglycoside-resistant laboratory lines, and it is a substrate in aminoglycoside resistance gene studies.
- [[Ivermectin]] — both are antiparasitics with very limited absorption and a clinically meaningful risk of CNS toxicity, though by different mechanisms: ivermectin's is P-glycoprotein-dependent exclusion from the brain, paromomycin's is intrinsic cochleotoxicity from systemic exposure.
