# Fibrin

**Fibrin** is the insoluble, fibrous protein that forms the scaffolding of a blood clot. It is generated when [[Thrombin]] cleaves two short peptides (fibrinopeptides A and B) from its soluble circulating precursor [[Fibrinogen]], exposing a polymerisation site that lets fibrin monomers self-assemble into half-staggered protofibrils, fibres and ultimately a branching gel. What makes fibrin a clot is not the monomer — it is the network, and that network is made mechanically stable by transglutamination.

> [!info] Fibrin is both a structural protein and a matrix
> Fibrinogen is one of the classic carriers of the [[RGD peptide|RGD]] integrin-binding motif (via its Aα chain, alongside [[Fibronectin]] and [[Vitronectin]]), and the fibrin gel is a provisional [[Extracellular Matrix]]. This dual identity — clot scaffold on one hand, adhesive substrate on the other — is why fibrin shows up in apparently unrelated contexts: haemostasis, wound healing, and tumour stroma.

## Formation and Structure

1. **Thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptide A**, exposing the α-chain N-terminal polymerization site. This initiates fibrinogen→fibrin conversion.
2. **Thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptide B** more slowly, and this second cleavage exposes the γ-chain C-terminal site, releasing the "knob" that binds the complementary "hole" and drives protofibril elongation and branching.
3. **Protofibrils** assemble end-to-end and side-to-side into fibres, which branch into a three-dimensional network spanning the polymerising solution.

Fibrin fibres display a characteristic naturally occurring **polymorphs** — a double twist and a half-staggered arrangement — and the fibre diameter, branching density and network pore size depend on fibrinogen and thrombin concentration, ionic strength and temperature.

> [!warning] Fibre structure, not just amount, determines clot strength
> Thicker, fewer fibres with a denser network tend to form at lower fibrinogen concentrations and are more resistant to fibrinolysis; at high fibrinogen the network becomes finer and more permeable. Clot viscoelasticity — and therefore mechanical strength — depends on this architecture, not only on total fibrin mass. This is what makes viscoelastic assays such as TEG/ROTEM informative about a patient's ability to form a functional clot.

## Cross-Linking by Factor XIIIa

Activated factor XIIIa, a plasma transglutaminase, forms γ-γ and α-α isopeptide bonds between adjacent fibrin monomers.

> [!important] Cross-linking changes structure, not just toughness
> FXIIIa cross-linking produces **thinner, denser fibres**, increases fibre mechanical stiffness, raises red cell and platelet retention within the clot, and slows fibrinolysis. Importantly it acts on fibrin's *formation* dynamics, not only on an already-formed clot — the Val34Leu FXIII polymorphism, which alters the rate of thrombin activation of FXIII, demonstrably shifts the resulting clot architecture. Direct FXIIIa cross-linking of clot structure reduces fibrinolysis independently of other plasma proteins (Walton et al., J Thromb Haemost 2015).

α2-antiplasmin cross-links into the fibrin network late in clot formation and is the principal mechanism by which a clot becomes resistant to degradation, giving it a functional life measured in hours to days.

## Clearance

Fibrin is removed by the fibrinolytic system: [[Plasminogen]] is activated (principally by [[Tissue Plasminogen Activator]] and urokinase) to [[Plasmin]], which cleaves fibrin into fibrin degradation products, including the cross-linked fragments measured as [[D-dimer]].

## Physiology and Pathology

- **Haemostasis** — a stable fibrin seal is essential to arrest bleeding at the site of vessel injury.
- **Wound healing** — the fibrin matrix is a provisional ECM that supports cell migration, growth factor binding and angiogenesis, and is progressively replaced by collagen during remodelling.
- **Thrombosis** — pathological fibrin deposition underlies [[Deep Vein Thrombosis]], pulmonary embolism and [[Arterial Thrombosis]]. The fact that **fibrin is polymerised, not soluble fibrinogen, that carries most of the prothrombotic activity** is why anticoagulation targets thrombin generation and fibrin polymerisation rather than fibrinogen itself.
- **Tumour stroma** — fibrin in and around a tumour supplies an adhesive matrix for endothelial cells, fibroblasts and tumour cells, supporting angiogenesis and invasion.
- **Erythrocyte contribution** — red blood cells are a large fraction of clot cellular content (up to 40% of thrombin generation in some pathological settings), and they alter viscosity and fibrin network structure; PS exposure on erythrocytes shortens clotting times and increases fibrin formation.

## Connections

- [[Fibrinogen]] — the direct precursor; thrombin cleavage of fibrinogen is what creates fibrin, and the two differ by the loss of two small peptides.
- [[Thrombin]] — the enzyme that converts fibrinogen to fibrin and that polymerised fibrin then binds and protects; the thrombin–fibrin loop is the core self-reinforcing element of clot formation.
- [[Factor XIIIa]] — the transglutaminase that cross-links fibrin into a mechanically stable, fibrinolysis-resistant clot.
- [[Fibrinolysis]] — the pathway whose substrate fibrin is; the balance between deposition and clearance is the practical determinant of whether a clot resolves or propagates.
- [[Plasmin]] — the protease that degrades fibrin and whose activity is the functional readout of the fibrinolytic system.
- [[D-dimer]] — the cross-linked fibrin degradation product whose plasma concentration reports that fibrin was formed *and* cross-linked *and* subsequently lysed, which is what makes it a specific marker of thromboembolism.
- [[Extracellular Matrix]] — fibrin functions as the provisional matrix during wound healing and as an adhesive substrate for tumour stroma, not merely as a clot component.
- [[Platelet]] — platelets adhere to the newly formed fibrin mesh and are entrapped within it; the fibrin mesh also carries the [[RGD peptide]] motif that makes it adhesive.
