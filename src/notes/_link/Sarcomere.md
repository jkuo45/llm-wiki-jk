# Sarcomere

The **sarcomere** (Greek *sarx* "flesh", *meros* "part") is the smallest functional unit of striated muscle and the repeating segment of a [[Myofibril]] bounded by two adjacent **Z-discs** (Z-lines, from German *zwischen*, "between"). Sarcomeres in series along a myofibril are what produce the striated banding pattern first described by van Leeuwenhoek. Smooth muscle has no sarcomeres.

> [!info] Architecture
> Two filament systems interdigitate inside each sarcomere. **Thin filaments** are two intertwined strands of [[Actin]] wound around [[Tropomyosin]] with [[Troponin]] complexes distributed at intervals. **Thick filaments** are bipolar assemblies of [[Myosin]] II, cross-linked at their bare center. Titin and nebulin act as molecular rulers setting filament length, and the Z-disc and M-band crosslink the two systems. Z-discs anchor actin plus titin through α-actinin; the M-band crosslinks myosin and titin through myomesin and myosin-binding protein C, and hosts creatine kinase.

## Bands and their meaning

- **Z-disc** — dark line; the sarcomere boundary and the anchor for actin thin filaments.
- **I-band** ("isotropic") — the region of thin filament not overlapped by thick filament; it narrows during contraction.
- **A-band** ("anisotropic") — spans the full length of the thick filament, contains both filament types, and is **constant in length** (about 1.85 µm in mammalian skeletal muscle) regardless of contraction state.
- **H-zone** ("heller", brighter) — the central region of thick filament with no actin overlap; it narrows and can disappear during contraction.
- **M-line** ("mittel") — the midline, the myosin crosslinking zone.

The diagnostic feature of contraction is therefore: **A-band length constant, I-band and H-zone shorten, Z-discs move closer together.** Filaments do not shorten; they slide.

## Excitation–contraction coupling

Tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites on actin at rest. Depolarization travels down [[T-tubule|T-tubules]] to the DHP/L-type calcium channel, which is mechanically coupled to the [[Ryanodine Receptor|RYR2]] on the [[Sarcoplasmic Reticulum|SR]], triggering calcium-induced calcium release. Calcium binds [[Troponin]] (specifically the TnC subunit), which shifts tropomyosin and exposes the cross-bridge sites. Myosin heads, cocked by ATP hydrolysis, bind actin, release ADP and Pi, and execute the power stroke; re-binding of ATP detaches the head. Relaxation is achieved by SERCA pumping calcium back into the SR. The M-band creatine kinase local system regenerates ATP from phosphocreatine right where the ATP-consuming heads are.

The biophysical basis of the sliding-filament mechanism is that myosin is an **ATP-dependent molecular motor** whose conformational cycle converts chemical energy into directed mechanical work. A sarcomere's force output therefore depends on actin–myosin overlap: too little overlap and few cross-bridges form, too much and the thin filaments collide. The resulting length–tension relation peaks at an optimal sarcomere length.

## Sarcomere length and its adaptive significance

Sarcomere length determine force-velocity tradeoff: longer sarcomeres have more cross-bridges in parallel and generate more force, but a shorter range of shortening. Vertebrates hold sarcomere length in a remarkably narrow band — essentially the same optimum across all muscles of an individual and between species — which is why vertebrate muscle performance is stereotyped. Arthropods, in contrast, show more than seven-fold variation in sarcomere length between species and even between muscles within one individual, matching their much wider diversity of locomotor demands. The reason vertebrates constrain sarcomere length is not fully settled.

## Pathology

Because the sarcomere is a load-bearing structure with no capacity to remodel continuously, sarcomeric proteins are a major locus of inherited disease. The [[Dystrophin]]–glycoprotein complex links the sarcomere to the costamere and sarcolemma, and its failure produces [[Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy]]. Sarcomere disarray and myocyte hypertrophy are the defining morphology of hypertrophic [[Cardiomyopathy]], and sarcomere loss through ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy-driven turnover is a common endpoint in [[Muscle Atrophy]]. Titin, as the largest protein in the body and the principal passive elastic element, is also being examined as a candidate sensor whose conformational state may feed back on calcium handling.

  - The cardiac sarcomere is shorter (~1.6 µm) and more compliant than the skeletal sarcomere, with titin splice isoforms supplying much of the difference; this is the mechanical basis of the Frank-Starling mechanism that sets cardiac output.

## Connections

- [[Cardiomyocytes]] — cardiomyocytes are built from sarcomeres in parallel, and the passive tension of the titin-actin-myosin network during diastole is what the Frank-Starling relationship measures. Sarcomere length is therefore a readout of cardiac filling, not only a structural detail.
- [[Myosin]] — myosin II is the thick filament and the motor protein; its ATPase cycle is the direct energy source for the power stroke.
- [[Actin]] — actin is the thin filament; its interaction with myosin in the A-band is the molecular event that generates force.
- [[Titin]] — titin spans from the Z-disc to the M-band and is both the principal passive elastic element and a molecular ruler for thick filament length; the huge titin knockout mouse is embryonic lethal, which reflects how much of sarcomeric integrity titin carries.
- [[Troponin]] — the calcium sensor of the sarcomere; TnC binding shifts tropomyosin to expose actin binding sites, which is the only route by which calcium becomes mechanical work.
- [[Ryanodine Receptor]] — RYR2 on the sarcoplasmic reticulum is the release channel through which stored calcium becomes cytosolic calcium, making it the link between membrane depolarization and cross-bridge cycling.
- [[Sarcoplasmic Reticulum]] — the specialized ER that stores and releases the calcium driving contraction and sequesters it again for relaxation.
- [[Dystrophin]] — the cytoskeletal anchor that transmits sarcomeric force across the membrane to the extracellular matrix; its loss disconnects the sarcomere and produces the dystrophinopathies.
- [[Muscle Atrophy]] — atrophy is characterized by loss of sarcomere content through selective degradation of sarcomeric proteins, which is why the sarcomere is a target in every atrophy intervention.
- [[Autophagy]] — the autophagic–lysosomal and ubiquitin–proteasome systems are the two clearance routes for sarcomeric proteins during atrophy and disuse, making sarcomere protein turnover a quantitative readout of autophagic flux in muscle.
