# Dalteparin

**Dalteparin** is a low-molecular-weight ([[Heparin|LMWH]]) fragment of unfractionated heparin, marketed as Fragmin, given by subcutaneous injection once or twice daily. It is one of three LMWHs in common use (with [[Enoxaparin]] and, outside the US, tinzaparin; nadroparin and bemiparin are used elsewhere). It is a pharmaceutical preparation, not a single defined molecule — LMWHs are polydisperse chains, and dalteparin's specific activity is defined by its anti-factor Xa potency (~70 IU/mg, with an anti-Xa to antithrombin activity ratio >1.5) rather than by an exact structure.

> [!info] Mechanism
> Dalteparin acts by potentiating [[Antithrombin III]]. Antithrombin is a stoichiometric, irreversible inhibitor of the activated forms of factor Xa and thrombin, and is a poor inhibitor on its own. Heparin binds antithrombin and induces a conformational change that accelerates thrombin inhibition roughly 1000-fold; LMWH chains, being shorter, bind antithrombin but are too short to bridge antithrombin to thrombin efficiently, so their dominant effect is on factor Xa. The net result is a preferential, predictable inhibition of factor Xa — hence monitoring by anti-Xa rather than by the [[INR]].

The clinical advantage of the LMWH class is pharmacokinetic, not pharmacodynamic. LMWHs are less extensively distributed into adipose tissue and are less rapidly cleared by the reticuloendothelial system than unfractionated heparin, giving higher subcutaneous bioavailability, a longer plasma half-life (roughly 3–5 hours for dalteparin anti-Xa activity, dose-dependent), and a longer and more predictable time above the therapeutic anti-Xa range. The ratio of anti-Xa to anti-IIa activity is >1.5, so the drug is "Xa-selective."

## Pharmacokinetics

Dalteparin is dosed in international units (IU) of anti-Xa activity, subcutaneously, with dosing based on body weight rather than anti-Xa monitoring in most indications. Elimination is renal, so dose reduction is required in renal impairment — a real difference from the situation with e.g. [[Warfarin]]. InhaLATE trial-derived dosing (H1/H2, 200 IU/kg) is used in VTE treatment, with a monthly dosing schedule for extended outpatient phase 2 management. A paediatric population pharmacokinetic analysis (2020) proposed weight-band regimens: 150 IU/kg q12h (1 month to <2 y), 125 IU/kg q12h (≥2 to <8 y) and 100 IU/kg q12h (≥8 to <19 y).

> [!warning] The "no monitoring" simplification is wrong in practice
> Routine anti-Xa monitoring is not required for dalteparin in most patients, but it *is* indicated in extremes of body weight (obesity, underweight), renal impairment, pregnancy, and for prolonged treatment. The therapeutic window is broad, but dalteparin accumulates with renal failure and the consequences of accumulation are bleeding, not a lab abnormality. Anti-Xa peak and trough targets of roughly 0.6–1.2 IU/mL and 0.2–0.4 IU/mL respectively are cited in the LMWH literature, though specific targets are institution- and indication-dependent.

## Clinical use

Approved and guideline-supported indications include:

- **VTE prophylaxis** in patients undergoing hip replacement surgery, abdominal surgery, or admitted with reduced mobility.
- **Treatment of VTE** in adults and, in the US, in paediatric patients aged one month and older, and in cancer-associated VTE.
- **Extended VTE treatment in cancer.** The CLOT trial (Lee et al. 2003) showed dalteparin superior to warfarin for recurrence reduction in patients with malignancy and acute VTE, and this established LMWH as standard of care for the first 3–6 months in cancer-associated thrombosis. The mechanism is plausible — malignancy patients have a persistently high recurrence risk that a fixed-intensity oral anticoagulant does not adequately suppress.
- **Palliative and hospice care**, where subcutaneous once-daily dosing and absence of routine monitoring are decisive practical advantages. Palliative drug references describe 200 IU/kg once daily for the first month, then 150 IU/kg daily for at least 3–6 months.

It does **not** cross the placenta and is therefore generally the anticoagulant of choice in pregnancy, where [[Warfarin]] is teratogenic.

## Adverse effects and limitations

- **Bleeding** is the principal toxicity, including a rare but serious intracranial and retroperitoneal haemorrhage.
- **Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)** occurs with LMWH at a lower but non-zero rate than with unfractionated heparin. Patients with documented HIT must receive an alternative: [[Fondaparinux]], or a direct oral anticoagulant.
- **Thrombocytopenia and the rare complication of spinal/epidural haematoma** after neuraxial procedures.
- **Osteopenia** with long-term use, an underappreciated cost in oncology patients who often have months to years of treatment.
- Dalteparin was not approved for acute coronary syndrome, where enoxaparin and fondaparinux are the LMWH choices.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> LMWH superiority over [[Warfarin]] is specific to *cancer-associated* VTE. In unselected VTE, direct oral anticoagulants are at least as effective and simpler to administer. The CLOT result should not be generalised beyond the population in which it was tested. Note also that no randomised trial has established the optimal duration of extended anticoagulation in cancer; 3–6 months is a guideline convention, not a demonstrated optimum.

## Connections

- [[Heparin]] — dalteparin inherits its mechanism (antithrombin potentiation) and its class toxicities (bleeding, HIT) from the parent compound, while its shorter chain length is what converts those into a longer half-life and a once-daily, non-monitored regimen.
- [[Antithrombin III]] — the stoichiometric sink that heparin and all its fragments accelerate. This is the single mechanistic point that separates LMWHs from direct thrombin inhibitors and from [[Warfarin]], which acts by reducing vitamin K-dependent synthesis of the clotting factors themselves.
- [[Warfarin]] — the historical standard of care, and the comparator in the trial (CLOT) that made dalteparin first-line in cancer-associated VTE. Warfarin's slow onset, variable INR, and failure to suppress cancer-associated recurrence are what dalteparin is preferred over.
- [[D-dimer]] — the biomarker that makes dalteparin's course manageable: D-dimer fall is the guide for whether continued anticoagulation is warranted in cancer-associated VTE.
- [[Thrombin]] — downstream of the factor Xa dalteparin predominantly inhibits. Because the LMWH chain is too short to efficiently bridge antithrombin to thrombin, dalteparin is an anti-Xa drug with only residual anti-thrombin effect — this is why "LMWH" is a misnomer for the class as a whole.
- [[Enoxaparin]] — the other main LMWH, better studied in acute coronary syndrome and with a longer record in STEMI; otherwise pharmacologically similar to dalteparin.
- [[Cancer]] — the population in which dalteparin is genuinely preferred over oral anticoagulation, and the reason its extended-dosing schedule exists.
- [[Pregnancy]] — the population in which dalteparin is preferred over [[Warfarin]], because it does not cross the placenta.
