# The Hallmarks of Protein and Amino Acid Restriction in Aging and Longevity

**Knopf & Lamming (2026)** — Cell Press Blue (open access, CC BY-NC-ND)

> [!info] Central Thesis
> Dietary [[Protein Restriction|protein restriction (PR)]] recapitulates many of the [[Caloric Restriction|caloric restriction]] benefits for [[Healthspan|healthspan]] and [[Longevity|lifespan]]. Restriction of specific [[Amino Acid|amino acids]] — particularly [[Methionine]], [[Isoleucine]], and [[Valine]] — recapitulates the benefits of PR, defining six hallmarks of PR: improved metabolic health, induction of [[Nutrient Sensing|nutrient-sensing]] pathways ([[GCN2]] activation and [[mTORC1]] inhibition), decreased [[Cellular Senescence|senescence]], improved [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] function, altered [[Epigenome|epigenome]], and promotion of healthy aging.

## Summary

Despite widespread recommendations for higher [[Protein|protein]] intake during aging, increasing evidence suggests that dietary protein restriction (PR) promotes metabolic health, [[Healthspan]], and [[Longevity|lifespan]] across diverse organisms. Mechanistic studies demonstrate that restriction of specific [[Amino Acid|amino acids]], particularly [[Methionine]], [[Isoleucine]], and [[Valine]], recapitulates many of the benefits of PR, highlighting the specific amino acid composition of the diet as a key determinant of aging. This review defines the hallmarks of PR through the lens of aging, examining how PR and restriction of specific essential amino acids impact [[Metabolic Health|metabolic health]], [[Nutrient Sensing|nutrient sensing]], [[Cellular Senescence|senescence]], [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial function]], and the [[Epigenome]]. It also discusses the known and unknown roles of [[Non-Essential Amino Acids|non-essential amino acids]] in healthy aging and highlights the therapeutic potential of PR-based interventions.

## Introduction

Since McCay's 1935 report that calorie restriction (CR) extends rat lifespan, CR has proven robust across organisms from yeast to non-human primates. However, CR is generally considered too difficult to sustain, raising the question of whether the healthspan and lifespan effects of CR can be achieved without reducing overall energy intake.

In rodents, the ratio of dietary macronutrients profoundly impacts lifespan, with low-protein, high-carbohydrate diets extending lifespan and improving metabolic health. A low-protein diet, also referred to as protein restriction (PR), is a robust geroprotective regimen that lowers total dietary protein intake while still meeting nutritional needs. Like [[Caloric Restriction|CR]], PR improves healthspan and increases lifespan of yeast, flies, and rodents.

> [!warning] Dietary Guidelines Conflict
> While human dietary recommendations encourage increasing protein intake (RDA 0.8 g/kg; 1.0–1.6 g/kg advised for older adults to prevent [[Sarcopenia]] and [[Frailty]]), accumulating evidence challenges the idea that higher protein intake is beneficial. High-protein diets are associated with increased risk of diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular mortality, and even sarcopenia in some cohorts.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) support the metabolic benefits of PR in humans: low-protein diets reduce body weight, fat mass, and fasting blood glucose despite increased caloric intake; improve [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and increase [[Energy Expenditure]]; and reduce inflammation, insulin, hemoglobin A1C, and kidney disease outcomes in diabetic patients.

## Hallmarks of Dietary Protein Restriction

PR extends lifespan of rodents (summarized in Table 1). The six hallmarks are defined below through an aging-focused lens.

### Hallmark 1: Metabolic Health

Aging is characterized by decreased energy expenditure, increased body weight, and fat accumulation. In young and aged rodents, PR reduces [[Adiposity|adiposity]], increases energy expenditure, and improves [[Glucose Homeostasis|glucose homeostasis]] — effects that occur despite increased food intake and are most pronounced in males but also present in females. PR remains effective when initiated late in life (18–20 months of age).

> [!important] FGF21 Is the Primary Mediator
> The metabolic benefits of PR are largely mediated by [[FGF21]], a hormone induced by nutritional stress. PR robustly elevates FGF21 in mice, rats, and humans. [[FGF21]] is required for many metabolic effects of PR — increased food intake, reduced body weight, increased energy expenditure, and extended lifespan. Other geroprotective diets (CR, [[Methionine Restriction]], [[Branched-Chain Amino Acids|BCAA]] restriction, [[Isoleucine]] restriction) also induce FGF21.

FGF21 promotes energy expenditure in part through [[UCP1]]-dependent thermogenic beiging of white adipose tissue (WAT), with PR stimulating WAT beiging via FGF21 action in the brain through sympathetic signaling. PR similarly increases [[Thermogenesis]] in [[Brown Adipose Tissue|brown adipose tissue (BAT)]] via an FGF21-dependent mechanism. The increased energy expenditure drives increased food intake with PR by shifting brain signaling toward protein appetite.

FGF21 also regulates lipid homeostasis (reducing circulating [[Triglycerides]]), mediates PR effects on [[Cellular Senescence]] and [[Inflammation]] in WAT, and reduces hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in obese models. Mice overexpressing *Fgf21* show ~30% (males) and ~40% (females) lifespan extension; *Fgf21*-null mice do not live longer on PR, identifying FGF21 as essential for PR's longevity benefits.

### Hallmark 2: Nutrient-Sensing Pathways

Amino acid sensing is physiologically important because scarcity promotes catabolic pathways and induces [[Autophagy]]. The coordinated response to PR requires activation of [[GCN2]] and inhibition of [[mTORC1]].

#### GCN2

[[GCN2]] is an evolutionarily conserved stress-responsive serine/threonine kinase activated by amino acid restriction through uncharged [[tRNA|tRNAs]], as well as by ribosome stalling and colliding on RNA. Activated GCN2 phosphorylates [[eIF2α|eIF2α]], suppressing global protein synthesis while selectively promoting translation of [[ATF4]], a key activator of stress response and autophagy genes.

- *Drosophila* lacking *Gcn2* die when amino acids are restricted; deletion of *Gcn2* in yeast blocks dietary restriction lifespan extension
- Overexpression of *Gcn2* in yeast and *Drosophila* extends lifespan
- During PR, GCN2 is initially required for [[FGF21]] induction; GCN2 also activates hepatic [[Gluconeogenesis]] in response to decreased amino acid availability

#### mTOR

The master nutrient sensor [[mTOR]] forms two complexes: [[mTORC1]] and [[mTORC2]], which impact aging differently. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of [[mTORC1]] extends lifespan in many species; inhibition of [[mTORC2]] decreases lifespan in *Drosophila*, *C. elegans*, and mice. Normal aging leads to constitutive mTORC1 activation in many tissues, and without inhibition of mTORC1 the benefits of PR are lost. PR inhibits mTORC1 in liver, heart, muscle, adipose tissue, and brain.

mTORC1 integrates nutrient availability and hormonal cues. Leucine signals to mTORC1 in specific neurons by binding to and activating a specific voltage-gated calcium channel (Cav3.1).

> [!info] PR Couples Autophagy Activation to Translation Suppression
> PR promotes [[Autophagy]] while inhibiting protein synthesis. Upon mTORC1 inhibition, [[ULK1]] initiates autophagosome formation; mice lacking hepatic autophagy activation have blunted responses to PR. mTORC1 inhibition suppresses translation by reducing phosphorylation of [[S6K1]] (whose genetic ablation extends lifespan) and of [[4E-BP1]], blocking [[eIF4E]] from initiating translation.

### Hallmark 3: Senescence

[[Cellular Senescence]] is an irreversible cell-cycle arrest triggered by cellular stressors; senescent cells develop a [[SASP|senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)]] characterized by pro-inflammatory pathways that promote tissue dysfunction and accelerate aging. Clearance of senescent cells can ameliorate age-related disease.

- High-protein diets increase hepatic senescence and [[SASP]]; PR reduces hepatic senescence
- High-protein diets increase inflammation and senescence in WAT; PR reduces both through an [[FGF21]]-dependent mechanism
- High-protein diets promote senescent cell accumulation in the kidney; PR reduces this burden
- FGF21 suppresses senescence in cultured cells, reducing drug-induced senescence and proinflammatory SASP markers

### Hallmark 4: Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Mitochondria generate [[Adenosine Triphosphate|ATP]] while serving as central regulators of metabolism. Mitochondria become less efficient with age, with reduced recycling, DNA damage accumulation, and impaired [[Electron Transport Chain|electron transport chain]] activity. Mitochondria are the primary source of [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species (ROS)]], which damage proteins, lipids, and DNA and drive senescence. Mitochondrial dysfunction is linked to many age-associated diseases, including [[Insulin Resistance]] and [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus|type 2 diabetes]].

- PR reduces hepatic ROS and increases mitochondrial activity
- High-protein diets reduce [[Electron Transport Chain|electron transport chain]] activity in skeletal muscle and impair exercise endurance, possibly via [[mTORC1]]-mediated suppression of [[AMPK]]
- High-protein diets decrease hepatic ATP synthesis
- Effects are not uniform across studies; some report no change or improvements with high protein, highlighting remaining knowledge gaps

### Hallmark 5: Epigenetic Modification

Epigenetic modifications, such as [[DNA Methylation]] and post-translational modifications of histone tails, allow organisms to adjust gene expression without altering DNA. Aging is accompanied by erosion of the epigenetic landscape, promoting the hallmarks of aging including cellular senescence. Histone methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation change with age.

- The Dutch Hunger Winter (1944/1945) caused transgenerational epigenetic effects on health
- CR blunts age-associated changes in [[DNA Methylation]]
- PR in pregnant rodents leads to loci-specific changes in [[Histone Methylation]] and gene expression; offspring effects are mixed
- Dietary levels of specific amino acids regulate histone post-translational modifications, linking nutrient sensing to the epigenome

### Hallmark 6: Healthy Aging

PR extends both lifespan and healthspan across species. A key factor in healthspan is [[Frailty]], a state of vulnerability increasing risk of dependency, falls, hospitalization, and mortality.

> [!warning] Protein Intake and Frailty: Conflicting Data
> Higher protein intake is associated with reduced frailty risk in some observational studies, but a study of British twins showed eating more protein is associated with increased risk of [[Sarcopenia]], while a low-protein Mediterranean diet was associated with increased muscle mass and strength. Protein source may matter (plant but not animal protein associated with reduced frailty risk). PR blunts age-associated increases in frailty, and exercise can largely offset lean mass loss on low-protein diets.

- The long lifespan of Okinawans may be related to their ~9% protein diet, strikingly similar to PR laboratory studies
- PR in *Drosophila* increases resilience (stress resistance) during aging, improving responses to starvation, bacterial infection, and heat stress

## Distinct Roles for Every Amino Acid?

Restriction of the essential amino acids (EAAs) recapitulates the benefits of PR on lifespan in both *Drosophila* and mice, while restriction of non-EAAs (NEAAs) has no benefit for lifespan. Among EAAs, methionine and the BCAAs — leucine, isoleucine, and valine — are likely the primary drivers.

### Essential Amino Acids

#### Methionine

[[Methionine Restriction]] extends lifespan of many species and, when initiated early or late in life, reduces body weight, increases lean mass, improves mitochondrial function, lipid metabolism, and frailty, and extends lifespan of rodents. The metabolic response is partially dependent on [[FGF21]] and also inhibits [[mTORC1]].

Methionine is the precursor of [[S-adenosylmethionine|S-adenosyl-L-methionine]], the universal [[Methyl Donor|methyl donor]]. Dietary methionine deprivation may impact physiology through changes in [[Histone Methylation]] due to decreased availability of methyl donors; cells can preserve heterochromatic stability through upregulation of [[H3K9me3|H3K9 mono-methylation]] in the liver.

In humans, sulfur amino acid restriction increased FGF21, reduced leptin, and reduced body weight; strict vegan diets reduce blood methionine and improve glucose homeostasis.

#### Branched-Chain Amino Acids (BCAAs)

High circulating [[Branched-Chain Amino Acids|BCAA]] levels are associated with [[Obesity]], [[Insulin Resistance]], and mortality in humans. High-BCAA diets promote obesity, insulin resistance, inflammation, and decreased lifespan in rodents. Conversely, dietary restriction of BCAAs promotes metabolic health, reduces frailty, and extends lifespan, recapitulating many benefits of PR. BCAA restriction also improves [[Alzheimer's Disease|Alzheimer's disease]] pathology and cognition.

BCAA restriction reduces [[mTORC1]] signaling, induces *Fgf21*, and reduces hepatic cellular senescence. Human studies show BCAA restriction improves insulin sensitivity, glucose/insulin homeostasis, and increases circulating FGF21.

> [!tip] Individual BCAAs Differ
> While BCAAs are often considered as a group, each individual BCAA plays distinct roles in aging — leucine, isoleucine, and valine are discussed separately below.

##### Leucine

[[Leucine]] is a potent [[mTORC1]] activator that stimulates skeletal muscle anabolism. In humans, leucine supplementation increases muscle protein synthesis but does not improve muscle function. Results on fat mass are mixed (leucine restriction increases body weight/adiposity in mice in some studies, reduces fat mass in others). Leucine's effects on cardiovascular health are divergent: some studies show supplementation accelerates atherosclerotic plaque progression in an mTORC1-dependent manner and its metabolite isovaleric acid accelerates [[Atrial Fibrillation|atrial fibrillation]], while others report improved vascular function. Leucine supplementation does not extend mouse lifespan, suggesting changes in leucine are unlikely responsible for PR benefits.

##### Isoleucine

[[Isoleucine]] has recently emerged as a key regulator of metabolism and aging. Dietary isoleucine correlates with body mass index in humans; increased circulating isoleucine is associated with increased mortality risk. In Western diet-fed mice, increasing dietary isoleucine promotes weight gain, fat accretion, glucose intolerance, and insulin resistance.

In contrast, [[Isoleucine]] restriction consistently improves metabolic health across dietary contexts and age groups, extends lifespan of both *Drosophila* and mice, reduces frailty and increases healthspan, rejuvenates aged mice at the molecular level, induces substantial epigenetic changes, and improves [[Alzheimer's Disease]] outcomes in male mice. Isoleucine is an exceptionally powerful modifier of the hallmarks of PR.

##### Valine

[[Valine]] metabolism involves conversion to succinyl-CoA, entering the [[TCA cycle]] to produce ATP. Restricting valine alone reduces body weight, improves metabolic health, reduces hepatic senescence, and extends lifespan of male mice. In female mice, valine restriction is protective in Alzheimer's disease progression. Dysregulated valine catabolism (via 3-hydroxyisobutyrate) contributes to insulin resistance in diabetic rats.

Human plasma analysis found reduced circulating valine is associated with aging and moderate cognitive impairment, but valine supplementation worsens glucose tolerance and induces gut inflammation and hepatic lipid deposition. The role of valine in healthy aging may vary between species.

#### Other Essential Amino Acids

- [[Tryptophan]] restriction extends rodent lifespan (1978 report; not reproduced in the 2000s)
- [[Histidine]] restriction blunts weight gain in Western diet-fed mice without extending lifespan
- [[Threonine]] restriction improves metabolic health of mice through direct [[FGF21]] induction
- [[Phenylalanine]] administration induces [[Insulin Resistance]] and contributes to type 2 diabetes progression
- [[Lysine]] restriction ameliorates obesity in mice

### Non-Essential Amino Acids (NEAAs)

There is currently little evidence that restriction of any single NEAA broadly recapitulates PR, since NEAAs can be synthesized as needed. However, emerging evidence suggests dietary NEAAs can impact specific aspects of aging.

#### Alanine

[[Alanine]] is a key metabolic amino acid that serves as a preferred substrate for [[Gluconeogenesis]] by the liver and stimulates [[Insulin Secretion|insulin secretion]] in pancreatic β cells. Alanine supplementation blunts high-protein-induced weight gain in mice and activates [[AMPK]] in mouse liver. In yeast, deletion of the alanine transaminase (ALT) gene leads to alanine accumulation and decreased chronological lifespan. Low ALT levels are associated with increased risk of frailty and mortality in humans; alanine declines with age.

#### Arginine

[[Arginine]] is a conditionally essential amino acid in mammals. It plays key roles in detoxification through the urea cycle, generation of [[Nitric Oxide]] for cardiovascular function, and immune regulation, and is metabolized into nitric oxide, urea, citrulline, creatine, agmatine, and polyamines including [[Spermidine]]. Arginine regulates [[mTORC1]] activity; arginine depletion reduces mTORC1 activity, similar to PR.

- *Drosophila* fed extra arginine have increased body weight, decreased lifespan, and increased oxidative stress
- Long-term arginine supplementation of human endothelial cells increases senescence, partially via increased mTORC1 activity
- Obese Zucker diabetic fatty rats supplemented with arginine show reduced weight gain, improved glucose tolerance
- Arginine deprivation of human cells reduces senescence, making it a potential aging target
- Increasing arginine catabolism is used as a cancer therapy (some cancer cells lack arginine synthesis enzymes)

#### Asparagine and Aspartate

Cellular [[Asparagine]] is metabolized into aspartate. These two amino acids serve as building blocks for glucose and protein synthesis and directly activate [[AMPK]] and [[mTORC1]]. Dietary asparagine restriction extends yeast chronological lifespan; *C. elegans* supplemented with 1–5 mM asparagine show lifespan extension but shortened lifespan at 10 mM; *Drosophila* females show lifespan extension.

#### Cysteine

[[Cysteine]] is a sulfur-containing amino acid that can be directly metabolized from methionine. Most dietary [[Methionine Restriction]] studies require cysteine depletion, or the benefits on metabolic health are lost. Cysteine accumulation may promote aging through mitochondrial decline. Dietary cysteine deficiency improves metabolic health through increased energy expenditure, activating thermogenesis and beiging in rodents; cysteine supplementation at <5 mM extends *C. elegans* lifespan 8–16%. Human studies link high cysteine levels to obesity, cardiovascular diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases; dietary cysteine restriction in humans increased FGF21 and reduced body weight.

#### Glutamic Acid / Glutamate

[[Glutamate]] is the most abundant neurotransmitter in the brain and has a critical role in learning and memory. In normal aging, glutamate levels increase in the brain, driving development of neurodegenerative disorders. Literature has conflicting results: supplementation and restriction both extend yeast chronological lifespan; *C. elegans* shows lifespan extension at 1–5 mM but shortened lifespan at 10 mM (dose-dependent). The exceptionally long-lived naked mole rat has higher circulating glutamate levels compared to mice.

#### Glutamine

[[Glutamine]] has an integral role in energy metabolism, entering the [[TCA cycle]] through α-ketoglutarate. Long-term glutamine deprivation in *Drosophila* increases senescence and decreases lifespan; glutamine supplementation ameliorates the senescent phenotype. Senescent cells require extracellular glutamine to exert the [[SASP]], and [[Glutaminase]] inhibition induces senescent cell death. Human trials of glutamine supplementation show limited, mixed effects on body weight.

#### Glycine

[[Glycine]] plays an important role in metabolism, serving as a substrate for [[Glutathione]], a key antioxidant scavenger. Glutathione levels are reduced in individuals with obesity, exacerbating adipose tissue pro-inflammatory phenotype.

> [!tip] Glycine Supplementation Extends Lifespan
> The Interventions Testing Program reported glycine supplementation from 9 months of age extended lifespan of male and female mice by 6.2% and 3.7%, respectively. Glycine also extends *C. elegans* lifespan. Supplementation with collagen (glycine-rich) improves healthspan and lifespan in *C. elegans*, *Drosophila*, and rodents. Glycine supplementation is associated with increased [[Glucagon-like peptide-1|GLP-1]] production; higher plasma glycine is associated with improved [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Glycine supplementation does not impact body weight in humans.

#### Proline

[[Proline]] serves as the major nitrogen donor for synthesizing [[Polyamine|polyamines]]. Impairment in the proline catabolic pathway leads to shortened lifespan through impaired mitochondrial function. Proline levels in plasma, muscle, and skin are reduced in aged organisms, and supplementation extends lifespan of yeast and *C. elegans*. Proline levels are reduced in senescent cells, and supplementation decreases senescence of mesenchymal stem cells in culture. Proline may promote lifespan through its ability to scavenge ROS.

#### Serine

[[Serine]] contributes to diverse metabolic processes, with serine intermediates playing roles in [[Glycolysis]], methionine cycling, and lipid synthesis. Rodent models of obesity and insulin resistance have reduced tissue serine; normal aging lowers circulating serine. Mice fed a serine-free diet have increased hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammation, and oxidative damage. Serine supplementation extends lifespan in yeast and *C. elegans*, improves glycemic control and reduces hepatic lipid accumulation in rodents, suggesting decreased serine is unlikely to drive PR benefits.

#### Tyrosine

[[Tyrosine]] restriction negatively impacts lifespan and [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Serum tyrosine is associated with obesity, diabetes, liver dysfunction, and increased all-cause mortality. Tyrosine levels increase with age. Dietary depletion of tyrosine in female *Drosophila* significantly extended lifespan, most likely through increased activation of the [[GCN2]]/[[ATF4]] pathway. In young individuals, short-term tyrosine supplementation enhances memory, but not in older individuals.

## Discussion

Over the past two decades, research has identified a critical role for dietary protein in the regulation of lifespan, with PR extending lifespan in *Drosophila* and rodents. The discovery that restricting specific amino acids can recapitulate the benefits of PR has transformed understanding of how diet regulates aging. There is a shared conserved response to aging-associated phenotypes between [[Caloric Restriction|CR]], [[Protein Restriction|PR]], and amino acid restriction.

The six hallmarks of PR — improved metabolic health, induction of nutrient-sensing pathways, decreased senescence, improved mitochondrial function, altered epigenome, and promotion of healthy aging — are highly interconnected. Some variation exists based on biological sex or animal strain. Understanding which hallmarks are most important in orchestrating the PR response could identify new therapeutic targets.

> [!warning] Caveats for Human Translation
> Some individuals have a greater demand for protein (pregnant women, growing children, calorie-restricted individuals, those recovering from injuries). Many elderly individuals are protein-deficient due to decreased appetite, financial constraints, and social isolation. Those who exercise may have higher protein needs. Most NEAA studies were done in lower-order organisms (yeast, *C. elegans*), highlighting the need for more mammalian research.

## Limitations

There remain many unknowns about the optimal levels of protein intake. While many individuals likely eat substantially more than the RDA and could benefit from protein or amino acid restriction, some have greater protein demand (pregnant women, growing children, calorie-restricted individuals, injury recovery). Many elderly individuals are protein-deficient, and restriction could move them into a protein- or amino-acid-insufficient state. Those who exercise may have higher protein needs. Designing suitable diets requires identifying methods and metrics to assess effects on health, frailty, and aging, and understanding how personal factors impact dietary needs.
