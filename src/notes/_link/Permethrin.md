# Permethrin

**Permethrin** is a **type I synthetic pyrethroid**, a synthetic analogue of the natural pyrethrins extracted from *Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium*. It is one of the most widely used insecticides in the world — agricultural, veterinary, and household — and one of the most widely used topical pediculicides and scabicides. As a medicine it is available as a **5% cream** (scabies), a **1% liquid/rinse** (head lice), and a **1% shampoo** (head lice).

## Mechanism of Action

> [!info] Mechanism
> Type I pyrethroids **delay closure of voltage-gated sodium channels** — they hold the channel in the open state far longer than normal, producing repetitive firing followed by depolarisation block and paralysis. Insects are far more sensitive than mammals because they have far fewer voltage-gated sodium channels per unit membrane area and a lower resting membrane threshold for channel opening. Some type I pyrethroids additionally enhance GABA-gated chloride channel conductance; this GABAergic component is much more prominent in type II pyrethroids and in mammals than in insects.

Because the drug acts on the arthropod nervous system and not on a microbial target, there is **no microbial resistance mechanism** in the bacterial sense. The resistance that does exist is target-site and metabolic.

## Resistance

Resistance in *Pediculus humanus capitis* and *Sarcoptes scabiei* is well documented and regionally variable, driven by:

- **kdr mutations** in the voltage-gated sodium channel gene (the pyrethroid target, VGSC) — the same target site affected by *kdr* in *Anopheles* mosquitoes
- **Metabolic detoxification** by cytochrome P450s and esterases
- In agriculture, metabolic resistance through P450 overexpression is the dominant mechanism worldwide

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Permethrin resistance in head lice is a real, not theoretical, problem. The American Academy of Pediatrics has shifted recommendations away from permethrin 1% as universal first-line for pediculosis toward newer agents where available (spinosad, malathion, benzyl alcohol, or the combing-intensive physical methods), while retaining permethrin as a reasonable low-cost option in combination with wet combing. Resistance rates in *S. scabiei* are reported but not as well quantified. In the UK and Australia, permethrin 5% cream remains the recommended first-line scabicide.

## Clinical Use

- **Scabies** — 5% cream applied to the entire body from the neck down (or from the hairline down in infants and young children, including the scalp and face), left for 8–14 hours, and repeated at 7 days. The second application at 7 days is essential because eggs that survive the first treatment hatch into new mites after the cream is gone.
- **Pediculosis capitis (head lice)** — 1% liquid or rinse, or 1% shampoo, applied to dry or damp hair, left 10 minutes, rinsed. Off-label but widely used for body and pubic lice.
- **Second-line for topical dermatophyte infection** — permethrin 3% cream is prescribed for scabies *and*, because of its broader acaricidal and fungistatic activity, sometimes for tinea. This use is off-label.

> [!warning] Practical pitfall
> Permethrin cream is **applied cold**; the vehicle is a petrolatum base that liquefies with heat. Applying it immediately after a hot bath — the intuitive move, since it feels as though you should treat after the mites come out in the warm — substantially reduces delivered dose. Standard advice is to wait until the skin is cool and dry.

## Toxicity

Topical permethrin has a favourable safety record because the applied dose is small, the skin barrier limits absorption, and the molecule is rapidly hydrolysed. Reported effects are usually local and transient: mild burning, stinging, itching, erythema, and rash. Serious systemic toxicity from proper topical use is rare.

> [!warning] Where the risk actually lies
> Accidental ingestion, or application to large areas of broken skin, can cause neurotoxicity — nausea, vomiting, dizziness, tremor, ataxia, salivation, seizures, and (rarely) coma. The serious poisonings in the literature are overwhelmingly from **deliberate ingestion or agricultural/occupational exposure**, not from treating an infant with scabies cream. The exception worth knowing is permethrin's activity as a weak estrogenic endocrine disruptor, which is a long-standing regulatory concern for agricultural exposure rather than for treated scabies.

## Connections

- [[Pediculosis]] — permethrin 1% is the classic first-line pediculicide, but its position has weakened in regions with documented kdr resistance. Its effectiveness is substantially improved by pairing it with systematic wet combing, which removes hatched lice and nits mechanically rather than chemically.
- [[Scabies]] — permethrin 5% cream is guideline first-line for scabies, applied twice (day 0 and day 7) to cover eggs that hatch after the first course. Failure to treat contacts, clothing, and bedding reinfests the patient and is a more common cause of "treatment failure" than drug resistance.
- [[Sarcoptes scabiei]] — the mite whose nervous system is the drug's target; both scabies and permethrin act through the same mechanism on different organisms, which is why the same compound serves as an acaricide and an insecticide.
- [[GABA]] — pyrethroid potentiation of GABA-gated chloride channels is the second action, more prominent in type II pyrethroids and in mammals than insects. It is a useful mechanistic parallel to the macrocyclic lactone anthelmintics.
- [[Doxycycline]] — both are front-line agents in the two major ectoparasitic conditions of the vault's clinical practice, and both are cheap and widely available; the parallel is one of role rather than mechanism.
- [[Ivermectin]] — ivermectin is the systemic alternative for crusted scabies and the off-label oral option when topical treatment fails or cannot cover the body. The pair covers complementary routes to the same disease.
