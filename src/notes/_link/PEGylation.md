# PEGylation

**PEGylation** is the covalent coupling of one or more chains of polyethylene glycol to a biologic — typically a protein, peptide or lipid — to change its physicochemical behaviour in vivo. It is the most widely used half-life-extension strategy in the clinic, used in peginterferon, pegfilgrastim, pegaptanib, pegaspargase, cerdulatinib and, more recently, in PEGylated lipid nanoparticles for nucleic-acid drugs.

## Overview

A polyethylene glycol chain is a synthetic, biocompatible, largely inert polymer of repeating ethylene oxide units. Attaching it to a drug accomplishes several things at once:

- **Half-life extension.** PEG increases hydrodynamic radius, reduces renal filtration, and reduces clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. The effect is roughly proportional to total PEG mass attached, so higher PEGylation typically means longer exposure.
- **Reduced immunogenicity.** PEG masks protein epitopes, reducing antibody recognition and complement activation against the unmodified protein — a major advance for enzymes such as asparaginase and interferons.
- **Improved solubility and stability.** PEG increases hydrophilicity, reduces aggregation, shields the protein from proteolysis and from rapid hepatic uptake, and can reduce immunogenicity of the modified drug.
- **Altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.** PEGylation usually lowers peak concentration, lengthens exposure, changes tissue distribution, and reduces renal clearance of small proteins and peptides.

## Mechanism

- **Chemistry.** Attachment is usually performed at defined positions so that the product is chemically homogeneous. Common strategies include reaction with primary amines (e.g. on lysine residues) using activated succinimidyl esters, attachment to N-termini, coupling to cysteine thiols using maleimide reagents, and attachment to hydroxyl groups. Site-specific or random conjugation both exist; random conjugation to a protein's lysines gives a defined average degree of substitution but a mixture of positional isomers.
- **Linker chemistry.** The choice of reactive group and linker controls how the conjugate is released. Ester-based linkers are hydrolysable; succinimidyl carbamate and "second-generation" linkers are stable. Linker choice determines whether the active drug is released at the site of action.
- **Liposomal and lipid PEGylation.** In [[Liposomes]] and [[Lipid Nanoparticles]], a DSPE-PEG or DSPC-PEG lipid is embedded in the particle surface; its PEG chains create a steric "brush" that prevents aggregation, protein adsorption and rapid opsonisation, and lengthens circulation time.
- **Steric stabilisation.** The hydrated PEG corona confers colloidal stability and reduces clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, though excessively large PEG can reduce cellular uptake — the trade-off between circulation time and target-tissue penetration.

> [!warning] PEG itself is not inert
> PEG chains are chemically stable but not biologically silent. Accumulated high-molecular-weight PEG causes "pegASUS" — a delayed, cumulative form of immune complex–mediated hypersensitivity — and repeated dosing can generate anti-PEG antibodies that cause rapid clearance of the PEGylated drug on subsequent administrations.

## Clinical Relevance

- **Complement activation and hypersensitivity.** PEGylated products have been associated with complement activation in the serum, causing infusion-related reactions, and with delayed anaphylaxis on repeat exposure. This is the main safety liability of the platform, and it drove the development of the PEGylated version of asparaginase (pegaspargase) and of hypersensitisation testing before re-dosing.
- **Reduced drug clearance and target-mediated drift.** Because PEGylated drugs are long-lived, dose-finding must account for accumulation; pegfilgrastim, for instance, has a much longer effective exposure than filgrastim.
- **Nucleic-acid and vaccine delivery.** PEG-lipids are standard in mRNA vaccine and siRNA nanoparticle formulations, where they tune biodistribution and reduce innate immune activation against the lipid component.
- **Manufacturing and regulatory burden.** PEG must be well-characterised (including for high-molecular-weight and crosslinked species), and anti-PEG antibody assays are increasingly required; regulatory guidance has tightened on these points.
- **Therapeutic alternatives.** Because PEG has its own issues — anti-PEG immunity, renal accumulation, and reduced tumour penetration — alternatives such as polysarcosine, XTEN, and albumin-binding domains are in clinical development.
