# Coumadin

**Coumadin** is a brand name, not a molecule. It is the trade name under which Bristol-Myers Squibb (originally DuPont Pharmaceuticals) sold [[Warfarin|warfarin sodium]] tablets, and it is the designation under which the drug entered the collective pharmacology lexicon. The active compound is the 4-hydroxycoumarin anticoagulant; everything mechanistic, pharmacokinetic and clinical in this note belongs to that compound and is covered in detail in [[Warfarin]]. This note exists to resolve the brand-name wikilinks and to record the brand's own history.

## Product history

Warfarin was isolated in 1939 by Karl Paul Link and colleagues from spoiled sweet clover, which was killing cattle; it was the first commercially produced anticoagulant and one of the most successful drugs ever derived from a natural toxicosis. DuPont sold it as Coumadin, patented the product in 1942 (the patent expired in 1962), and the brand dominated the US market for the next four decades.

Generic warfarin sodium was FDA-approved as bioequivalent and therapeutic equivalent in March 1997 and launched in July 1997 at a fraction of the branded price. DuPont's response — including promotional material discouraging substitution — became the subject of a consolidated consumer class action settled for $44.5 million plus $10 million in fees (*In re Warfarin Sodium Antitrust Litigation*, 391 F.3d 516, 3d Cir. 2004). Coumadin still held roughly two-thirds market share when the complaints were filed, more than five years after generic entry, which is what made the case unusual.

> [!info] Discontinuation
> In April 2020 Bristol-Myers Squibb announced that all strengths of Coumadin tablets would be discontinued in the US, Canada, Latin America and Saudi Arabia, beginning around 1 June 2020 and completing by 31 August 2020. The stated cause was an **unresolved manufacturing issue**, explicitly not a safety, quality or efficacy problem. The brand's NDA (009218) now shows every tablet and vial strength discontinued. The molecule is unaffected: generic warfarin sodium remains available from many manufacturers, and the alternative brand Jantoven (Upsher-Smith) was not affected at the time.

## Why the name still appears in the literature

Coumadin persists in the clinical vocabulary for three reasons. It is the name under which warfarin's narrow therapeutic index and its laboratory monitoring requirement — the [[INR]] — were established in decades of practice, so it appears throughout the trial literature on vitamin K antagonists. It is the name patients recognise, and it persists in clinical records and patient communications long after generic entry. And it is the historical reference point in pharmacogenomics, since the CYP2C9 and VKORC1 dose-prediction literature overwhelmingly used Coumarin-labelled drug.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Switching from brand to generic warfarin can move the INR and requires closer monitoring on the transition. A standard practice is to expect a small change once and to re-check the INR rather than to assume continued stability, since formulation differences are permitted within bioequivalence limits. Conversely, patients who have been stable on one brand indefinitely should not be switched for convenience without a follow-up INR.

## Connections

- [[Warfarin]] — Coumadin is the brand name of warfarin sodium; every pharmacological and clinical property of the drug is that of the compound described in the Warfarin note. Warfarin itself lists Coumadin as an alias, so the two notes are two views of one substance.
- [[INR]] — The prothrombin time normalised to the international normalized ratio is how Coumadin therapy is monitored, and its narrow therapeutic window is the defining clinical problem of the brand.
- [[Vitamin K]] — Coumadin's whole pharmacological identity is as a vitamin K antagonist; the drug's history runs from spoiled sweet clover through Link's isolation to the modern vitamin K cycle.
- [[Vitamin K Epoxide Reductase]] — VKORC1 is the molecular target that defines the drug class; coumarins block recycling of vitamin K epoxide to the reduced cofactor needed for gamma-carboxylation.
- [[VKORC1]] — The VKORC1 gene is the single largest determinant of warfarin (Coumadin) dose requirement, and the -1639G>A promoter variant is the cornerstone of pharmacogenetic dosing algorithms originally developed for the brand.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — Coumadin was for decades the default oral anticoagulant worldwide and remains the reference oral vitamin K antagonist against which direct oral anticoagulants are compared.
- [[Thromboembolism]] — Prevention and treatment of thromboembolic disease was Coumadin's original and remains its core indication, from atrial fibrillation to mechanical heart valves.
- [[Protein C]] — Protein C is a vitamin K-dependent anticoagulant protein that Coumadin also depletes, and its early fall is why warfarin initiation requires heparin overlap and why skin necrosis and paradoxical thrombosis occur.
- [[CYP2C9]] — CYP2C9 metabolises the far more potent S-warfarin enantiomer; the *2 and *3 alleles were the first pharmacogenetic dosing variables entered for Coumadin prescribing.
- [[Vitamin K Epoxide Reductase Complex]] — Coumarins inhibit the reductase complex as a whole rather than VKORC1 in isolation, and the complex's other subunits are why coumarin resistance maps to VKORC1 rather than elsewhere.
- [[CYP3A4]] — CYP3A4 is one of several P450 isoforms clearing the R-enantiomer; inhibitors and inducers of it are the commonest source of clinically important INR drift on Coumadin.
- [[Deep vein thrombosis]] — Treatment and secondary prevention of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism were core Coumadin indications and remain indications for generic warfarin.
- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] — Stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation is the indication for which Coumadin has most effectively been displaced by direct oral anticoagulants, driving the brand's commercial decline.
