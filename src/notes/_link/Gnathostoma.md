# Gnathostoma

*Gnathostoma* is a genus of spirurid [[Nematode|nematodes]] (roundworms) characterised by a head armed with cutting spines — the name means "jaw mouth". Around a dozen species infect humans, but **G. spinigerum** accounts for the overwhelming majority of cases, with *G. hispidum* and *G. doloresi* as documented but rarer causes. It was described from the stomach of a dead tiger at London Zoo by Richard Owen in 1836; the first human case was reported by Levinson in 1889 in a Thai woman; and the full lifecycle was worked out in Thailand by Svasti Daengsvang and Chalerm Prommas in 1933–1936.

## Lifecycle

> [!info] Transmission and migration
> 1. Adult worms mature in a **tumour-like granuloma in the gastric wall of the definitive host** (cats, dogs, tigers, raccoons) and shed eggs into the gut lumen.
> 2. Eggs hatch in water into first-stage larvae, which are ingested by **copepods of the genus *Cyclops*** — the intermediate host.
> 3. Inside the copepod the larva develops to the infective **third-stage (L3) larva**.
> 4. Humans become infected by eating raw or undercooked freshwater fish (raw *som*fuk in Thailand, *raad* in Korea), raw or undercooked frog/snake, or by drinking water carrying infected copepods; it is a food-borne zoonosis.
> 5. L3 larvae are released in the stomach, **penetrate the gastric mucosa**, and then migrate aberrantly through skin, subcutaneous tissue and, in a minority of cases, the eye or the CNS. It is this migration — not a local infection — that produces the clinical syndrome.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Diagnosis rests on a **rising antibody titre** and on the migratory character of the swelling; larvae are recovered by surgical exploration in only a minority of cases. Serology is species-discriminatory but not perfectly sensitive, and antibody can persist for years after cure, which complicates relapse assessment. Immunocompromised hosts with a high parasite burden are described, which is one of the few places in parasitic disease where a worm burden really does scale with host immune status.

## Clinical presentation

The classic syndrome is **creeping eruption**: intermittent, non-pitting, oedematous, erythematous swellings of the face, trunk, or limbs, each lasting days to weeks, resolving, then recurring at a different site. This migratory track is diagnostic and follows the larval path rather than any anatomical structure. Accompanying features include pruritus, pain, and — when the larva dies — an intense inflammatory flare that can mimic [[Eosinophils|eosinophilic]] cellulitis.

> [!info] Extra-cutaneous disease
> - **Neurognathostomiasis**: the most serious form. Larvae entering the CNS produce eosinophilic meningitis or a focal mass lesion, with headache, cranial nerve palsies and radicular pain. The hallmark CSF picture is eosinophilic pleocytosis with markedly elevated protein and normal glucose.
> - **Ocular**: subconjunctival or orbital granuloma, usually unilateral, often requiring excision.
> - **Pulmonary**: larva in the airway or lung parenchyma, causing cough, haemoptysis or a wandering opacity on imaging; the larvae have been successfully recovered by bronchoscopy.

## Treatment

Surgical removal of an accessible larva is the most reliable intervention. The two effective chemotherapeutics are [[Albendazole]] (400 mg daily, 21 days) and [[Ivermectin]], both used as adjuncts to surgery where a larva can be located.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> CDC reports cure rates at 6 months of **>90% with albendazole** and **76–95% with ivermectin**, but these come from trials too small to firmly establish efficacy, and two small studies with ≥1 year follow-up found albendazole cure rates **falling over time**. Relapse is not uncommon with either drug and has been documented **up to 26 months** after treatment — a consequence of the fact that drugs act on the worm, not on already-deposited inflammatory damage.
>
> Whether to treat ocular or CNS disease with antihelminthics at all is **genuinely controversial**, because there are no published efficacy studies and both drugs can make things worse: albendazole provokes outward larval migration, and ivermectin can flare cutaneous symptoms. With CNS involvement, killing a larva in place may worsen the neurologic deficit, and the risk of death or permanent damage is not quantifiable. Local steroid cover is used for the eosinophilic meningitis component.
