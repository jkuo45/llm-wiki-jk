## Overview

Checkpoint inhibitors are therapeutic antibodies (and some small molecules) that disrupt the inhibitory "checkpoint" receptors used by tumors and senescent cells to silence cytotoxic lymphocytes. By removing these brakes, they re-enable T-cell recognition and killing of **[[Cancer]]** cells. They counter one of the principal mechanisms of **[[Immune Evasion]]** and have produced durable responses across multiple malignancies.

## Mechanism of Action

- **PD-1 / PD-L1 blockade** (nivolumab, pembrolizumab; atezolizumab, avelumab): prevents PD-1 engagement on T cells, reinvigorating exhausted CD8+ T cells. See [[PD-1]] and [[PD-L1]].
- **CTLA-4 blockade** (ipilimumab): interferes with the CD28-competing inhibitory receptor expressed early in T-cell activation, broadening priming.
- **Emerging checkpoints** (LAG-3, TIGIT, and the NK/T-cell receptor [[NKG2A]]) are being targeted alone or in combination to overcome resistance.

> [!important] Response correlates with tumor neo-antigen burden and pre-existing T-cell infiltration; primary resistance arises when alternate escape routes (antigen loss, other checkpoints, immunosuppressive stroma) dominate.

## Clinical and Research Significance

Checkpoint blockade has redefined oncology, producing long-term remissions in melanoma, lung, renal, and other cancers. Toxicity manifests as immune-related adverse events from loss of self-tolerance. In aging research, the same axis is relevant because **[[Senescent Cells]]** exploit PD-L1/PD-1 and HLA-E/NKG2A to avoid clearance — suggesting senolytic or checkpoint-modulating combinations.
