# Antithrombin III

**Antithrombin III** (ATIII, gene *SERPINC1*) is the body's most important circulating
**natural anticoagulant** — a ~58 kDa plasma glycoprotein of the serpin family that
irreversibly inactivates the coagulation serine proteases, above all **[[Thrombin]]**
(factor IIa) and **factor Xa**, and with lower activity against IXa, XIa, XIIa and
plasmin. Despite the Roman numeral, "III" is historical: antithrombins I–IV were named in
sequence of discovery and only this one is clinically relevant.

## Mechanism

ATIII is a "slow" inhibitor: it forms a covalent, kinetically trapped complex with its
target protease, with a bimolecular rate constant too slow to matter at physiological
thrombin concentrations. Two things accelerate it by orders of magnitude:

1. **Heparin / heparan sulfate.** The pentasaccharide unit binds a conformational
   "open" form of ATIII and presents it to thrombin, giving a rate enhancement of roughly
   1000-fold. The heparin–ATIII complex then also sequesters thrombin onto a negatively
   charged endothelial surface.
2. **Heparan sulfate on endothelium.** The same mechanism operates physiologically:
   endothelial heparan sulfate keeps ATIII locally activated, so the vessel wall is
   anticoagulant while circulating blood is not.

ATIII also accelerates inhibition by **heparin cofactor II**, another serpin that
selectively targets thrombin.

> [!info] The conformational switch
> ATIII exists in a closed, latent conformation (the Michaelis-like reactive centre
> loop is buried) and an open, R-state conformation. Heparin binding springs the loop
> out, exposing the reactive centre. This conformational activation — not the chemistry
> of heparin — is what makes heparin a cofactor rather than a catalyst in the ordinary
> sense.

## Genetics and clinical significance

Heterozygous loss-of-function *SERPINC1* variants give **hereditary antithrombin
deficiency**, prevalence roughly 1 in 500–5000 depending on the ascertainment method —
the most consequential of the natural anticoagulant deficiencies. It is a strong,
recurrent thrombophilia: venous thrombosis, often at a young age, and notably
**heparin-resistant** anticoagulation, because the drug has no cofactor to potentiate.
Heterozygous carriers are at roughly a 3–8-fold increased lifetime thrombosis risk.
Homozygous deficiency is very rare and often lethal in utero or in infancy.

Acquired deficiency occurs in hepatic synthetic failure, in [[Sepsis]] and
[[Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome]] (where antithrombin falls as a negative
acute-phase reactant and consumption increases), in malignancy, in nephrotic syndrome
(urinary loss), and in pregnancy. Low ATIII levels correlate with poor outcome in sepsis;
whether replacing it improves outcomes remains genuinely unsettled, and this is a real
point of ongoing dispute rather than settled practice.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> The most important practical consequence of ATIII deficiency is that UFH and LMWH may
> under-anticoagulate, so patients may need anti-Xa monitoring or higher doses. ATIII
> concentrates (available in some regions) are used in heparin-resistance from
  deficiency and in selected obstetric and surgical settings; routine
  supplementation in sepsis or critical illness is **not** supported by strong
  randomised evidence.

## Role in the vault's other notes

- [[Heparin]] works *only* through ATIII — the mechanistic hinge for the whole heparin
  class.
- [[Warfarin]] lowers ATIII indirectly and slowly, which is why its anticoagulant effect
  takes days to appear.
- [[Heparin-induced Thrombocytopenia]] is an immune reaction to heparin–platelet
  complex, mediated by PF4 antibodies; AT is not involved, but AT-lowering heparin
  resistance is a common mimic.

## Documents

- [[Heparin]] — heparin has no intrinsic anticoagulant activity; it acts entirely by
  potentiating antithrombin, and the ~1000-fold rate enhancement is the single number
  that explains the heparin class.

## Connections

- [[Heparin]] — heparin is a cofactor for ATIII, not an inhibitor in its own right.
  This distinction is why heparin resistance (from ATIII deficiency, or from an
  antithrombin-binding heparin-neutralizing antibody in HIT) presents as heparin
  "failure" rather than as an alternative target.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — ATIII is the endogenous member of the anticoagulant category; the
  drug classes listed there all either boost it ([[Heparin]]) or reduce the supply of its
  substrates ([[Warfarin]]).
- [[Thrombin]] — ATIII's principal target and the protease whose inhibition dominates
  the physiological antithrombotic effect, since factor Xa is largely intercepted
  upstream by the vessel wall.
- [[Protein C]] — the other physiologically important natural anticoagulant, with a
  different locus of action: protein C inactivates factors Va and VIIIa in the
  prothrombinase complex, whereas ATIII acts directly on the protease. Their deficiencies
  produce similar thrombophilias, which is why both appear together in thrombophilia
  workups.
- [[Warfarin]] — vitamin K depletion lowers ATIII alongside the procoagulant factors, so
  warfarin's effect on the natural anticoagulant is a small and delayed part of its total
  action.
- [[Sepsis]] — acquired ATIII deficiency is a recognized marker of sepsis severity;
  whether correcting it changes outcomes is unresolved and an active area of debate.
