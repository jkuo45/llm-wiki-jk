# Methotrexate

**Methotrexate (MTX)** is a folate antagonist of the [[Antifolate|antifolate]] class (C₂₀H₂₂N₈O₅, MW 454.4). It is one of the most widely used drugs in medicine, sitting at the intersection of cytotoxic chemotherapy and low-dose immunomodulation. As an anticancer agent it is a backbone of leukemia, lymphoma, breast cancer and osteosarcoma regimens; as an immunosuppressant it is the first-line disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) in [[Rheumatoid Arthritis]] and is also used in [[Psoriasis]], [[Multiple Sclerosis]] and severe [[Autoimmune Diseases|autoimmune disease]].

## Mechanism

> [!info] Mechanism
> Methotrexate is a competitive inhibitor of **dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)**, with roughly 1000-fold higher affinity for the enzyme than the natural substrate dihydrofolate. DHFR regenerates tetrahydrofolate, the one-carbon donor required for *de novo* synthesis of thymidylate and purine bases. Blocking it therefore depletes reduced folate pools, stalls [[Thymidylate Synthase]], and inhibits DNA, RNA and thymidylate synthesis. In proliferating tumour cells — which have no salvage capacity to spare — this is lethal. The signature biochemical lesion is **folate polyglutamation**: MTX trapped in polyglutamated form cannot efflux, so inhibition becomes irreversible over time.

At the low weekly doses used in autoimmunity, DHFR inhibition is **not** thought to be the main mechanism. The prevailing model is multi-mechanistic: inhibition of purine metabolism with extracellular [[Adenosine]] accumulation (which is where much of the anti-inflammatory effect comes from, acting at A2A/A3 receptors and on [[NADPH Oxidase]]), reduced T-cell and B-cell activation and adhesion molecule expression, increased sensitivity of activated T cells to [[Fas]]-mediated death, and inhibition of methyltransferase activity.

## Pharmacology

Oral bioavailability is roughly 60% at low doses and lower at high doses. Protein binding is 35–50% for the parent drug and 91–93% for the polyglutamated 7-hydroxymethotrexate. Elimination half-life is 3–10 hours at low dose and 8–15 hours at high dose; 80–100% is excreted in urine. The drug is transported into cells by [[SLC19A1]] (reduced folate carrier 1), and reduced SLC19A1 expression is a well-characterised resistance mechanism in leukemia. Dosing is **weekly, not daily** — daily dosing causes fatal toxicity. Renal clearance is the dominant route, so impaired kidney function requires dose reduction.

## Adverse effects

| System | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Haematologic | [[Leukopenia]], anemia, [[Thrombocytopenia]], marrow suppression |
| Gastrointestinal | Nausea, [[Stomatitis]], [[Mucositis]], ulceration |
| Hepatic | Transaminase elevation, chronic hepatotoxicity, fibrosis and cirrhosis with long-term use |
| Pulmonary | Acute hypersensitivity [[Pneumonitis]] (rare but serious) |
| Renal | Acute kidney injury at high dose; reduced clearance at low dose |
| Reproductive | Teratogenic and abortifacient; avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding |
| Immunologic | Infection risk; blunted vaccine responses |

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Drug interactions are the practical hazard at routine rheumatology doses. [[Trimethoprim]]/sulfamethoxazole, [[Hydroxyurea]], probenecid, proton-pump inhibitors, NSAIDs, and trimethoprim all either reduce MTX clearance or add hepatotoxicity/hematotoxicity; the TMP-SMX combination has been reported to cause rapid, life-threatening toxicity at doses as low as 5–25 mg/week. Routine monitoring is CBC, liver enzymes, and creatinine at least every two months.

## Folate supplementation: the apparent contradiction

MTX inhibits folate metabolism, yet **folic acid** is routinely co-prescribed to reduce stomatitis, cytopenias and hepatotoxicity in low-dose therapy, and does not appear to blunt the antiproliferative effect at those doses. The reason is dose-dependent: at weekly rheumatology doses the anti-inflammatory effect is largely adenosine-mediated and does not require folate depletion, while at continuous high-dose oncology schedules a folate "rescue" is needed to protect normal tissues.

**[[Folinic acid]] (leucovorin)** is the specific rescue agent for MTX toxicity and for high-dose regimens. It is a reduced folate that does not require DHFR for initial activation, so it bypasses the blocked step. In acute MTX overdose, the combination of high-dose folinic acid, aggressive hydration with alkalinisation, and sometimes enzymatic cleavage with [[Carboxypeptidase G2]] is the standard of care. Where MTX is used purely for immunomodulation, folinic acid is *not* the standard adjunct — folic acid is.

## Clinical uses

- **Oncology** — acute lymphoblastic leukemia, non-Hodgkin [[Lymphoma]], breast [[Breast Cancer]], osteosarcoma, and choriocarcinoma/gestational trophoblastic disease (historically one of the first solid tumours cured by chemotherapy).
- **Autoimmune** — first-line weekly MTX in rheumatoid arthritis (~5–25 mg/week), also psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, myositis, systemic sclerosis, Crohn's disease, and vasculitis; combination with anti-TNF agents is common.
- **Other** — ectopic pregnancy management (an abortifacient acting on trophoblastic tissue), and as a low-dose immunosuppressive in selected antibody-resistant cases of [[Pompe Disease]] alongside [[Rituximab]].

Methotrexate was developed in 1947–1950 from the aminopterin series by Yellapragada Subbarao and Sidney Farber; the 1956 demonstration of choriocarcinoma cures established it as a curative agent. It is on the [[World Health Organization]] Model List of Essential Medicines.
