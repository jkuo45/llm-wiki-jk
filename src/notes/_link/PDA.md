# Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma (PDA)

Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common type of [[Pancreatic Cancer]], accounting for approximately 85% of all pancreatic malignancies. It arises from the ductal epithelium of the exocrine pancreas and is among the most lethal of all cancers. The 5-year survival rate remains approximately 12% across all stages combined, dropping below 3% for metastatic disease. Incidence has been steadily rising in developed nations, and the disease is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related death by 2030. The median age at diagnosis is 70 years, with peak incidence between 65 and 80. Established risk factors include cigarette smoking (2–3 fold increased risk), obesity (BMI ≥30), new-onset diabetes mellitus (within 2–3 years of diagnosis), chronic pancreatitis, and hereditary predisposition. Germline mutations in [[BRCA2]], [[PALB2]], [[CDKN2A]], [[PRSS1]], and [[STK11]] confer elevated lifetime risk, and approximately 5–10% of cases have a familial component. Despite incremental advances in systemic therapy, PDAC remains a formidable clinical challenge characterized by early metastasis, profound chemoresistance, and a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

## Molecular Pathogenesis

The molecular landscape of PDAC is defined by a relatively small number of recurrently mutated driver genes. Activating mutations in [[KRAS]] are found in over 90% of cases and are considered an early, initiating event. The predominant variants — G12D, G12V, and G12R — constitutively activate the [[MAPK]] and [[PI3K]] signaling cascades, driving uncontrolled proliferation, survival, and metabolic reprogramming. In parallel, biallelic inactivation of the [[p53]] tumor suppressor occurs in more than 70% of PDAC, abrogating cell-cycle checkpoint control and apoptotic signaling. Homozygous deletion or epigenetic silencing of [[CDKN2A]] (encoding p16^INK4a^) is observed in approximately 90% of cases, removing a critical brake on [[Cyclin-Dependent Kinases]] 4 and 6. Loss of [[SMAD4]] (DPC4), a key mediator of [[TGFβ]] signaling, occurs in roughly 55% of advanced tumors and is associated with poor prognosis and metastatic propensity. Mutations in [[APC]] are found in approximately 10% of cases, linking a subset of PDAC to [[Wnt Signaling]] pathway dysregulation.

PDAC develops through well-characterized precursor lesions. The most common is pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), which progresses through low-grade (PanIN-1, PanIN-2) to high-grade (PanIN-3, carcinoma in situ) stages before invading the basement membrane. Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) and mucinous cystic neoplasm (MCN) represent additional precursor pathways, often identifiable on cross-sectional imaging and carrying variable malignant potential. The stepwise accumulation of genetic hits — KRAS activation followed by CDKN2A loss, TP53 inactivation, and SMAD4 deletion — mirrors the histopathological progression from PanIN to invasive carcinoma.

A hallmark of PDAC is its pronounced desmoplastic reaction. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), pancreatic stellate cells, and immune cells deposit a dense extracellular matrix rich in collagen, hyaluronic acid, and fibronectin. This stroma is hypovascular and hypoxic, creating a physical and biochemical barrier that severely impairs drug delivery. The tumor microenvironment is further characterized by infiltration of immunosuppressive cells — regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and tumor-associated macrophages — that collectively orchestrate immune evasion. Understanding this stromal architecture is critical, as it underlies the therapeutic resistance that defines PDAC.

## Clinical Presentation & Diagnosis

Early-stage PDAC is typically silent, contributing to the late stage at which most patients present. Symptoms emerge as the tumor grows or invades adjacent structures. Tumors of the pancreatic head often cause obstructive jaundice due to compression of the common bile duct, accompanied by pruritus, dark urine, and pale stools. Body and tail tumors more frequently present with nonspecific abdominal or back pain, unexplained weight loss, and new-onset diabetes mellitus — a red flag in patients over 50 without traditional risk factors for [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]]. Steatorrhea (fatty stools) may result from exocrine pancreatic insufficiency when the duct is obstructed.

Diagnosis relies on a combination of imaging and tissue sampling. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with a dedicated pancreatic protocol is the primary modality for detection, staging, and assessment of vascular involvement. Endoscopic ultrasound with fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) provides tissue diagnosis and molecular characterization, particularly valuable for small lesions or equivocal imaging. Serum [[CA19-9]] (carbohydrate antigen 19-9) is the most widely used tumor marker; it is useful for monitoring treatment response and detecting recurrence, but is not suitable for screening due to limited sensitivity and specificity (it may be elevated in benign biliary obstruction). Staging follows the TNM system, with clinical categorization into resectable, borderline resectable, and unresectable (locally advanced or metastatic) disease, guiding therapeutic decision-making.

## Therapeutic Landscape

### Surgical Resection

Surgical resection offers the only potential for cure, yet only approximately 20% of patients are resectable at diagnosis. The Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy) is the standard operation for tumors of the pancreatic head, involving en bloc resection of the pancreatic head, duodenum, distal common bile duct, gallbladder, and regional lymph nodes. Distal pancreatectomy is performed for body and tail lesions. Even with R0 (margin-negative) resection, recurrence rates exceed 70%, underscoring the importance of multimodal therapy. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (administered before surgery) is increasingly employed for borderline resectable disease to downstage tumors and select favorable responders.

### Systemic Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is the backbone of PDAC treatment. FOLFIRINOX (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) demonstrated superior overall survival compared to gemcitabine in metastatic PDAC (median OS ~11.1 vs. 6.8 months) and is now a standard first-line option for fit patients. The combination of [[Gemcitabine]] plus nab-paclitaxel (Abraxane) provides an alternative regimen with a more favorable toxicity profile. For patients with poor performance status, gemcitabine monotherapy remains appropriate for palliation. Adjuvant chemotherapy — typically 6 months of modified FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine-based therapy — is standard after resection, based on the PRODIGE 24 and ESPAC-4 trials.

### Radiation Therapy

The role of radiation in PDAC is limited and generally reserved for specific scenarios. It may be considered for borderline resectable disease (as part of neoadjuvant protocols), locally advanced tumors that do not progress on chemotherapy, or for palliation of painful bone metastases. Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is an option for focal, unresectable lesions.

### Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

Targeted therapies have had a modest but meaningful impact in molecularly defined subsets. [[Olaparib]], a PARP inhibitor, is approved as maintenance therapy for patients with germline [[BRCA1]] or [[BRCA2]] mutations who have not progressed on platinum-based chemotherapy, based on the POLO trial. [[Pembrolizumab]] is approved for tumors with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), though this represents fewer than 1–2% of PDAC cases. Immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors has shown limited efficacy in most PDAC due to the immunosuppressive, "cold" tumor microenvironment and low tumor mutational burden relative to cancers like melanoma.

### Emerging Approaches

Several investigational strategies are being explored. KRAS G12C inhibitors ([[Sotorasib]], [[Adagrasib]]) have shown activity in the small subset (~1–2%) of PDAC harboring this specific mutation. Stromal-targeting approaches aim to normalize the tumor vasculature and improve drug delivery, including [[Hyaluronidase]] (PEGPH20) and hedgehog pathway inhibitors, though clinical results have been mixed. mRNA-based personalized neoantigen vaccines, in combination with checkpoint inhibitors, represent a promising frontier in harnessing adaptive immunity against PDAC. Adoptive cell therapies, oncolytic viruses, and strategies to reprogram the tumor microenvironment are under active investigation.
