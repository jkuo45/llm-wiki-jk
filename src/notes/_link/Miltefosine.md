# Miltefosine

**Miltefosine** (hexadecylphosphocholine) is an alkylphosphocholine originally developed as a topical antineoplastic and now the only **oral** agent licensed for visceral [[Leishmaniasis]] and for cutaneous leishmaniasis in several regions. Its development was unusual: it was approved in 2002 without being formally designated a new chemical entity, on the basis of repurposing data from oncology and dermatology.

## Chemistry and membrane physics

> [!info] Mechanism
> Miltefosine is **zwitterionic** — a permanently charged phosphocholine head group on a saturated C16 hexadecyl tail. It partitions preferentially into the outer leaflet of lipid bilayers, where the charged head anchors and the hydrocarbon tail intercalates among the acyl chains. The result is a membrane that is simultaneously **more fluid** (disordered) and less structurally coherent: membrane protein function, lipid raft organisation, and permeability are all disturbed. The parasite's membrane is the primary target because miltefosine is taken up preferentially by [[Leishmania]] promastigotes and amastigotes and concentrated there.

The most consistently reproduced observation across *L. donovani*, *L. braziliensis*, and *L. amazonensis* models is a dose-dependent loss of membrane integrity: loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, perturbations of lipid and protein dynamics, altered activity of Ca²⁺-ATPase and related transporters, and, at higher concentrations, cell lysis.

Beyond the membrane, several additional targets have been proposed and none is fully settled:

- **Phospholipid metabolism** — inhibition of *Leishmania* phosphatidylcholine synthesis via inhibition of a *Leishmania*-specific **phosphatidylcholine synthase** (the alkylphosphocholine mimics the substrate choline, and the drug is a substrate-trapping inhibitor). Miltefosine also perturbs sphingolipid metabolism, notably by disturbing the subcellular distribution and metabolism of [[Sphingomyelin]] and [[Sphingosine]] pools.
- **Acidocalcisomes** — the drug impairs the function of these polyphosphate-rich organelles, with downstream effects on cation homeostasis and on stress signalling. This is a comparatively recent and still-developing line of evidence.
- **Ca²⁺ channels and signalling** — a plasma-membrane Ca²⁺ channel has been reported to be activated by miltefosine in *L. donovani*, altering intracellular calcium and, downstream, sphingosine-dependent signalling and apoptosis. Work in a *Leishmania* model also implicates altered sphingolipid-dependent signalling in the parasite's own commitment to cell death.
- **Mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase** — listed as a target in drug databases, though the weight of the direct evidence is modest compared with the membrane literature.

> [!warning] Evidence caveat
> The mechanism is genuinely multi-target, and the literature is not convergent on a single dominant target. The membrane-disruption model is best supported and appears sufficient to explain killing at clinical concentrations; the specific enzymatic and organelle targets are best described as proposed or supported in a subset of systems, not settled.

## Pharmacology and clinical use

Miltefosine is well absorbed orally but extensively distributed, with a long terminal half-life (roughly 4–7 days, sometimes longer) driven by tissue and cell accumulation — and it is notably **lipophilic and cationic**, which gives it a long intracellular persistence. This long residence is a double-edged feature: it allows once-daily 28-day regimens, but also makes toxicity and drug-interaction management slow.

Standard regimens: 28 days of oral drug, weight-based, for visceral leishmaniasis; 28 days for cutaneous disease caused by *L. braziliensis* and related species, and 21 days for *L. major*.

**Adverse effects and caveats** are significant:
- **Teratogenicity** is the headline risk — the drug is contraindicated in pregnancy, and effective contraception is required during and for some time after treatment.
- Gastrointestinal intolerance is common (diarrhoea, vomiting, abdominal pain).
- Reproductive toxicity in animal studies led to sustained recommendations against use in pregnancy, and it is generally avoided in breastfeeding.
- **Resistance is a growing clinical problem**, particularly in the Indian subcontinent where relapse rates have risen substantially; the resistance mechanism is not fully characterised.

In practice, miltefosine is now usually second-line behind liposomal amphotericin B, except in settings where amphotericin B is unavailable and in some cutaneous indications. Its genuine contribution was proving that an oral antileishmanial was possible, which changed the treatment-access landscape for a disease of the poorest.
