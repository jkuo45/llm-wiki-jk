# Uncoupling Protein 3

**Uncoupling Protein 3 (UCP3)** is a member of the mitochondrial carrier (SLC25) family of uncoupling proteins, expressed most highly in [[Skeletal Muscle|skeletal muscle]] and to a lesser extent brown adipose tissue and heart. Like [[UCP2]] and [[UCP1]], it localises to the inner mitochondrial membrane and has been proposed to mediate proton conductance ([[Mitochondrial Uncoupling|mild uncoupling]]) and fatty-acid handling. Whether UCP3's primary function is thermogenic uncoupling, protection against mitochondrial oxidative stress, or export of non-esterified fatty acids from the matrix remains debated (Vidal-Puig _et al_. 2000; Schrauwen _et al_. 2001).

## Regulation by Exercise

- Endurance training lowers UCP3 protein expression in type I, IIa, and IIx muscle fibres (Russell _et al_. 2003); acute exercise and training may differentially affect UCP3 mRNA (Hesselink & Schrauwen, 2003; Jones _et al_. 2003).
- Exercise-induced increases in muscle UCP3 have also been reported as a component of mitochondrial biogenesis (Jones _et al_. 2003), so the direction of the training response is context- and protocol-dependent.

> [!info] Source: [[_document_ - speakman2003_Resting Metabolic Rate|Speakman & Selman (2003)]]
> The review invokes training-induced down-regulation of muscle UCP3 as one candidate explanation for why RMR can fall after intensive training despite gains in lean mass: lowering UCP3 may improve muscle mechanical efficiency during exercise at the cost of a reduced post-exercise resting metabolism (Russell _et al_. 2003). The authors note this interpretation is contested.

## Role in Resting Metabolic Rate

Polymorphisms near the UCP2/UCP3 locus have been linked to 24-h energy expenditure and substrate oxidation (Astrup _et al_. 1999), and UCP3 is a candidate determinant of the residual variation in [[Resting Metabolic Rate|resting metabolic rate]] not explained by lean mass. Whole-body implications are therefore that UCP3 modulates the efficiency of skeletal muscle energy conversion both during exercise and at rest.
