# Acid Hydrolases

Acid hydrolases are a superfamily of approximately 60 hydrolytic enzymes that catalyze the degradation of macromolecules—proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, nucleic acids, and phosphates—at the acidic pH (4.5–5.0) maintained within the [[Lysosome]] lumen. Synthesized in the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]], glycosylated in the [[Golgi Apparatus]], and targeted to lysosomes via mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptors, these enzymes represent the primary catabolic machinery of the endolysosomal system. Each acid hydrolase cleaves a specific type of chemical bond, and deficiency of any single enzyme results in a characteristic [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases|lysosomal storage disease]] (LSD) in which the undigested substrate accumulates to pathological levels. Beyond their intracellular degradative role, acid hydrolases are secreted by osteoclasts during [[Bone Remodeling|bone resorption]] and are present on the surface of activated immune cells, linking lysosomal function to systemic physiology.

## Classification

Acid hydrolases are grouped by the type of substrate they hydrolyze:

- **Proteases** — degrade proteins and peptides. Key members include [[Cathepsin D]], [[Cathepsin B]], [[Cathepsin L]], and [[Cathepsin H]]. Cathepsin D (an aspartyl protease) is the dominant endopeptidase of the lysosome, while Cathepsin B functions as both an endopeptidase and a carboxydipeptidase.
- **Lipases** — hydrolyze ester and acyl bonds in lipids. [[Lysosomal Acid Lipase]] (LAL) breaks down cholesteryl esters and triglycerides; [[Acid Sphingomyelinase]] cleaves sphingomyelin into ceramide and phosphocholine; [[Acid Ceramidase]] further degrades ceramide.
- **Glycosidases** — cleave glycosidic bonds in complex carbohydrates and glycoconjugates. Examples include β-glucocerebrosidase (Gaucher enzyme), [[Hexosaminidase A]] (Tay-Sachs enzyme), acid α-glucosidase (Pompe enzyme), α-galactosidase A (Fabry enzyme), and iduronidase (MPS I).
- **Nucleases** — degrade nucleic acids. Acid DNase and acid RNase break down DNA and RNA, respectively, within autophagolysosomes.
- **Phosphatases** — remove phosphate groups from phosphorylated substrates. Acid phosphatase is a well-established histochemical marker for lysosomes.
- **Sulfatases** — cleave sulfate esters from sulfated glycosaminoglycans and glycolipids. Arylsulfatase A (deficient in metachromatic leukodystrophy) and arylsulfatase B (deficient in Maroteaux-Lamy syndrome) are clinically important members.

## Mechanism of Action

All acid hydrolases share the requirement for an acidic catalytic environment. The lysosomal lumen is acidified to pH 4.5–5.0 by the vacuolar H⁺-ATPase (V-ATPase), a multisubunit proton pump embedded in the lysosomal membrane. This pH optimum is critical: at cytosolic pH (~7.2), most acid hydrolases retain minimal residual activity, providing a safety mechanism against nonspecific degradation if an enzyme is accidentally released into the cytoplasm.

The catalytic mechanisms vary across subclasses:

- **Aspartyl proteases** (e.g., Cathepsin D) use two catalytic aspartate residues to activate a water molecule for peptide bond hydrolysis.
- **Cysteine proteases** (e.g., Cathepsin B, L) employ a catalytic dyad of cysteine and histidine to form a thioester intermediate.
- **Glycosidases** use either a retaining or inverting mechanism, utilizing acidic residues (glutamate or aspartate) as proton donors and nucleophiles to cleave glycosidic bonds.
- **Lipases and phosphatases** typically employ serine-based catalytic triads or metal-ion-dependent mechanisms.

All acid hydrolases are synthesized as inactive preproenzymes. Signal peptides direct them to the ER, where they undergo folding, disulfide bond formation, and N-linked glycosylation. Mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) tags are then added in the Golgi, directing them to lysosomes.

## Lysosomal Targeting

The mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) pathway is the principal mechanism by which acid hydrolases are sorted to lysosomes:

1. **ER synthesis and glycosylation** — Acid hydrolase precursors are co-translationally translocated into the ER lumen, where N-linked glycans are added to asparagine residues.
2. **M6P tag addition** — In the cis-Golgi, GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase (encoded by [[GNPTAB]]) recognizes a conformational signal patch on acid hydrolases and transfers GlcNAc-phosphate to specific mannose residues. Two subsequent cleavage steps expose the M6P mark.
3. **Receptor binding** — In the trans-Golgi network, M6P residues are recognized by two M6P receptors: the cation-dependent M6P receptor (CD-MPR) and the cation-independent M6P receptor (CI-MPR/[[IGF2]] receptor). These receptors concentrate acid hydrolases into clathrin-coated vesicles.
4. **Endosomal delivery** — Vesicles bud from the trans-Golgi and fuse with early endosomes, which mature into late endosomes (multivesicular bodies). The acidic pH of endosomes causes acid hydrolases to dissociate from M6P receptors, which are then recycled to the Golgi.
5. **Lysosomal fusion** — Late endosomes fuse with lysosomes, delivering active acid hydrolases into the degradative compartment.

A small fraction of acid hydrolases escapes the M6P pathway and is secreted into the extracellular space. These secreted enzymes can be recaptured by cell-surface M6P receptors and redirected to lysosomes—a process exploited therapeutically in enzyme replacement therapy (ERT).

In addition to the M6P pathway, some acid hydrolases use alternative targeting signals. [[Cathepsin A]] and β-glucocerebrosidase, for example, are trafficked to lysosomes via sorting nexin (SNX) family members and LIMP-2 (lysosomal integral membrane protein-2) independent of M6P.

## Physiological Function

Acid hydrolases are essential for virtually every aspect of cellular and organismal catabolism:

- **Macromolecule degradation** — Proteases degrade endocytosed and autophagosomal proteins; glycosidases and lipases break down glycolipids, glycoproteins, and membranes; nucleases degrade nucleic acids within autophagolysosomes. The resulting monomers (amino acids, fatty acids, sugars, nucleosides) are exported to the cytoplasm via lysosomal permeases and transporters for reuse.
- **Nutrient recycling** — During starvation, autophagy delivers cytoplasmic material to lysosomes, where acid hydrolases liberate amino acids and lipids that fuel metabolic pathways. This recycling is regulated by [[mTOR]] and other nutrient-sensing pathways.
- **Bone remodeling** — Osteoclasts secrete acid hydrolases (notably Cathepsin K, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase, and [[Acid Sphingomyelinase]]) into the resorption lacuna, a sealed acidic compartment beneath the osteoclast. The low pH dissolves hydroxyapatite, while the enzymes degrade the organic bone matrix (primarily type I collagen).
- **Immune defense** — Macrophages and dendritic cells use acid hydrolases to degrade phagocytosed pathogens and process antigens for MHC class II presentation. Cathepsin S, for instance, is critical for invariant chain degradation during antigen processing.
- **Signaling regulation** — Acid ceramidase and [[Acid Sphingomyelinase]] regulate ceramide/sphingosine-1-phosphate rheostat, influencing [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]], inflammation, and cell survival.
- **Autophagic flux** — Acid hydrolases degrade autophagosomal cargo delivered to lysosomes, completing the autophagic process. Impaired acid hydrolase activity leads to autophagic stress and accumulation of undegraded material.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Deficiency of any single acid hydrolase produces a specific [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases|lysosomal storage disease]] (LSD), a group of over 50 inherited metabolic disorders collectively affecting approximately 1 in 5,000 live births. Undigested substrates accumulate within lysosomes, impairing cellular function with particular severity in the nervous system, liver, spleen, bone marrow, and muscle.

### Therapeutic Approaches

- **Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT)** — Intravenous recombinant acid hydrolase is taken up by cells via M6P receptors, delivered to lysosomes, and restores enzymatic activity. Approved ERT products include imiglucerase/taliglucerase alfa (Gaucher), agalsidase alfa/beta (Fabry), alglucosidase alfa (Pompe), laronidase (MPS I), idursulfase (MPS II), and others. ERT is effective for visceral manifestations but has limited penetration of the blood–brain barrier, limiting efficacy in neurological LSDs.
- **Substrate reduction therapy (SRT)** — Small-molecule inhibitors reduce the rate of substrate synthesis, preventing accumulation even with reduced enzyme activity. Eliglustat and miglustat (Gaucher) inhibit glucosylceramide synthase; siponimod/Miglustat reduces glycosphingolipid synthesis.
- **Pharmacological chaperone therapy (PCT)** — Small molecules stabilize misfolded mutant acid hydrolases in the ER, enhancing trafficking to lysosomes. Miglustat (also used as PCT for Fabry) and lumacaftor (for CFTR, related principle) exemplify this approach.
- **Gene therapy** — AAV and lentiviral vectors deliver functional gene copies to affected tissues, including CNS-directed delivery via intrathecal or intracerebroventricular injection. Gene therapy trials are underway for Krabbe, metachromatic leukodystrophy, and Pompe diseases.
- **mRNA therapy** — Emerging approaches deliver messenger RNA encoding the deficient acid hydrolase, enabling patient cells to produce functional enzyme. This strategy bypasses the immunogenicity and half-life limitations of recombinant protein.
