# PCI

**PCI** — percutaneous coronary intervention, synonymous with **coronary angioplasty with stenting** — is a minimally invasive, non-surgical treatment for obstructive [[Coronary Artery Disease|coronary artery disease]]. A catheter is advanced from a small puncture in a peripheral artery (usually the radial artery at the wrist, or the femoral artery at the groin) to the culprit coronary lesion; a balloon is inflated and a **stent** scaffold is deployed to open the artery and restore flow. The whole procedure is performed in a catheterisation laboratory under fluoroscopy with contrast.

> [!warning] Terminology collision in this vault
> Outside cardiology, "PCI" is also used for **prophylactic cranial irradiation**, the standard post-treatment step in limited-stage [[Small-cell lung cancer|small-cell lung cancer]]. This note covers the coronary sense; the cranial sense should be given its own note, and the small-cell lung cancer note repointed at it.

## Indications

- **Primary PCI** for STEMI — the default reperfusion strategy when it can be delivered within guideline time targets; preferred over fibrinolysis whenever achievable, because it achieves higher patency and lower reinfarction and stroke rates, and it is obligatory when fibrinolysis is contraindicated.
- **NSTE-ACS** — early invasive strategy for high-risk non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes, driven by haemodynamics, troponin and GRACE score.
- **Elective** — refractory angina, multivessel or left main disease, and anatomy unsuitable for bypass surgery.
- **Post-arrest** — urgent revascularisation is indicated after resuscitation for STEMI or high suspicion of coronary occlusion, as part of post-ROSC care.
- **Chronic total occlusion**, bifurcation, and calcified lesions are addressed with increasingly complex techniques (intravascular imaging, lithotripsy, atherectomy).

## Access, Devices and Pharmacology

- **Transradial access is now the default route** for coronary angiography and PCI because it avoids the large-vessel morbidity of the femoral route; distal transradial and other distal access variants are adopted mainly to reduce hand dysfunction and vascular complications after wrist access.
- **Stents**: bare-metal (rarely used now) and **drug-eluting** stents, which markedly reduce in-stent restenosis and target-lesion revascularisation.
- **Antithrombotic therapy**: [[Aspirin]] plus a P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel, ticagrelor or prasugrel), with an unfractionated heparin or bivalirudin during the procedure. Dual antiplatelet therapy is continued after the procedure for a duration that has shortened over time — **around six months** by default for stable disease, **1–3 months** where bleeding risk is high (e.g. with oral anticoagulation), and **12 months** where ischaemic risk is high. Proton-pump inhibitors are co-prescribed when upper-gastrointestinal bleeding risk is elevated.
- **Physiology-guided revascularization** — fractional flow reserve and iFr are used to decide whether intermediate stenoses are ischaemia-producing before stenting them.

## Complications

Access-site bleeding and haematoma; contrast-induced and contrast-associated [[Acute Kidney Injury]] (mitigated by hydration and low- or iso-osmolar contrast); [[Myocardial Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury|no-reflow]] during acute occlusion; periprocedural myocardial infarction; stent thrombosis (early, catastrophic; late, very rare); in-stent restenosis; contrast allergy; and, rarely, stroke or aortic dissection. Radial-specific complications include hand dysfunction and radial artery occlusion, which is why access-site selection and closure-device choice are actively studied.

## Connections

- [[Coronary Artery Disease]] — PCI is the interventional treatment arm of CAD management, complementing medical therapy with statins and antihypertensives; plaque-modifying therapy changes what lesions need intervening.
- [[Myocardial infarction]] — Acute coronary occlusion is the single commonest indication, and time-to-reperfusion is the outcome that drives door-to-balloon programmes and regional transfer networks.
- [[Cardiac arrest]] — After ROSC, urgent PCI is indicated for STEMI or suspected coronary occlusion, so revascularisation is part of post-arrest care rather than a separate problem.
- [[Aspirin]] — Aspirin is the backbone of periprocedural and post-procedural antiplatelet therapy; its withdrawal within the DAPT window is one of the main causes of late stent thrombosis.
- [[Acute Kidney Injury]] — Contrast exposure and atheroembolism make renal injury the most frequent non-cardiac complication, and pre-existing kidney disease is a major access and imaging decision point.
- [[Coronary Heart Disease]] — PCI outcomes, cost-effectiveness and appropriateness are evaluated against the medical-management literature that defines the alternative strategy in stable coronary disease.
