# Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy

## Overview

Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy is acute or subacute myocardial injury and systolic dysfunction driven by sustained excess of [[Catecholamines]], particularly [[Norepinephrine]] and epinephrine. It classically presents as stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy after intense emotional or physical stress, or as pheochromocytoma-associated cardiomyopathy. The syndrome spans from reversible wall-motion abnormalities to fulminant heart failure and cardiogenic shock.

## Mechanism of Function

Supraphysiologic catecholamines bind cardiac β1-adrenergic receptors, overactivating the Gs–cAMP–PKA pathway. This causes intracellular calcium overload, hypercontracture, and myocyte necrosis. [[Oxidative Stress]] rises via mitochondrial ROS and uncoupled eNOS, while [[Mitochondria]] undergo permeability transition and bioenergetic failure. Histology shows contraction-band necrosis without dominant coronary occlusion.

## Pathological and Clinical Relevance

Clinical features include chest pain, dyspnea, and acute heart failure; ECG shows ST changes and troponin rise mimicking myocardial infarction. Echocardiography reveals apical (Takotsubo) or diffuse hypokinesis with preserved basal function. Triggers include pheochromocytoma, intracranial hemorrhage, sepsis, and high-dose pressors. Management removes the catecholamine source, provides supportive heart-failure care, and uses β-blockers once hemodynamically stable. Most cases recover within weeks, but recurrence and rare mortality occur. Recognition prevents inappropriate reperfusion therapy.
