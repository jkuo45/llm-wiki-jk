# Resting Metabolic Rate

**Resting Metabolic Rate (RMR)** is the energy expended per unit time by a subject at rest in a post-absorptive state, at thermoneutral temperature, typically measured in the morning after an overnight fast using [[Indirect Calorimetry]]. It is closely related to, but slightly higher than, the basal metabolic rate (BMR), which is measured under stricter conditions (awake, supine, fasting, thermoneutral). In most human societies RMR is the largest single component of the daily energy budget, accounting for roughly 60 % of [[Energy Expenditure|daily energy expenditure]].

## Determinants

- **[[Fat-Free Mass]]** is the dominant determinant, accounting for between 50 and 70 % of the individual variation in RMR (Westerterp _et al_. 1992; Heshka _et al_. 1993; Zhang _et al_. 2002).
- Residual variation beyond lean mass is substantial — two individuals of identical fat-free mass can differ by up to 3 MJ/d — and is biological rather than methodological in origin, given a measurement precision of ~3 % (≈200 kJ/d).
- Residual RMR is heritable and correlates with thyroid status and circulating [[Triiodothyronine]], [[Protein Turnover|protein turnover]], [[Mitochondrial Uncoupling|mitochondrial proton leak]] (Rolfe & Brand, 1996), [[UCP2]]/[[UCP3]] polymorphisms (Bouchard _et al_. 1997; Astrup _et al_. 1999), β3-adrenoreceptor and sulphonylurea-receptor variants, circulating [[Leptin]] (Toth _et al_. 1999), and ribosomal protein L3.

> The 2003 review compiles the determinants of RMR and its residual variance, and reviews the acute (EPOC) and chronic (training) responses of RMR to physical activity in animals and humans.

## Physiological Role

RMR sustains the metabolic activity of internal organs, brain, and resting muscle (the "cost of living"). It sets the baseline against which [[Physical Activity Level|physical activity level]] (DEE:RMR) is computed. The comparative physiology literature shows that free-living small mammals average a daily energy expenditure of 2·6 × RMR (range 1·4–7·5), whereas contemporary Western adults average only 1·66 × RMR — meaning modern humans expend ~60 % of daily energy at rest (Black _et al_. 1996; Speakman, 2000).

## Exercise and RMR

Two distinct effects of physical activity on RMR are recognized:

1. **Long-term (training)**: increases in lean tissue mass raise RMR, and residual metabolism may also increase as tissue-specific metabolic intensity rises (e.g., induction of aerobic enzymes such as cytochrome-c oxidase and citrate synthase in [[Skeletal Muscle|skeletal muscle]] and heart).
2. **Acute (single bout)**: transient elevations in RMR after exercise, termed [[Excess Post-Exercise Oxygen Consumption|EPOC]], lasting from < 2 h up to ~48 h.

Results are heterogeneous: many training studies raise RMR, others find no effect, and intensive or forced regimens can paradoxically lower RMR despite gains in lean mass (Byrne & Wilmore, 2001), possibly via down-regulation of muscle [[UCP3]] or compensatory responses to overly demanding protocols.

## Clinical Relevance

RMR is the basis of predictive equations for human energy requirements (Harris & Benedict, 1919; Schofield _et al_. 1985). These equations modestly overestimate the energy demands of tropical and sub-tropical populations (≈0·5 MJ/d), and modern Western populations show no evidence of reduced BMR relative to predictions — implicating reduced physical activity rather than a lowered resting metabolism in the [[Obesity|obesity]] epidemic. Low RMR relative to [[Fat-Free Mass]] has been proposed as a risk factor for body weight regain.
