# Cochlea

The **cochlea** is the auditory portion of the inner ear: a spiral, fluid-filled bony canal in the temporal bone containing the organ of Corti, where [[Hair Cells|hair cells]] transduce mechanical vibrations into neural signals. The scala media (endolymph) and scalae vestibuli/tympani (perilymph) compartmentalize the ionic environment required for mechanotransduction. The cochlea is metabolically demanding and poorly regenerating, and its cellular populations — especially outer hair cells and spiral ganglion neurons — accumulate oxidative and mitochondrial damage over a lifetime, making cochlear aging a primary determinant of [[Age-related Hearing Loss|age-related hearing loss (AHL)]].

## SIRT3 in cochlear antioxidant defense

[[Caloric Restriction|Caloric restriction (CR)]], a well-established anti-aging intervention, alleviates AHL by reducing oxidative damage in cochlear cells — but this protective effect is abolished in [[SIRT3]]-deficient mice[350],[351]. Mechanistically, SIRT3 enhances [[IDH2]] activity under CR, boosting mitochondrial [[NADPH]] production and ROS clearance, reducing oxidative damage in cochlear cells and preserving hair cell integrity[352],[353]. The cochlea therefore exemplifies organ-specific SIRT3 dependence of an anti-aging intervention: without SIRT3, CR fails to protect auditory function.

## Clinical relevance

AHL lacks effective interventions. The SIRT3–CR coupling identifies SIRT3 as a promising therapeutic target for aging-related auditory dysfunction, with the open question of whether pharmacological SIRT3 activation can replicate CR's cochlear benefits — and whether SIRT3 levels could predict AHL susceptibility.
