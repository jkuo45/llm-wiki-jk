# Reverse Electron Transport

Reverse electron transport (RET) is the process by which electrons flow backward through the [[Electron Transport Chain]], from reduced ubiquinol (QH₂) at [[Complex I]] toward [[NADH]] rather than forward toward oxygen. Under normal forward respiration, NADH donates electrons to Complex I, which pumps protons and reduces ubiquinone. RET occurs when the mitochondrial membrane is highly polarized and ubiquinol is abundant—conditions produced by succinate oxidation through [[Complex II]] or by high substrate supply—such that the Q-pool becomes highly reduced and the high proton-motive force forces electrons upstream against their thermodynamic gradient.

RET is biologically important because it is one of the largest single sources of mitochondrial [[ROS]]. When electrons accumulate at the flavin site of Complex I in the reduced state, they can leak to molecular oxygen, generating superoxide. This makes RET a key contributor to the low-level [[Oxidative Stress]] that initiates mitohormesis, and a focus of research on metabolic and ischemic–reperfusion injury.

> [!warning] RET is potentiated by succinate accumulation (e.g., during ischemia) and by inhibitors such as metformin or high membrane potential, linking it to both pathology and therapeutic redox modulation.

Pharmacologically, RET can be suppressed by lowering membrane potential (uncoupling), by Complex I inhibitors, or by preventing succinate buildup, which reduces pathological superoxide emission. Understanding RET connects redox signaling to [[Mitochondria]] energetics and to longevity interventions that tune electron flux.
