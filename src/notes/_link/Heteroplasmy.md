# Heteroplasmy

**Heteroplasmy** describes the coexistence of more than one [[Mitochondrial DNA|mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)]] genotype within a cell, tissue, or organism. Because each cell contains hundreds to thousands of mtDNA copies distributed among dozens of nucleoids, a pathogenic mutation usually exists alongside wild-type molecules; the **mutant heteroplasmy load** — the fraction of mutant genomes — is the primary determinant of whether biochemical dysfunction and clinical disease manifest. Heteroplasmy is the inverse concept of **homoplasmy**, the state in which all mtDNA molecules in a cell share an identical sequence.

## Origins of Heteroplasmy

- **Maternal inheritance bottleneck**: During oogenesis, the mtDNA population passes through a genetic bottleneck, allowing the mutant load to shift dramatically between generations; a mildly affected mother can transmit either low or high heteroplasmy to offspring.
- **Somatic mutation accumulation**: New mutations arise throughout life due to the high replication rate of mtDNA, its proximity to [[Mitochondrial ROS|reactive oxygen species]], and relatively limited repair capacity. These somatic mutations create a mosaic of heteroplasmy levels across cells and tissues.
- **Segregation during cell division**: mtDNA is partitioned randomly (with replicative dynamics) between daughter cells, so heteroplasmy levels drift and can become tissue- and cell-specific over time.

## Threshold Effect

> [!important] Threshold Effect
> Clinical symptoms typically appear only when mutant heteroplasmy exceeds a biochemical threshold, often ~60–80% depending on the mutation and tissue. Below this threshold, wild-type mtDNA can complement the mutant molecules through [[Mitochondrial Fusion|mitochondrial fusion]] and proteostatic quality control, maintaining sufficient respiratory output. This threshold concept underlies the rationale for heteroplasmy-shifting therapies: reducing mutant load even partially can restore function.

- Wild-type mtDNA can buffer the effect of mutant molecules through complementation within the mitochondrial network.
- Tissues with high energy demand ([[Skeletal Muscle]], heart, brain) and rapidly dividing compartments are most sensitive to high heteroplasmy.
- [[Mitochondrial Fusion|Fusion]] between organelles mixes contents and can dilute mutant genomes, whereas fragmentation impairs this buffering.

## Heteroplasmy and Aging

- Somatic mtDNA mutations accumulate with age in post-mitotic and [[Stem Cells|stem cell]] compartments, driving clonal expansion of mutant genomes and rising heteroplasmy in aged tissues (Bautista & López-Cortés, 2026).
- High heteroplasmy in [[Intestinal Stem Cell|intestinal stem cells]] impairs LGR5+ cell function and organoid-forming capacity, mediated by ATF5-driven [[Mitochondrial Unfolded Protein Response|UPR^mt^]] activation and reversible with [[NMN]].
- Rising heteroplasmy is framed both as a hallmark of aging and as a driver of stem cell aging, feeding respiratory insufficiency into inflammatory signaling and metabolic–epigenetic reprogramming.
- In stem cell compartments, mitochondrial age and organelle heterogeneity — rather than heteroplasmy alone — can stratify functionally distinct subpopulations.

## Heteroplasmy as a Therapeutic Target

- Because disease manifests only above a mutant-load threshold, **reducing heteroplasmy** toward wild-type is a central therapeutic goal in mitochondrial disease and aging.
- **Selective elimination** strategies cleave only mutant mtDNA molecules: [[mitoZFNs]] (zinc finger nucleases) and [[mitoTALENs]] (TALE–FokI nucleases) introduce double-strand breaks preferentially in mutant genomes, which are then degraded while wild-type molecules repopulate the cell.
- [[DdCBE]] (DddA-derived cytosine base editors) provide an alternative, nick-based approach to correct mutant sequence rather than eliminate it.
- Proof-of-concept studies in patient cybrids and mouse models (e.g., the m.8993 T>C and m.3243A>G [[MELAS]] mutations) demonstrate that heteroplasmy shifting can restore [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]].
