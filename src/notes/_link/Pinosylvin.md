# Pinosylvin

**Pinosylvin** is a 3,5-dihydroxy-*trans*-stilbene — a [[Stilbene|stilbenoid]] and the closest structural relative of [[Resveratrol]], differing only in the absence of the para (4′) hydroxyl group. It was first isolated as the principal antifungal constituent of pine heartwood, and it is the stilbene that pine trees actually make in quantity: pine (*Pinus*), *Pseudotsuga* (Douglas fir), and *Vitis* species all produce it, and it is a **phytoalexin**, accumulating in response to fungal attack, wounding, or ultraviolet exposure rather than being a constitutive metabolite.

## Chemistry and Occurrence

The 3,5-dihydroxy pattern is not cosmetic. It creates a strong intramolecular hydrogen-bond network that holds the molecule in a rigid, planar cisoid geometry, which is precisely the geometry that fits the active site of the enzyme pinosylvin targets. This is a real mechanistic difference from resveratrol's more flexible, freely-rotating 3,4',5-trihydroxystilbene scaffold, and it is the main reason the two are not interchangeable.

> [!info] Better enzyme fit, worse pharmacology
> Pinosylvin's rigid geometry makes it a genuinely good ligand — a stronger and more selective inhibitor of several enzymes than resveratrol at matched concentration. It is also markedly **less bioavailable orally** than resveratrol, largely because of extensive first-pass conjugation of both hydroxyls (glucuronidation and sulfation) and because the planar polyphenol is a strong substrate for the transporters that limit gut permeability. The result is a consistent pattern across the literature: more potent in vitro, less useful in vivo.

Pinosylvin dimethyl ether, the naturally co-occurring methylated form, is more lipophilic and somewhat more bioavailable, and it is the form most often used in comparative studies.

## Reported Activity

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> **Pinosylvin has no approved therapeutic use in humans.** Everything below is in vitro, ex vivo, or animal work. Reviews of this compound are largely systematic tabulations of such studies, and the number of independent labs replicating any single claim is small. Treat activity claims as hypothesis-generating.

- **Antifungal.** The founding and best-supported activity: potent growth inhibition of *Candida albicans* and dermatophytes, attributed to sterol biosynthesis inhibition and disruption of ergosterol accumulation, with additional effects on membrane integrity and ATP-dependent ion transport. This is a *Pinus* defence chemical, so the antifungal framing is the physiologically meaningful one.
- **Antibacterial and antiparasitic.** Activity reported against Gram-positive organisms, *Mycobacterium*, *Leishmania*, and *Trypanosoma*, generally at concentrations in the low micromolar range.
- **Anti-inflammatory / analgesic.** Suppresses [[COX-2]]-mediated prostaglandin production and shows activity in carrageenan and other oedema models, plausibly via interference with the eicosanoid arm of the [[Cyclooxygenase|cyclooxygenase]] pathway. Both COX isoforms are inhibited in vitro, and the 3,5-dihydroxystilbene scaffold is a recognised peroxidase-mediated COX-1 inactivator in the same series as resveratrol.
- **Anticancer and chemopreventive.** Cytotoxic against several leukaemia and solid tumour cell lines at micromolar concentrations; reported to modulate NF-κB signalling and, in one study, to activate the Nrf2/ARE oxidative-stress response. Not selective — the cytotoxicity is the same mechanism as the anti-inflammatory effect at higher dose.
- **Cardiovascular and metabolic.** Reported effects on lipid accumulation, adipogenesis, and vascular tone, again largely from in vitro work.

## Place in the Stilbene Family

Pinosylvin sits in a family where structure dictates the pharmacology:

- **Resveratrol** (3,4',5-trihydroxystilbene) — far more studied, far more bioavailable, and the only member of this class with a substantial human clinical literature, which is itself equivocal
- **Pterostilbene** (3,5-dimethoxy-4′-hydroxystilbene) — the 3,5-dimethoxy substitution plus a single free OH is the classic pharmacokinetic improvement on resveratrol
- **Piceatannol** (3,4',5,3′-tetrahydroxystilbene) — more hydroxyls, correspondingly more conjugation
- **Pinosylvin** (3,5-dihydroxystilbene) — the minimal 3,5-diol, the most rigid, the least bioavailable

The consistent trade-off across this family is **planarity and enzyme affinity versus conjugation and absorption**. Pinosylvin is the extreme case, and the lesson generalises: it is a good structural probe of the stilbene pharmacophore and a poor drug.

## Connections

- [[Resveratrol]] — the two differ by a single hydroxyl and behave quite differently as a result. Pinosylvin is the more potent enzyme inhibitor and the poorer drug; resveratrol is the reverse. Together they are the cleanest illustration in the stilbene class of the planarity-versus-bioavailability trade-off.
- [[Stilbene]] — pinosylvin is the archetypal stilbene phytoalexin: a pine defensive compound made in response to fungal attack. Its antifungal mechanism is the plant's own, not something borrowed from medicine.
- [[COX-2]] — pinosylvin inhibits cyclooxygenase activity in vitro, which is the mechanistic basis for its reported anti-inflammatory and chemopreventive effects. Note the enzyme-inhibition data are assay-dependent and the two COX isoforms are not equally affected.
- [[Cyclooxygenase]] — the peroxidase-mediated inactivation of COX-1 by 3,5-dihydroxystilbene scaffold compounds is a well-characterised sub-mechanism within the resveratrol series that pinosylvin belongs to.
- [[Leishmaniasis]] — leishmanicidal activity has been reported for pinosylvin, which makes it a point of contact between this phytochemical and the antiparasitic drugs in the vault ([[Pentamidine]], [[Paromomycin]]). The evidence is early-stage and not a basis for clinical inference.
- [[Pterostilbene]] — both are pine-derived stilbenes; pterostilbene's methylation pattern gives it the better pharmacokinetics, pinosylvin's planarity gives it the better enzyme binding.
- [[Polyphenols]] — pinosylvin is a polyphenolic phytoalexin, and the conjugation-driven poor bioavailability is the standard limitation of that whole class rather than something specific to this molecule.
- [[Resveratrol]] and the sirtuin connection — worth stating explicitly that the resveratrol/sirtuin hypothesis was built on resveratrol, and evidence that resveratrol meaningfully activates sirtuins in vivo is contested. Transferring any resveratrol narrative to pinosylvin on the basis of structural similarity alone is not warranted.
