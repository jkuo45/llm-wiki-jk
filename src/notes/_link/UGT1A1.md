# UGT1A1

**UGT1A1** is the uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 enzyme — one member of the [[UDP-glucuronosyltransferases]] superfamily of phase II conjugating enzymes. It is the enzyme that glucuronidates **bilirubin** in the liver, and it is also the enzyme that inactivates **SN-38**, the active metabolite of [[Irinotecan]]. That second role is why UGT1A1 has become one of the most heavily genotyped pharmacogenes in oncology: a single promoter polymorphism, UGT1A1\*28, predicts both a benign hyperbilirubinaemia and a serious chemotherapy toxicity.

## Mechanism

UGTs are resident endoplasmic reticulum enzymes that transfer glucuronic acid from UDP-glucuronic acid onto a lipophilic substrate's nucleophilic group — a hydroxyl, carboxyl, amine or thiol. The resulting glucuronide is more water-soluble and typically more easily excreted in bile or urine.

Two structural features matter for UGT1A1:

- **One gene encodes one protein but multiple functional isoforms** through alternative splicing. The UGT1A1\*1 isoform glucuronidates bilirubin efficiently; the splice variant UGT1A1\*28 — formed by incorporating an extra exon encoding a K282Q substitution in the C-terminal domain — has markedly reduced bilirubin conjugation activity. The same protein contributes to the glucuronidation of many other substrates.
- **Substrate selectivity is broad but not unlimited**, so a genotype affects the disposition of several drug classes at once.

> [!info] The same genotype, two very different clinical consequences
> Reduced UGT1A1 activity produces mild, benign unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (Gilbert syndrome, present in up to ~10% of the population) and simultaneously raises exposure to the active drug metabolite SN-38, producing severe, potentially fatal irinotecan toxicity. One functional consequence is trivial, the other is life-threatening — which is precisely why UGT1A1 genotype must be interpreted against a specific drug, not as a general "poor metaboliser" flag.

## Clinical Relevance

**Gilbert syndrome.** A benign inherited reduction in bilirubin glucuronidation causing intermittent mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, typically triggered by fasting, illness, dehydration or strenuous exercise. It requires no treatment and is distinguished from haemolysis and from the conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia of hepatobiliary disease by the isolated unconjugated pattern with normal transaminases. The diagnosis is clinical and genetic confirmation is rarely needed.

**Irinotecan pharmacogenetics.** Irinotecan (CPT-11) is a prodrug converted by carboxylesterases to SN-38, a far more potent topoisomerase I inhibitor. UGT1A1 is the principal route of SN-38 clearance. Homozygous \*28/\*28 carriers have reduced glucuronidation, higher SN-38 exposure, and substantially increased risk of **severe neutropenia and delayed-onset diarrhoea** — the two irinotecan dose-limiting toxicities. Dose reduction or alternative agent selection is considered in these patients; Innocenti et al. (2006) proposed a ~20% irinotecan dose reduction based on SN-38 exposure, though dosing is increasingly individualised by genotype rather than by a fixed rule. The same toxicity logic applies to sacituzumab govitecan, an antibody–drug conjugate that delivers an SN-38 payload.

**Chemoprevention and polyphenols.** Dietary flavonoids are extensively glucuronidated in enterocytes and liver, which limits oral bioavailability — the constraint the vault's fisetin and apigenin notes both confront. UGT1A1 induction is one route under active investigation for raising circulating aglycone exposure: apigenin induces UGT1A1 transcription (but not GSTA1) in colorectal adenocarcinoma cells, and it does so synergistically with sulforaphane, which induces both.
