# Anticoagulant

An **anticoagulant** is any agent that reduces the *formation* of a blood clot. This
distinguishes it from an [[Platelet aggregation|antiplatelet]] agent, which blocks the
primary haemostatic plug, and from a fibrinolytic, which *dissolves* a clot that has
already formed. Anticoagulants act on one of three arms: a cofactor-activated serine
protease inhibitor ([[Antithrombin III]]), the vitamin K-dependent carboxylation
machinery ([[Warfarin]]), or the thrombin/factor Xa enzymes directly (the DOACs).

## The coagulation target

Coagulation is a protease cascade. Tissue injury exposes tissue factor, which with
factor VIIa activates the extrinsic arm; the intrinsic arm (XII, XI, IX, VIII) is
largely a contact-activation amplifier. Both converge on the **common pathway**:
factor Xa, with Va, generates thrombin; thrombin cleaves [[Fibrinogen]] to fibrin and
activates factor XIII to crosslink it. Anticoagulants all attack one of:

- **Thrombin** (factor IIa) — the effector enzyme.
- **Factor Xa** — the convergence point, upstream of thrombin.
- **The cofactors** — antithrombin, protein C, protein S.
- **The synthesis machinery** — vitamin K epoxide reductase.

## Classes

**Heparin-class (indirect, parenteral).** Unfractionated [[Heparin]] and low
molecular-weight heparins act by massively accelerating [[Antithrombin III]], so they
are *indirect* agonists. UFH is reversible, monitored with [[aPTT]], and is
antidoted by protamine; LMWH is longer-acting, weight-based, and less reversibly
antagonised.

**Vitamin K antagonists (oral, indirect).** [[Warfarin]] inhibits [[VKORC1]], preventing
recycling of oxidised vitamin K and therefore the γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX
and X and of the natural anticoagulants [[Protein C]] and protein S. Slow onset and offset,
and dependence on [[Protein C]] depletion, produce a paradoxical early
pro-coagulant state — the rationale for bridging heparin in high-risk situations.
Monitoring by [[INR]].

**Direct oral anticoagulants (oral, direct).** These inhibit the enzymes themselves:
rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban (factor Xa) and dabigatran (thrombin). No routine
monitoring, faster onset/offset, fewer drug–drug interactions with
cytochrome-P450-metabolised drugs, and no bridging. Trade-offs: renal clearance (a contraindication in advanced
renal failure for dabigatran, dabigatran's faecal elimination complicating
gut-level drug interactions), and the absence of specific antidotes at the time of
writing — andexanet alfa reverses factor Xa inhibitors.

**Older agents.** Unfractionated heparin, warfarin and DOACs displaced heparinoids,
ticlopidine-class agents and much of the routine use of aspirin, which occupies a
narrower place in primary prevention.

## Monitoring

- **aPTT** for UFH, **anti-Xa** for LMWH in renal impairment or obesity.
- **INR** for warfarin; the target is 2.0–3.0 for most indications, lower with a
  mechanical valve context.
- DOACs: no routine assay; drug-specific calibrated anti-Xa or diluted thrombin time if
  levels are needed.
- Thrombocytopenia with heparin should prompt exclusion of heparin-induced
  thrombocytopenia (HIT), which is prothrombotic despite the low count.

## Clinical use

Primary indications: [[Atrial Fibrillation]] stroke prevention, [[Pulmonary Embolism]]
and deep-vein thrombosis treatment, prevention of VTE in surgical and cancer patients,
mechanical heart valves, and prevention of thrombosis on vascular access or during
dialysis.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Anticoagulation reduces thromboembolic events at the cost of bleeding, and in atrial
> fibrillation the net benefit is only clear once stroke risk (commonly scored with
> CHA2DS2-VASc) exceeds bleeding risk. Bleeding risk is *not* captured by
> HAS-BLED-type scores, which instead flag modifiable bleeding drivers. Anticoagulants
> also interact additively with [[Platelet|antiplatelet]] agents and with
  non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which is the single most common preventable
> cause of anticoagulant-related harm.

## Documents

- [[Heparin]] — indirect antithrombin-dependent anticoagulant, parenteral, monitored by
  aPTT; the reference fast-acting agent.
- [[Warfarin]] — oral vitamin K antagonist; the reference oral anticoagulant, and the
  one most often contrasted with DOACs in the vault.

## Connections

- [[Antithrombin III]] — the obligatory cofactor for the heparin class; its deficiency is
  itself a thrombophilia, and it is the mechanistic hinge between [[Heparin]] and the
  endogenous anticoagulant system.
- [[Heparin]] — the exemplar indirect anticoagulant; also the clearest illustration of
  cofactor-based rather than direct enzyme inhibition, and the one that is
  reversibly antagonised.
- [[Warfarin]] — the exemplar oral anticoagulant with a narrow therapeutic index and
  CYP/VKORC1 pharmacogenetics, and the target most often displaced by the DOACs.
- [[Thrombin]] — the central effector protease that both the heparin class (via
  antithrombin) and dabigatran target; the reason factor Xa inhibitors upstream and
  thrombin inhibitors downstream produce similar clinical benefit.
- [[Protein C]] — the vitamin K-dependent natural anticoagulant whose transient depletion
  explains warfarin's paradoxical early pro-coagulant effect, and whose deficiency
  (like [[Antithrombin III]] deficiency) is an inherited thrombophilia.
- [[Plasminogen]] — the fibrinolytic system is the physiological opposite of
  coagulation; tPA-mediated conversion to [[Plasmin]] is what anticoagulants leave
  intact, and what thrombolytics directly activate.
- [[Fibrinogen]] — the substrate thrombin acts on; the clot-forming endpoint that every
  anticoagulant class acts upstream of.
- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] — the most common indication for chronic anticoagulation, and
  the reason DOACs displaced warfarin in the ambulatory population.
