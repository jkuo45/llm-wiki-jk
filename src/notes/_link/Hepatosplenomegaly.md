# Hepatosplenomegaly

Hepatosplenomegaly (HSM) is the simultaneous enlargement of the [[Liver]] and the spleen. It is important to treat it correctly as a **clinical sign, not a diagnosis** — the useful question is never "is it big?" but "which category of pathology is loading both organs at once, and why do both organs share the answer?"

> [!info] Why one process enlarges both
> The liver and spleen are the two largest filters of the venous blood returning from the splanchnic circulation, and they are linked by a single pressure relationship. Roughly 75% of hepatic blood flow comes from the portal vein, which drains the gut, spleen and pancreas. **Raised portal pressure therefore congests the spleen directly**, while the liver is being damaged by the same process that raised the pressure. This is why splenomegaly in cirrhosis is a pressure phenomenon, not an infiltrative one — the spleen is almost never infiltrated in portal hypertension, it is simply downstream of a hydraulic load.
>
> The second shared mechanism is **increased workload of the reticuloendothelial system**: chronic haemolysis (requiring more splenic and hepatic macrophages) and chronic infection with intracellular or sequestering organisms both drive enlargement of both organs through the same filter overload.

> [!info] Differential diagnosis
> **Haematological**
> - Chronic haemolytic anaemia: hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia intermedia or major, pyruvate kinase deficiency, sickle cell disease
> - Myeloproliferative and lymphoproliferative disease: [[CML]], [[Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia|CLL]], polycythaemia vera, essential thrombocythaemia, primary myelofibrosis, hairy cell leukaemia, non-Hodgkin and Hodgkin lymphoma
> - Myelodysplastic syndromes, multiple myeloma
>
> **Infectious**
> - Parasitic: malaria, visceral leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, leptospirosis
> - Viral: viral hepatitis, [[Epstein-Barr virus|EBV]], CMV, HIV
> - Mycobacterial and fungal: miliary tuberculosis, histoplasmosis
>
> **Hepatic / vascular**
> - Cirrhosis with portal hypertension (the most common cause worldwide in adults)
> - Budd–Chiari syndrome and other hepatic venous outflow obstruction
> - Congestive heart failure with right-sided failure ("nutmeg liver" plus congestive splenomegaly)
> - Hepatic tumours, including [[Hepatocellular Carcinoma|HCC]]
>
> **Infiltrative / storage**
> - Sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, haemochromatosis, [[Gaucher disease]] and other lysosomal storage diseases
> - metabolic liver disease (MASLD/NAFLD) with steatosis
>
> **Endocrine / other**
> - Acromegaly, hyperthyroidism

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Two practical points deserve emphasis. First, **massive** splenomegaly (spleen palpable ≥8 cm below the costal margin) narrows the differential sharply to chronic myeloid leukaemia, primary myelofibrosis, polycythaemia vera, hairy cell leukaemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma and Gaucher disease — a list short enough to be worth memorising. Second, splenomegaly can cause **hypersplenism**: splenic sequestration of platelets and red cells, producing cytopenias that are corrected by treating the spleen or its cause, not by transfusion. In the tropics, the Merck Manual notes that malaria, visceral leishmaniasis and schistosomiasis are the most common causes — i.e. the differential is genuinely geography-dependent, and a temperate-clinic differential that omits infection will mislead.

## Visceral leishmaniasis

The vault's canonical hepatosplenomegaly case is visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar), caused by *Leishmania donovani* complex (Old World) or *L. infantum* (New World) transmitted by sandfly. The organism is taken up by macrophages in the skin and then disseminated to the reticuloendothelial organs — spleen, liver, and [[Bone Marrow]] — where it replicates inside macrophages.

> [!info] Mechanism
> The hepatosplenomegaly is produced by three additive processes: (i) **macrophage hyperplasia and direct infection** of splenic red pulp and hepatic Kupffer cells; (ii) **parasitised macrophages exiting the marrow and spleen** to repopulate the liver, which explains why splenomegaly tends to precede hepatomegaly in the natural history; and (iii) **Kupffer cell replacement/destruction with sinusoidal obstruction**, which raises portal pressure secondarily. Anemia is multifactorial — bone marrow suppression, hypersplenism, and autoimmune haemolysis all contribute. The classic laboratory signature is pancytopenia with a hypergammaglobulinaemia and a markedly raised ESR.

Splenic aspirate has the highest diagnostic yield; bone marrow aspirate is safer (less risk of fatal haemorrhage from a congested friable spleen) but less sensitive. Treatment is liposomal amphotericin B, the current first-line.

## Connections

- [[Leishmaniasis]] — the vault's inbound document link; visceral disease is the textbook hepatosplenomegaly, with hepatomegaly among the most frequent presenting manifestations and the splenic involvement determining the anaemia.
- [[Leishmania]] — the causative protozoan, and a useful contrast to [[Helminth]]: protozoan tissue parasitism is intracellular and macrophage-resident, whereas helminths are extracellular and drive a Th2 response. The two produce very different organomegaly patterns.
- [[Portal hypertension]] — the commonest adult mechanism linking the two organs in one patient; splenic congestion is a hydraulic consequence, which is why splenectomy or shunt reduces the spleen but not the liver.
- [[Anemia]] and [[Thrombocytopenia]] — hypersplenism and marrow infiltration are the haematological consequences, and cytopenias are often the presenting feature rather than the organomegaly.
- [[Malaria]] — the other great tropical cause; chronic malaria produces splenomegaly by the same filter-overload mechanism plus chronic immune stimulation of the spleen.
- [[Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia]] and [[CML]] — the malignant end of the differential, and the haematological-disease notes that most often present with HSM.
- [[Hepatocellular Carcinoma]] and [[Liver]] — primary hepatic causes; the liver is enlarged and nodular rather than smooth, which is a useful bedside discriminator from congestive or infiltrative causes.
- [[Gaucher disease]] — an infiltrative/storage cause worth remembering because it can be mistaken for a haematological malignancy.
- [[Bone Marrow]] — shared site of disease in haematological malignancy, storage disease and visceral leishmaniasis, which is why marrow aspiration features in so many of these workups.
