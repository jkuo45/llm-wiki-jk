# aPTT

**aPTT** (activated partial thromboplastin time) is a laboratory clot-timing assay that measures how fast plasma forms a fibrin clot after the intrinsic coagulation pathway is triggered by a contact activator. Historically called the kaolin-cephalin clotting time (KCCT), it is the companion test to the [[INR|prothrombin time (PT)]], which interrogates the extrinsic and common pathways.

## Methodology

> [!info] Step by step
> 1. Whole blood is drawn into a **citrate tube**; citrate chelates calcium and arrests clotting ex vivo. Citrate:blood ratio matters — underfilled tubes falsely prolong the aPTT.
> 2. The sample is centrifuged to separate cells from **citrated plasma**.
> 3. Plasma is warmed to 37 °C and an excess of **calcium** is added in a phospholipid suspension, reversing the citrate chelation.
> 4. A **contact activator** is added — kaolin, silica, celite, or ellagic acid. These activate the intrinsic pathway (and the common pathway) by triggering factor XII and the complement/contact system.
> 5. The time to clot formation is recorded optically, or detected mechanically (which avoids lipemic and icteric interference, a real advantage in jaundiced or dyslipidemic patients).

The "**partial**" refers to the reagent: unlike PT, the aPTT thromboplastin contains **phospholipid but no tissue factor**. Tissue factor is the receptor for factor VII that initiates the extrinsic pathway; omitting it means the assay bypasses factor VII entirely, which is exactly why **factor VII deficiency is not detected by aPTT** (and neither is factor XIII deficiency, since factor XIII acts after clot formation).

## What It Tests

A normal aPTT requires an intact set of: factor I ([[Fibrinogen|fibrinogen]]), II ([[Prothrombin|prothrombin]]), V, VIII, IX, X, XI, and XII. Because the assay also depends on phospholipid surface and calcium, it reports on the whole intrinsic-plus-common cascade rather than on any single factor.

Reference range is typically **25–33 seconds**, laboratory-dependent, with a slightly higher normal in infants (up to ~50 s). The aPTT is usually normal in pregnancy and trends slightly lower in late pregnancy.

## Interpretation

> [!info] Prolonged aPTT
> - **Anticoagulant therapy or effect** — [[Heparin]] (unfractionated), or [[Warfarin]] at therapeutic levels; heparin contamination of the sampling line is a classic false positive
> - **Vitamin K deficiency** — including from [[Vitamin K]] antagonist overdose or malabsorption
> - **Factor deficiency** — hemophilia A (VIII) and B (IX), [[Systemic Lupus Erythematosus|antiphospholipid antibodies]] including lupus anticoagulant, von Willebrand disease, severe liver disease/cirrhosis (reduced factor synthesis), and specific factor XI or XII deficiency
> - **Factor consumption** — sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation
> - **Specific factor inhibitors** — acquired factor VIII inhibitors

> [!warning] The lupus anticoagulant paradox
> Lupus anticoagulant **prolongs the aPTT while increasing thrombotic risk in vivo.** It is an antibody that interferes with the phospholipid-dependent assay in vitro, while in the patient it promotes clotting. A prolonged aPTT in this context is a marker of hypercoagulability, not of bleeding tendency — one of the most important pitfalls in interpreting this test.

> [!warning] Shortened aPTT
> Generally considered of little clinical significance, but at least one line of work associates short aPTTs with increased thrombin generation and elevated thromboembolic risk. This association is not used clinically and is much less well established than the causes of prolongation.

## The Mixing Study

The standard next step for an isolated prolonged aPTT is a **mixing test**: patient plasma is mixed 1:1 with normal plasma and re-run.

- **Corrects** → a factor deficiency (the normal plasma supplies the missing factor). Factor VIII, IX, XI, XII deficiencies and von Willebrand disease (via low factor VIII) typically correct.
- **Does not correct** → an inhibitor is present: heparin, antiphospholipid antibodies, or a specific factor inhibitor.

Incubation (immediate vs. time-dependent mixing) further separates immediate-acting inhibitors (lupus anticoagulant, heparin) from time-dependent factor VIII inhibitors, which are characteristic of acquired hemophilia A.

## Clinical Uses

1. **Monitoring unfractionated [[Heparin]]** — this is the test's most important practical role, precisely because UFH has a narrow therapeutic window and unpredictable pharmacokinetics, so fixed dosing is unsafe. Historically the target was 1.5–2.5× the control. Low-molecular-weight heparins are monitored by anti-Xa levels instead, since they do not reliably prolong aPTT at therapeutic doses.
2. **Detection of an intrinsic-pathway factor deficiency or inhibitor** — the presenting lab abnormality in hemophilia and in lupus anticoagulant.
3. **Screening for an unexplained bleeding tendency or thrombosis** alongside PT/INR and [[Platelet|platelet count]].
4. **APCR testing** — a modified aPTT performed with and without activated protein C yields a ratio (APCr/APCsr) used to detect factor V Leiden–type activated protein C resistance. The test was developed in 1993 and has largely been displaced by direct genotyping in most settings.

## Connections

- [[Heparin]] — the primary clinical reason the aPTT is ordered. UFH potentiates [[Antithrombin III]], which inactivates [[Thrombin]] and factor Xa; the prolongation of clot time is the pharmacological readout, and it is why UFH (unlike LMWH) requires monitoring.
- [[Antithrombin III]] — the molecular target through which heparin acts; its insufficiency causes heparin resistance, which shows up as a therapeutic aPTT that will not prolong.
- [[Thrombin]] — generated downstream in the common pathway during the assay; the endpoint (fibrin polymerization) is the point at which the timing is stopped.
- [[Prothrombin]] and [[INR]] — the extrinsic-pathway counterpart; PT/INR and aPTT together separate extrinsic from intrinsic defects, and the pair is what makes a mixing study interpretable.
- [[Fibrinogen]] — factor I; its deficiency (afibrinogenemia or dysfibrinogenemia) prolongs aPTT, and the thrombin time is the specific test used to distinguish a fibrinogen problem from a factor deficiency.
- [[Platelet]] — platelets supply the phospholipid surface for the assembly complexes, and low platelet counts prolong clot time by a different mechanism than factor deficiency; hence platelet count is always read alongside aPTT.
- [[Vitamin K]] and [[Warfarin]] — vitamin K deficiency or warfarin therapy reduces the gamma-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, and X and prolongs aPTT (PT/INR is more sensitive, which is why INR is the warfarin monitoring test).
- [[Systemic Lupus Erythematosus]] — antiphospholipid antibodies, including lupus anticoagulant, are a classic cause of a non-correcting prolonged aPTT in a patient who is paradoxically prothrombotic.
- [[Liver]] — hepatic synthetic failure reduces multiple coagulation factors and prolongs aPTT, though usually alongside prolonged PT and thrombocytopenia.
- [[Sepsis]] — disseminated intravascular coagulation consumes clotting factors and prolongs aPTT as part of a broader consumptive picture.
- [[COVID-19]]-associated coagulopathy *(suggested)* — the "acquired hypocoagulability" seen in severe COVID involves aPTT prolongation and is part of the thrombotic-then-hypocoagulable biphasic picture.
