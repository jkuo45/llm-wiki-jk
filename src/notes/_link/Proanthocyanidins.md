# Proanthocyanidins

**Proanthocyanidins (PACs)** — also called **oligomeric proanthocyanidins (OPCs)** or **condensed tannins** — are a large class of polyphenols assembled from flavan-3-ol monomer units ([[Catechin]], epicatechin, epigallocatechin, gallocatechin). They are among the most abundant polyphenols in the human diet and are prominent in grape seed, pine bark (*Pycnogenol*), cranberry, cocoa, apples, and red wine.

## Structure & Classification

- PACs are oligomers or polymers (typically dimers through decamers+) of **flavan-3-ol** subunits linked mainly through B-type (C4→C8 / C4→C6) or A-type (additional C2→O→C7 ether) bonds.
- **A-type PACs** (e.g., cranberry, some grape seed fractions) are more biochemically distinctive and resistant to degradation than B-type PACs.
- They are the polymeric end of the same flavan-3-ol axis that includes monomeric [[Catechin]]/[[EGCG]], and sit under the broader [[Polyphenols]] and [[Flavonoids]] umbrella.
- Unlike hydrolyzable tannins, condensed tannins do not hydrolyze to simple sugars plus phenolic acids; they yield anthocyanidin pigments (e.g., cyanidin) upon strong acid treatment — the origin of the name "pro-anthocyanidin."

## Mechanism of Action & Pathways

- **Antioxidant capacity:** PACs are exceptionally potent radical scavengers, often rated well above vitamin C/E in vitro (ORAC/TEAC), acting via catechol/galloyl H-donation, metal chelation, and synergism with other antioxidants. They activate the [[NRF2]]/KEAP1 antioxidant transcriptional program.
- **SIRT1 modulation:** Alongside other flavonoid STACs ([[Quercetin]], [[Butein]], [[Fisetin]], [[Kaempferol]], [[Catechin]]), dietary proanthocyanidins have been reported to activate [[SIRT1]], consistent with the shared **K-type allosteric activation** mechanism (lowering substrate K~m~) described for the STAC class (`src/notes/sirtuins/_triples.json:5813`). Their *in vivo* SIRT1 engagement is subject to the same bioavailability and fluorophore-artifact caveats that qualify natural-polyphenol STACs.
- **Metabolic / signaling crosstalk:** PACs also engage [[AMPK]], inhibit [[NF-κB]] inflammatory signaling, and modulate [[Glycation]] and [[Advanced Glycation End Products]] formation.
- **Antimicrobial adhesion:** Cranberry A-type PACs block uropathogenic *E. coli* type-1 and P-fimbrial adhesion to urothelium.

## Physiological Function

- **Vascular & endothelial protection:** Historically used for chronic venous insufficiency, edema, and microcirculation; PACs improve endothelial function, capillary resistance, and may lower blood pressure, overlapping with the cardiovascular benefits attributed to [[Polyphenols]] and [[Resveratrol]].
- **Skin & connective tissue:** Support collagen cross-linking and photoprotection; common in dermatological/cosmeceutical anti-aging formulations.
- **Neuroprotection:** Cross the [[Blood-Brain Barrier]] and attenuate oxidative/inflammatory neuronal injury in models of [[Alzheimer's Disease]] and [[Parkinson's Disease]].

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

- **Aging & age-related disease:** PACs counter age-associated [[Oxidative Stress]], [[Inflammation]], and glycative damage, and contribute to the vascular, metabolic, and neuroprotective signatures of polyphenol-rich (e.g., Mediterranean, cocoa, red-wine) diets.
- **Metabolic:** Improve insulin sensitivity and markers of [[Diabetes Mellitus]] and [[Cardiovascular Disease]] risk in several human and rodent studies, consistent with SIRT1/AMPK activation.
- **Urinary tract health:** Cranberry PACs reduce recurrent urinary tract infection incidence via anti-adhesion rather than bacteriocidal action.
- **Therapeutic positioning:** As natural [[STACs]] and [[Antioxidant|antioxidants]], PACs are investigated for healthy-aging, cardiovascular, and dermatological applications, though inter-individual gut-metabolite conversion and low systemic bioavailability limit direct target engagement.
