# Excess Post-Exercise Oxygen Consumption

**Excess Post-Exercise Oxygen Consumption (EPOC)**, historically the "oxygen debt", is the sustained elevation of metabolic rate above [[Resting Metabolic Rate|resting levels]] following a bout of physical activity. Because the energy equivalent of this extra oxygen consumption is counted beyond the exercise itself, omitting EPOC from energy-cost calculations can substantially underestimate the total energetic cost of an exercise bout.

## Phases

- **Rapid component (< 2 h)**: driven largely by replenishment of muscle and blood O₂ stores and [[ATP]] resynthesis (phosphocreatine regeneration), plus restoration of tissue oxygen stores.
- **Prolonged component (up to 48 h)**: a smaller but longer-lasting elevation, involving a shift from carbohydrate to fat metabolism, increased fat oxidation, protein turnover, and β-adrenergically mediated metabolic stimulation (Borsheim _et al_. 1998).

## Magnitude and Dependence on Intensity

- For long-duration, low-intensity activity EPOC is markedly less than the energy of the activity itself.
- For short, high-intensity activity EPOC can be 80–90 % of the total energy expended (Baker & Gleeson, 1998, 1999; Edwards & Gleeson, 2001).
- In mice sprinting maximally for 5–60 s, total costs including EPOC were 1·5–2·5-fold higher than activity energy alone; the level of EPOC was independent of duration or intensity below 60 s.

> [!info] Source: [[_document_ - speakman2003_Resting Metabolic Rate|Speakman & Selman (2003)]]
> The review highlights EPOC as the acute route by which physical activity raises resting metabolism, and warns that training studies failing to allow the long-phase EPOC to resolve may confound long-term RMR measurements. In dogs, the long-term EPOC is sensitive to β-antagonists, implicating the β-adrenergic system — a parallel to human post-exercise metabolism.

## Negative EPOC After Flight

An instructive exception: in small insectivorous bats forced to fly, post-flight RMR fell to a nadir (~0·33 W vs. ~0·55 W pre-flight) and recovered only after ~2·5 h — i.e., **negative EPOC** — with the post-flight suppression nearly matching the energy cost of flight (Speakman & Racey, 1991). Forced flights in birds similarly produce compensatory suppression of resting metabolism overnight (Deerenberg _et al_. 1998). These findings indicate that EPOC direction depends on the voluntary vs. forced nature and intensity of exercise.
