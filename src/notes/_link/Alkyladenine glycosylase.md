# Alkyladenine glycosylase

**Alkyladenine glycosylase** — more precisely *alkyladenine DNA glycosylase* — is the
human repair enzyme usually called **AAG** (also **MPG**, 3-methyladenine DNA glycosylase,
EC 3.2.2.17). It is the initiating enzyme of the **base-excision repair** pathway for
alkylated bases: a DNA glycosylase, and specifically a small helix-hairpin-helix
(HhH) superfamily enzyme that flips the damaged base out of the duplex, cleaves the
N-glycosidic bond, and releases the free alkylated base, leaving an abasic (AP) site that
APE1 and polymerase then process.

## Substrate spectrum

AAG is a **broad** alkyladenine glycosylase, historically the reason it was named "N1-
methylpurine DNA glycosylase" and now often called the 1-methyladenine glycosylase. It acts on:

- **[[3-Methyladenine]]** (3-meA) — the most abundant and best-characterized alkylation
  product, because N3 of adenine lies in the minor groove and is highly accessible.
- **1-Methyladenine** (1-meA) — blocked replication; excision by AAG is essential because
  no DNA polymerase inserts correctly opposite it.
- **7-Methylguanine** (7-meG) — abundant but not strongly mutagenic; repair is
  beneficial mainly because 7-meG is a good leaving group and its depurination produces
  mutagenic AP sites.
- **O6-methylguanine** — repaired by the *separate* and much more consequential enzyme
  O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT); AAG does not handle it. This distinction
  is often conflated and matters, because O6-meG mispairs with thymine and is the driver
  of alkylating-agent mutagenesis.

AAG also excises **etheno adducts** (1,N6-ethenoadenine, 3,N4-ethenocytosine) formed by
lipid peroxidation or aflatoxin, which links it to both carcinogenesis and exocyclic base
chemistry.

> [!info] Why alkyladenines are dangerous despite being chemically abundant
> 3-meA is the *most common* DNA alkylation lesion and by itself is poorly mutagenic.
> Its threat is that the weak N-glycosidic bond makes it prone to spontaneous
> depurination; if unrepaired, replication across the resulting AP site stalls or
> mispairs. AAG converts a bulky lesion into a small, well-tolerated AP site.

## Sources of alkylated DNA

AAG's substrates are generated by both endogenous and exogenous chemistry:

- **Alkylating carcinogens** — nitrosamines (via metabolic activation to
  N-nitroso compounds, the classical source of 7-meG, O6-meG and 3-meA),
  [[Cyclophosphamide]]/[[Ifosfamide]], platinum agents such as [[Cisplatin]],
  dacarbazine, and the environmental alkylator
  4-nitroquinoline N-oxide.
- **Endogenous methylation** — S-adenosylmethionine is a weak methylating agent and
  background N7-methylguanine accumulates at a high rate in human DNA.
- **Lipid peroxidation and reactive aldehydes.** Oxo-deoxyguanosines and etheno adducts
  arise from endogenous lipid-derived electrophiles, placing AAG in the same damage
  context as [[M₁dG]].

## Clinical and pharmacological relevance

AAG is the dominant repair enzyme for alkylating damage, which is why it is a classic
sensitization target for alkylating chemotherapy. O6-benzylguanine and its prodrug
PUGNAc block the *parallel* MGMT route, but loss of MGMT partially redirects repair
burden onto the AAG pathway. Conversely, AAG is also a liability: **AAG over-expression
in tumour cells is a known resistance mechanism against alkylating agents**, since it
removes the cytotoxic lesions the drug creates.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> AAG inhibitors are in preclinical development only; no AAG-directed therapy is
> approved. The AAG/MGMT axis as a resistance determinant is well documented in
> laboratory models, less well in patients.

AAG activity also contributes to the mutagenic burden that initiates
[[Tumorigenesis]] — persisting alkylated bases mispair during replication, producing
transition mutations such as G:C→A:T, the signature of alkylating carcinogens.

## Documents

- [[M₁dG]] — the exocyclic guanine adduct of lipid peroxidation; the same
  endogenous-electrophile damage environment in which AAG participates in base-excision
  repair, adjacent to but chemically distinct from alkyladenines.

## Connections

- [[M₁dG]] — both are base-excision-repair substrates arising from electrophilic
  damage; AAG excises alkyladenines and etheno adducts, while the M1dG adduct is
  handled by dedicated exocyclic-base glycosylases, so the two are parallel branches of
  the same repair pathway rather than the same substrate class.
- [[Nucleotide Excision Repair]] — the complementary pathway for bulky helix-distorting
  lesions; AAG handles small chemical modifications that do not grossly distort the
  duplex and therefore do not trigger NER.
- [[OGG1]] — another HhH superfamily glycosylase acting on an oxidatively generated
  base (8-oxoG); the HhH fold and base-flipping mechanism are directly analogous.
- [[Malondialdehyde]] — the dialdehyde that generates M1dG and related exocyclic
  adducts; it is also an alkylating-weak electrophile whose products can fall to AAG,
  which is the mechanistic overlap with [[M₁dG]].
- [[Cyclophosphamide]] — an alkylating chemotherapeutic whose lesions are repaired
  largely by AAG and MGMT, so AAG status modulates its cytotoxicity.
