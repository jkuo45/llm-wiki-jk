# Thermic Effect of Food

**The Thermic Effect of Food (TEF)**, or diet-induced thermogenesis, is the increase in [[Energy Expenditure|energy expenditure]] above [[Resting Metabolic Rate|resting levels]] that follows ingestion of a meal, reflecting the energy cost of digestion, absorption, transport, and storage of nutrients. Together with resting metabolism and physical activity (including [[Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis|NEAT]]), TEF is one of the three canonical components of the daily energy budget (Blaxter, 1989).

## Determinants

- **Meal size and composition** — TEF is proportional to energy intake; protein elicits a higher thermic response than carbohydrate or fat.
- **Protein content of the diet** — the heat increment of feeding tracks dietary protein, but there is no evidence that protein intakes declined disproportionately as total intake fell in Western populations (Willett & Leibel, 2002).

> [!info] Source: [[_document_ - speakman2003_Resting Metabolic Rate|Speakman & Selman (2003)]]
> Speakman & Selman argue that TEF cannot explain the obesity epidemic: because it depends on food intake, a decline in expenditure via this route would require a decline in intake — which has not occurred. Its contribution to the energy budget is therefore relatively fixed, leaving physical activity as the variable expenditure component.

## Clinical Relevance

TEF is included in predictive energy-requirement equations and is one reason fixed physical-activity multipliers (e.g., ×1·2–1·9 for varying PAL) approximate daily needs. Small but systematic differences in TEF between individuals contribute to the residual variance in [[Energy Balance|energy balance]] and weight-gain susceptibility.
