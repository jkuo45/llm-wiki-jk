# Sarcoptes scabiei

**Sarcoptes scabiei** is a parasitic astigmatid mite, roughly 0.3–0.4 mm, causing the disease [[Scabies]]. Different host-adapted varieties (varieties or subspecies) infest different mammals — *S. s.* var. *hominis* in humans, *S. s.* var. *canis* in dogs, *var. nyctereutis* in raccoon dogs — and cross-species transmission occurs but is usually self-limiting, except for *var. canis* in immunocompromised or crusted cases.

## Life cycle and transmission

The stages are egg, larva, protonymph, tritonymph and adult. Adults mate in a burrow on the skin surface; the fertilised female then burrows into the stratum corneum, leaving a serpiginous tunnel behind her and laying roughly 2–3 eggs per day for the 2–3 weeks of her life. Larvae hatch in about 3–4 days, pass through two nymphal stages in the burrow or on the surface, and mature to adults over about 10–15 days. Adult males and immature females die in or near the burrow; only the gravid female is permanently burrowed.

> [!info] Asymptomatic transmission
> Roughly one third of close contacts of an index case carry mites while remaining symptom-free, which is the main reason crusted scabies and outbreaks in care homes, prisons, refugee camps and shelters persist despite effective acaricides. Symptomatic disease develops when mite salivary and faecal antigens provoke a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction — which is why the incubation period for a first infestation is 2–6 weeks, and only 1–4 days on re-exposure to previously encountered mite.

## Pathogenesis

The mite itself eats dissolved tissue debris, so physical damage to the epidermis is small. Disease comes from host response:

- **Type I hypersensitivity** to salivary antigens and to faecal enzymes is responsible for the itch and for the papular/vesicular rash.
- **Type IV delayed hypersensitivity** contributes to the nodular and eczematous lesions seen in crusted and post-scabetic states.
- Secondary **bacterial superinfection**, most often with *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*, is the main complication; impetiginisation and, in infants, glomerulonephritis complicate epidemics.
- **Crusted (Norwegian) scabies** occurs in immunocompromised, elderly, debilitated or Down syndrome patients, where the host itch response is blunted. Burrows are replaced by hyperkeratotic crusts harbouring millions of mites, making the patient a reservoir.

## Immunology and immune evasion

The mite genome encodes a multigene family of at least 24 homologs of a serine protease allergen, **Sm-RI**-type proteins, of which all but one are predicted to be catalytically dead because of mutations in the catalytic triad. The working hypothesis (Holt et al. 2003) is that these are functionally inert decoys that absorb and neutralise host antibodies — a molecular form of antigenic camouflage. Other confirmed immune-evasion strategies include limited enzymatic repertoire in the mite's own digestion, and IgE-binding proteins such as SmSPI and SmD3.

Serum IgE and IgG to mite extracts are near-universal in chronic infestation, and the current diagnostic workhorse is recombinant Sm-RI allergen IgE ELISA, which is far more specific than microscopy of skin scrapings. Transcriptome-driven efforts are also building lateral-flow assays against conserved mite proteins (e.g. a vitellogenin-like protein) to replace scraping, which has well under 50% sensitivity in practice.

## Treatment and control

- **First-line acaricides**: topical permethrin 5% or 1% applied to the whole body and left for 8–14 h, repeated at 7 days; **ivermectin** 200 µg/kg orally as a single dose, repeated at 7 days.
- **Second-line**: crotamiton, sulfur, lindane (rarely, due to toxicity), and topical [[Selamectin]] (used systemically in dogs for *var. canis* and *var. cuniculi*, and explored in human crusted scabies).
- **Crusted scabies** requires repeated ivermectin plus keratolytics plus topical permethrin, often for weeks to months.
- **Adjuncts**: topical or oral corticosteroids, antihistamines, and antibiotics for secondary infection. Oral steroids are a recognised driver of crusted scabies and should be avoided unless essential.
- **Contact and fomite measures**: treat all household contacts, contacts of contacts, and all healthcare workers with exposure; wash or bag bedding and clothing at ≥60 °C, or seal for 72 h (mites survive about 2–3 days off host).

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Bites persist for weeks after successful acaricide treatment, because the reaction is to residual antigen, not to a live mite. Re-treatment should be based on new burrows and new lesions, not on itch resolution — treating on itch alone is a common route to incomplete eradication.

- [[Ivermectin]] — Ivermectin is the main oral acaricide for human scabies and the reference point for understanding how a macrocyclic lactone kills an ectoparasite. Its role here is antiparasitic, entirely separate from any supposed anti-cancer or autophagy effect.
- [[Inflammation]] — Scabies is a pure inflammation-of-immunity example: the inflammatory infiltrate is what produces the itch, and the few people who cannot mount it (crusted scabies) are the ones with the worst infestation. This is an unusually clean illustration of inflammation as host defence, not disease in itself.
- [[CD4+ T Cells]] — The balance between Th2-biased IgE responses to mite allergen and the IgG responses associated with clearance is usually framed in terms of which CD4 T cell program dominates; chronic infestation shifts toward a Th2/IgE-skewed profile, which is why atopy is discussed as a modifier of scabies severity.
- [[Eosinophils]] — Mite faecal and salivary allergens drive an immediate-type reaction whose eosinophil-rich infiltrate is the histologic signature of an ordinary scabetic papule.
