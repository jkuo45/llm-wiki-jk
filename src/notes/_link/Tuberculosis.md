# Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis is a chronic infectious disease caused by the bacterium *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, which infects primarily the lungs but can involve any organ. It remains one of the top ten causes of death worldwide and the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. Its pathology is unusual: the majority of exposed people carry latent infection with no symptoms, and disease arises only when containment fails.

> [!info] Latency is the defining feature
> Approximately a quarter of the global population is infected, and only 5–10% of those ever develop active disease — though that fraction rises sharply with immunosuppression, malnutrition, diabetes, silicosis, HIV, tobacco exposure and anti-TNF therapy. The latent state is a dynamic equilibrium between bacterial persistence and host immune control, not sterility.

## Pathogenesis

1. **Aerosol transmission.** Droplet nuclei from a person with pulmonary or laryngeal disease are inhaled and reach the alveolar compartment. There is no person-to-person spread through surfaces; transmission follows close, prolonged indoor exposure.
2. **Alveolar macrophage entry.** The bacillus is phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages and, remarkably, survives *inside* them, blocking the phagosome–lysosome fusion that would kill it.
3. **Granuloma formation.** Two to three weeks after infection, cell-mediated immunity — specifically, [[TNFα|TNF]]-dependent granuloma formation driven by [[Interferon|IFN-γ]] from T cells and macrophages — walls the infected region off. The granuloma is the lesion of both pathology and containment: it houses the bacilli and, on breakdown, produces the cavitary disease that makes the patient infectious.
4. **Latency or progression.** If the granuloma holds, the bacilli persist in a low-metabolic, walled state for years or decades. If it fails, subclinical disease and then active cavitary disease follow.
5. **Caseous necrosis.** Breakdown of the granuloma centre produces the cheese-like necrosis seen grossly and radiographically, and the erosion of airways that discharges bacilli into the airways and makes the patient infectious.

> [!tip] The macrophage is the battleground
> Reviews of TB immunopathogenesis consistently frame outcome as a macrophage-centred contest: how quickly infected macrophages are killed, recruited, and organised determines whether the granuloma is protective or permissive. This is the same dichotomy already documented in the vault's granulomatous inflammation notes.

## Clinical Presentation

Pulmonary TB is chronic cough with haemoptysis, night sweats, low-grade fever, fatigue and weight loss, with chest imaging showing upper-lobe infiltrates and cavitation. Extrapulmonary disease — lymph node, pleural, bone and joint, genitourinary, meningeal, and disseminated/miliary forms — occurs in a substantial minority, and the presentation is frequently non-specific. Meningeal TB carries high mortality.

## Treatment and Prevention

Active pulmonary TB requires at least two months of a four-drug intensive phase — isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol — followed by a continuation phase; newer shorter regimens are now recommended in eligible patients. Latent infection is treated with far lighter regimens, including three months of isoniazid plus rifapentine. Drug toxicity is substantial: hepatotoxicity from isoniazid, rifampicin and pyrazinamide; optic neuritis from ethambutol; and a flu-like syndrome plus hepatotoxicity from rifampicin, whose enzyme induction also causes widespread drug interactions. Pyrazinamide is also implicated in a characteristic hyperuricaemia.

[[BCG]] remains the only licensed vaccine, based on attenuated *Mycobacterium bovis*. Its variable and inconsistent protection against adult pulmonary disease, and the logistical constraints that keep it out of general use in many countries, reflect both its limited efficacy and the risk of disseminated BCG infection in immunocompromised vaccinees.

> [!warning] Resistance is now a defining feature
> Multidrug-resistant TB (resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin) and extensively drug-resistant TB are spreading in several regions, driven by inadequate regimens, poor adherence and programmatic gaps. Treatment becomes longer, more toxic and markedly more expensive. Any TB control claim now has to be stated alongside its resistance surveillance data.

## Connections

- [[TNFα]] — the indispensable cytokine for granuloma formation and maintenance; its blockade by anti-TNF biologics is the strongest known iatrogenic driver of reactivation and disseminated disease.
- [[BCG]] — the live attenuated vaccine strain of *M. bovis* and one of the earliest demonstrations that deliberate infection generates protective immunity.
- [[Interferon]] — IFN-γ from T cells and natural killer cells is required to activate macrophage killing of intracellular bacilli.
- [[Cytokines]] — the signalling network through which the granuloma is assembled and maintained.
- [[Inflammation]] — the process granulomatous TB instantiates, with the chronicity and tissue destruction characteristic of type 4 hypersensitivity.
- [[Nitric Oxide]] — a macrophage-generated bactericidal effector against intracellular mycobacteria, induced by IFN-γ and constrained by host iron availability.
- [[Matrix Metalloproteinase]] — secreted by infected macrophages to degrade host matrix and remodel tissue, and a driver of the cavitation that makes TB transmissible.
- [[Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease]] — shares risk factors (smoking, biomass exposure, prior lung damage) with TB, so the two are frequently confounded and can coexist.
- [[Aging]] — shifts the balance toward reactivation, and is one reason TB incidence rises in older populations even as global incidence falls.
- [[Neglected Tropical Diseases]] — TB sits within the wider WHO portfolio of high-burden infectious diseases of the poor, though it is not itself formally an NTD.
- [[Epigenetics]] — host chromatin state and mycobacterial effectors both modify the host epigenome, contributing to latency and to the transcriptional reprogramming of infected macrophages.
- [[Autophagy]] — xenophagy of mycobacteria is a genuine host restriction mechanism, and *M. tuberculosis* actively blocks it via effectors such as KIF5C and the ESX-1 system.
- [[Cancer]] — chronic inflammatory signalling from TB has historically been linked to malignancy risk, and TB granulomas share their architecture with sarcoid and fungal granulomas.
