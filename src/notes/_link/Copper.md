# Copper

**Copper** is an third-row transition metal with two accessible oxidation
states (Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺) separated by a single electron, which is precisely the
property that makes it both an indispensable enzyme cofactor and a
catalytically efficient generator of free radicals. The body handles it
without any storage organ: there is no dedicated copper reservoir, plasma
copper is carried essentially entirely by [[Ceruloplasmin]], and absorption
and biliary excretion are tightly matched.

## Enzymatic roles

Copper is required by a small number of enzyme families whose reaction
chemistry all depend on the Cu(I)/Cu(II) redox couple cycling rapidly.

> [!info] The cuproenzymes
> - **Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV)** — the terminal step of the
>   [[Electron Transport Chain]]; copper and iron alternate in the catalytic
>   centre, with Cu(I) binding and delivering electrons to dioxygen to make
>   water. The source of the nomenclature confusion with the
>   [[_link/Silver|colour metal]] notwithstanding, this is the
>   best-characterised copper function in physiology and the one that makes
>   copper deficiency a mitochondrial disease.
> - **Superoxide dismutase (SOD1)** — the Cu/Zn form catalyses
>   2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. Copper is the redox-active centre;
>   the [[Zinc]] is structural. Loss of SOD1 catalytic copper is a
>   distinct pathological entity from loss of the protein (see ALS below).
> - **Ceruloplasmin** — a ferroxidase that oxidises Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ for
>   [[Transferrin]] loading, and is the major plasma copper pool.
> - **Lysyl oxidase** — requires copper for the oxidative deamination step in
>   collagen and elastin cross-linking; this is why copper deficiency
>   produces vascular and connective-tissue phenotypes.
> - **Tyrosinase** — the copper enzyme of melanin synthesis; the
>   [[Melanin|melanin]] story in this vault touches it directly.

## Redox chemistry: the Haber-Weiss reaction

> [!info] Copper as a Fenton catalyst
> Free Cu⁺ reacts with [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] at a rate constant high enough to
> be diffusion-limited, generating hydroxyl radical:
> Cu⁺ + H₂O₂ → Cu²⁺ + •OH + OH⁻.
> Copper is more catalytically potent than iron for this reaction — the
> Fenton constants for Cu(I)/H₂O₂ are roughly an order of magnitude above
> those for Fe(II)/H₂O₂, and unlike Fe²⁺ the Cu(II)/Cu(I) couple is not
> rate-limited by the slow H₂O₂-independent return reaction. This is why
> copper chelation, not just iron chelation, matters in models of
> oxidative injury.
>
> Combined with superoxide dismutation, this closes the
> **Haber-Weiss cycle**: SOD1 converts O₂•⁻ to H₂O₂, and the resulting H₂O₂
> drives Fenton chemistry on any available transition metal. In the
> vault's adrenochrome thread, this is the mechanistic bridge between
> copper release and the [[Adrenochrome]] quinone one-electron cycling
> documented in [[Chelating agents]] — Fe²⁺ and Cu⁺ are the two catalysts
> that make catecholamine autoxidation proceed.

> [!warning] Labile copper, not total copper
> The relevant quantity in oxidative stress is *labile* copper — the
> exchangeable, non-protein-bound pool measured by [[EPR spectroscopy]] as a
> paramagnetic Cu(II) signal. The overwhelming majority of intracellular
> copper is protein-bound and redox-inert. Claims that copper chelation
> rescues oxidative injury must be read against the total-copper
> concentration, which is not the same measurement and is generally not
> elevated in chronic inflammatory disease.

## Homeostasis, deficiency, and excess

Absorption occurs in the duodenum via CTR1, with copper routed to the
trans-Golgi network and loaded onto copper chaperones — Atx1 and CCS for
SOD1 — for delivery. Copper is excreted almost entirely through the
hepatobiliary route, and this is why cholestatic liver disease, which blocks
biliary copper excretion, causes copper accumulation in the brain in
[[Wilson's Disease]].

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> The genetic and acquired copper disorders are easier to confuse than they
> look. **Menkes disease** (ATP7A loss) causes copper malabsorption with
> profound deficiency, neurodegeneration, kinky hair, and failure to thrive.
> **Wilson's disease** (ATP7B loss) causes impaired biliary copper
> excretion, hepatic and basal ganglia accumulation, and is treated with
> penicillamine, trientine, or zinc salts. **Idiopathic copper toxicosis**
> resembles Wilson's in adults. In acquired disease, the opposite situation
> dominates: the most common cause of copper *excess* in chronic illness is
> long-term parenteral or high-dose supplementation, and chronic
> inflammatory states raise ceruloplasmin (an acute-phase reactant) enough to
> inflate measured serum copper without any change in tissue loading.
>
> Serum copper therefore correlates poorly with copper status in
> inflammation, and should be interpreted with ceruloplasmin and, when
> relevant, 24-hour urinary copper rather than in isolation. In
> [[Alzheimer's Disease]], the copper-binding domain of [[APP]] and the
> interaction of Cu²⁺ with [[Amyloid Beta]] are real but mechanistically
> contested claims; clinical trials of copper supplementation or chelation
> in AD have not produced a clear signal.

## Documents

- [[APP]] — APP carries a Cu²⁺-binding domain; the amyloidogenic processing
  of APP and copper binding are linked in the Alzheimer's literature.
- [[Chelating agents]] — copper is one of the two transition metals identified
  as catalysts of catecholamine oxidation to adrenochrome; chelation slows
  the rate, which is the therapeutic logic in that note.
- [[EPR spectroscopy]] — the standard readout of the labile copper pool, whose
  paramagnetic signal is diagnostic of Cu(II) in biological samples.

## Connections

- [[Ceruloplasmin]] — carries 90–95% of plasma copper and is the ferroxidase
  that couples copper to iron handling; its induction as an acute-phase
  reactant is the reason serum copper is unreliable as a status marker in
  [[Inflammation]].
- [[Zinc]] — zinc is the structural partner of copper in SOD1, and zinc and
  copper compete for the same intestinal and hepatic transporters; the
  [[Zinc]] note's balance discussion and copper's are the same conversation.
- [[Superoxide Dismutase]] — SOD1 requires copper for catalysis and zinc for
  structure; loss of the copper specifically produces a catalytically dead
  but correctly folded protein, which is the mechanistic substrate of
  ALS-associated SOD1 toxicity.
- [[Adrenochrome]] — Cu⁺ catalyses the one-electron oxidation of
  adrenaline-quinone to adrenochrome, linking copper availability to the
  adrenochrome hypothesis of [[Schizophrenia]] and to the quinone-cycling
  chemistry in [[Chelating agents]].
- [[EPR spectroscopy]] — the technique that detects the paramagnetic
  Cu(II) signal in biological samples; the same unpaired-electron chemistry
  that makes copper useful as a cofactor makes it a spin trap-adjacent
  redox hazard.
- [[Hydrogen Peroxide]] — the substrate for copper-catalysed Fenton
  chemistry; together with superoxide dismutation, this closes the Haber-Weiss
  cycle that makes copper redox cycling more potent than iron's.
- [[Amyloid Beta]] — Cu²⁺ coordination promotes Aβ oligomerisation; the
  interaction is real in vitro and the therapeutic extrapolation is not
  established.
- [[APP]] — the Cu²⁺-binding domain links copper to amyloidogenic secretase
  processing, and the [[Melanin]]/[[Tyrosinase]] copper site is the
  mechanistic ancestor of the APP site.
- [[Transferrin]] — iron loading onto transferrin requires
  ceruloplasmin-mediated ferroxidation, so copper deficiency causes
  functional iron deficiency with iron-low indices — the link between
  copper status and anaemia.
