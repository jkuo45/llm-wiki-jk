# L-Carnitine

L-Carnitine (levocarnitine) is the naturally occurring, biologically active L-enantiomer of [[Carnitine]]. It is a zwitterionic quaternary ammonium compound synthesized endogenously from the amino acids [[Lysine]] and [[Methionine]] and obtained from the diet (notably red meat). Its principal role is the transport of long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane into the matrix for [[Fatty acid oxidation]] via the carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT) system.

## Biosynthesis and Physiology

- **Synthesis**: Produced mainly in the liver and kidneys from [[Lysine]] and [[Methionine]], requiring vitamin C and iron-dependent hydroxylases; mature [[Mitochondria]] cannot synthesize it de novo.
- **Transport**: The carnitine/acylcarnitine translocase exchanges cytosolic acylcarnitine for intramitochondrial carnitine, coupling fatty-acid entry to energy production.
- **Whole-body pools**: Skeletal and cardiac muscle, which have high oxidative demands, retain the bulk of body carnitine.

## Functional Role

L-Carnitine buffers the  ratio of free CoA to acyl-CoA, preventing the accumulation of toxic acyl intermediates and sustaining [[Mitochondria]]l β-oxidation, ketone production, and ATP generation. Clinically it is used to treat primary and secondary carnitine-deficiency states and is investigated for cardiovascular, renal, and exercise-performance indications.

## Relationship to Acetyl-L-Carnitine

The acetylated derivative [[Acetyl-L-Carnitine]] (ALC) shares L-carnitine's mitochondrial fatty-acid transport function while additionally supplying an acetyl group for energy metabolism and acetylcholine synthesis. ALC is studied as an anti-aging and glycation-countering agent, grouping it with [[Carnosine]] and [[Aminoguanidine]] as compounds reported to help prevent protein [[Cross-linking]].
