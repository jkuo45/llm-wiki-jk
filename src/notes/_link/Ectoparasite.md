# Ectoparasite

An **ectoparasite** is an organism that inhabits the skin or its outgrowths on another organism, using the host for sustenance, maturation and reproduction. PAHO/WHO notes that the term is used more narrowly than "blood-feeding arthropod" to mean parasites that attach or burrow into the skin and remain there for relatively long periods — ticks, fleas, lice, parasitic flies and mites — rather than mosquitoes and other temporary biters.

> [!info] The defining clinical property
> Parasite–host interaction is confined to the upper layers of the skin. This is what makes the group tractable (topical agents work; systemic drugs often do not need to reach deep tissue), and also what makes it so damaging: the skin barrier is the thing being breached, so the dominant morbidity is not the parasite itself but **secondary bacterial infection, sleep-disrupting pruritus, and disfigurement**.

Scabies and pediculosis are the two conditions in this vault, and they are linked by more than taxonomy: both are epidermal parasitic skin diseases, both are stigmatised, both are strongly associated with overcrowding and poverty, and both respond poorly to single-patient treatment unless the whole household or community is treated simultaneously.

## Transmission and burden

- **Scabies** spreads by prolonged direct skin-to-skin contact, with fomite transmission possible under specific conditions. Institutional and community outbreaks are well documented, particularly in care homes and refugee settings.
- **Pediculosis** spreads by direct person-to-person contact and by fomites (combs, clothing, bedding). Head lice are predominantly a paediatric problem; pubic lice are sexually transmitted and are often a marker of other sexually transmitted infections, so they should prompt STI screening.

> [!info] Burden is not proportional to mite number
> Ordinary scabies carries a low parasite burden — an average of 10–12 mites over the first three months of infection. Neglected children in under-resourced communities may carry hundreds, and **crusted (Norwegian) scabies** can carry millions of mites. This has direct clinical consequences: crusted scabies is far more contagious, more difficult to treat (the hyperkeratotic crust is a barrier to topical penetration, requiring prolonged combination therapy), and is strongly associated with immunosuppression, institutional crowding, and — notably — with HTLV-1 infection.

## Pathology and complications

The primary lesion is the mite bite, the burrow, and the host immune response to both. Pruritus is the dominant symptom and is the main driver of morbidity. Complications:

- **Secondary bacterial infection** — impetigo from *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*, cellulitis, and in resource-limited settings post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. This is the main route by which scabies kills.
- **Crusted scabies** — as above.
- **Delousing-related complications** — in body lice, bacteraemia, anaemia, and rickettsial infection.
- **Psychosocial burden** — sleep deprivation, school absence, and substantial stigma. In the case of ectoparasites, stigma is a clinical variable, not just a social one: it drives delayed presentation and continued transmission.

## Treatment and control

- **Scabies:** topical permethrin 5% applied to the whole body, or oral [[Ivermectin]]; the two are not interchangeable in all settings. Treatment must cover all household contacts and be repeated at 7–14 days because the drugs do not reliably kill eggs.
- **Pediculosis:** wet combing (mechanical removal) plus pediculicides — [[Permethrin]], malathion, pyrethrins, or lindane. **Resistance is a documented and growing problem**, which is why the combination of mechanical and chemical approaches is preferred in current guidance.
- For livestock, macrocyclic lactones such as [[Selamectin]] and [[Milbemycin Oxime]] are used; [[Moxidectin]] is used in both veterinary and (in a key-advantage setting) human onchocerciasis programmes.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Diagnosis in low-prevalence settings should be confirmed before treatment, since the differential for pruritus without visible infestation is broad and empiric treatment of the whole household is disruptive. The "itchy spots at the waist, wrists and axillae" distribution is characteristic but not diagnostic. In children, the differential also includes atopic dermatitis, insect-bite hypersensitivity, and papular urticaria. Antibiotic treatment of secondary infection does not treat the infestation, and recurrence after apparently successful treatment almost always means reinfestation or incomplete household treatment, not drug failure.

## Connections

- [[Scabies]] — the archetypal ectoparasitosis: burrowing within the stratum corneum, household transmission, and morbidity mediated almost entirely by secondary infection rather than by the parasite.
- [[Sarcoptes scabiei]] — the organism itself. Its short life cycle on the host (roughly 10–14 days from egg to adult) is what makes the whole transmission chain so fast and why treatment intervals must account for eggs.
- [[Pediculosis]] — the other major ectoparasitism in this vault, sharing household-transmission dynamics and stigma but differing in organism, topography, and the growing problem of acaricide resistance.
- [[Ivermectin]] — the key systemic ectoparasiticide, working by opening glutamate-gated chloride channels in the parasite; its oral efficacy against scabies and onchocerciasis transformed the field, and the local absence of parallel mammalian toxicity is itself a useful pharmacological fact.
- [[Neglected Tropical Diseases]] — the policy and funding frame. Ectoparasitoses are NTDs precisely because they are common, disabling and stigmatising while remaining commercially unattractive, which is why mass drug administration and community-wide treatment drive most progress.
- [[Wolbachia]] — an obligate intracellular endosymbiont of *Brugia malayi* and *Onchocerca volvulus*. Wolbachia depletion with doxycycline kills the adult worms, an example of an ectoparasite-pathogen partnership being itself a drug target.
- [[Anaplasmosis]] and [[Babesiosis]] — tick-borne infections, the reminder that the same ectoparasites that cause persistent infestations are vectors for acute systemic disease.
- [[Pruritus]] — the symptom that dominates the clinical picture in both scabies and pediculosis and the main reason these conditions cause morbidity at their very high prevalences.
