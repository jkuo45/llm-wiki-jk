# Hydrocele

A hydrocele is a collection of serous fluid within a normally potential space — most often the **tunica vaginalis**, the peritoneal-derived sac surrounding the testis, which in a normal adult contains only a few millilitres of fluid. The defining pathophysiology is not excess production but **imbalance between fluid secretion and absorption**: the secretory surface is a mesothelial lining whose rate of fluid production is normally exactly matched by lymphatic and venous drainage. Anything that raises production or impairs absorption produces a hydrocele, and the mechanism determines whether the sac can still reabside fluid spontaneously.

> [!info] Classification by mechanism
> - **Communicating**: fluid tracks between the peritoneal cavity and the tunica vaginalis through a patent processus vaginalis. Common in children (a developmental remnant) and in adults after a vasectomy or inguinal surgery. Usually self-limiting.
> - **Non-communicating (encapsulated)**: the processus is closed and the sac has lost its drainage capacity. This is the adult filarial and idiopathic pattern.
> - **Filarial**: a distinct pathophysiology again — see below.

> [!info] Filarial hydrocele
> *Wuchereria bancrofti* is by far the most common cause of adult hydrocele worldwide, affecting populations across more than 50 countries and estimated to affect tens of millions of men. Adult worms live 6–8 years in the lymphatic vessels, obstructing them and producing chronic inflammation with fibrosis. Three processes combine:
> 1. **Lymphatic obstruction** from the worms themselves and from the host granulomatous response, blocking the drainage the sac depends on.
> 2. **Directly thickened, fibrotic tunica vaginalis** — the parietal layer becomes so opaque and thickened that even after the worms die it no longer reabsorbs fluid. This is why a chronic filarial hydrocele does not resolve spontaneously once the infection is cleared.
> 3. **Elongation of the spermatic cord** in longstanding disease.
>
> Hydrocele is the commonest clinical manifestation of bancroftian filariasis; in endemic regions a large fraction of adult men are affected. The WHO baseline estimate was 25 million men with hydrocele and over 15 million people with lymphoedema, with at least 36 million people left with these chronic, disfiguring manifestations. The harm is disproportionately social: scrotal swelling carries stigma, affects marriage and employment, and imposes a substantial economic burden.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> - A hydrocele is **benign but must not be assumed benign**. It should be evaluated rather than transilluminated and forgotten, because a surrounding scrotal or testicular mass is not excluded by a hydrocele appearance. Ultrasound is the correct test.
> - Management depends on the mechanism, and this is where errors are made. Adult communicating hydroceles and those with a thick, scarred tunica need surgical repair (eversion or excision of the tunica, or a Lord procedure); aspiration alone has a high recurrence rate. Filarial hydrocele is additionally treated with [[Diethylcarbamazine]] or [[Ivermectin]] under mass drug administration, but the hydrocele itself often still needs surgery because of the scarring.
> - The differential is geography-dependent. In the United States, hydrocele is "virtually nonexistent" as a filarial cause and iatrogenic causes (post-vasectomy, post-hernia repair), infection, and neoplasia predominate.
> - Sperm sampling is worth noting: the great majority of men with filarial hydrocele remain fertile, so hydrocele should not be conflated with infertility.

## Connections

- [[Lymphatic Filariasis]] — the vault's inbound document link and the disease context in which hydrocele matters at population scale. WHO classifies hydrocele as one of the two chronic manifestations of lymphatic filariasis, alongside lymphoedema.
- [[Wuchereria bancrofti]] — the cause in the large majority of tropical adult cases; its 6–8 year adult lifespan is what makes the disease chronic and the hydrocele chronic.
- [[Microfilariae]] — the diagnostic target; the nocturnal periodicity of *W. bancrofti* microfilaraemia is what makes blood films optimally timed, and it is the basis of the "night blood" method.
- [[Brugia malayi]] and [[Onchocerca volvulus]] — the other filarial nematodes. *B. malayi* is a less common cause of filarial hydrocele; *Onchocerca* causes nodules and skin disease rather than hydrocele, and is a useful contrast.
- [[Lymphedema]] and [[Elephantiasis]] — the sibling chronic manifestation of lymphatic filariasis, from the same obstruction mechanism. The distinction is anatomical: lymphoedema is extremity tissue swelling, hydrocele is a discrete sac.
- [[Diethylcarbamazine]], [[Ivermectin]], [[Albendazole]] and [[Moxidectin]] — the antihelminthic arm of management, delivered through mass drug administration programmes; the vault's ivermectin note covers the mass-administration pharmacology.
- Dirofilaria and other nematode tissue migration — a non-filarial differential for chronic scrotal swelling, and a reminder that [[Helminth]] disease presenting in the scrotum is a described pattern beyond the filariae.
- [[Scabies]] and [[Pediculosis]] — the other chronic, stigmatised, socially disabling infestations the vault tracks; together they make the case that disability-adjusted burden, not mortality, is the metric that matters in neglected tropical disease.
