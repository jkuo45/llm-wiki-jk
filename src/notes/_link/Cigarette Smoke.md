## Overview

Cigarette smoke is an environmentally delivered mixture of thousands of chemicals, including a substantial burden of gaseous and particulate oxidants and [[Free Radicals]] (semiquinone radicals, hydroxyl radicals, nitric oxide). It is a major exogenous driver of oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, particularly in the lung, and it disrupts protective sirtuin–transcription-factor interactions that normally restrain [[NF-κB]]–mediated inflammation.

## Mechanism / Structure

> [!info] Redox-cycling radicals
> Tar-phase semiquinones can redox-cycle and generate sustained [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] long after the inhalational event. The oxidant effect therefore outlasts the acute exposure, producing a chronic, self-perpetuating burden on tissue antioxidant defenses.

The mechanistic link between smoke and inflammation operates at the level of protein–protein regulation:

> [!warning] Smoke disrupts the SIRT1–RelA/p65 complex
> Exposure to cigarette-smoke extract disrupts the [[SIRT1]]–RelA/p65 interaction, diminishing deacetylation of the [[NF-κB]] subunit RelA. With RelA left in a more acetylated, transcriptionally active state, expression of inflammatory mediators rises in airway and other smoke-exposed cells. This provides a direct mechanistic bridge between smoking, impaired sirtuin function, and chronic inflammatory lung disease.

## Role in Oxidative Stress and Inflammation

By both directly delivering oxidants and indirectly disabling sirtuin counter-regulation, cigarette smoke pushes cells toward a pro-oxidant, pro-inflammatory phenotype. The loss of SIRT1-mediated restraint on NF-κB mirrors patterns seen in [[Aging]] and metabolic stress, where declining sirtuin activity permits low-grade inflammation ("inflammaging"). Smoke thus accelerates a process that overlaps with age-related inflammatory reprogramming.

## Clinical & Research Significance

The SIRT1–NF-κB disruption model helps explain why smokers are prone to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, atherosclerosis, and other inflammatory conditions, and it offers a molecular rationale for antioxidant and sirtuin-activating interventions. Reducing exposure remains primary, but understanding the specific sirtuin-axis impairment identifies potential adjunctive therapeutic targets.
