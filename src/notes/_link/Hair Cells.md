# Hair Cells

**Hair cells** are the mechanosensory receptor cells of the inner ear, located in the organ of Corti of the [[Cochlea|cochlea]] (auditory hair cells) and in the vestibular organs (vestibular hair cells). Each cell bears an apical bundle of stereocilia; sound-induced deflection opens mechanotransduction channels, depolarizing the cell and triggering neurotransmitter release onto auditory nerve fibers. Mammalian cochlear hair cells are terminally differentiated and cannot regenerate, so their loss — driven by cumulative [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], noise trauma, ototoxins, and aging — is irreversible and is the cellular basis of age-related hearing loss (AHL).

## SIRT3-dependent protection under caloric restriction

[[Caloric Restriction|Caloric restriction (CR)]] alleviates [[Age-related Hearing Loss|AHL]] by reducing oxidative damage in cochlear cells, and this protective effect is abolished in [[SIRT3]]-deficient mice[350],[351]. Mechanistically, SIRT3 enhances [[IDH2]] activity under CR, boosting mitochondrial [[NADPH]] production and facilitating efficient [[ROS]] clearance — a mitochondrial antioxidant defense mechanism crucial for preserving cochlear hair cell integrity and delaying hearing loss progression[352],[353]. The hair cell is therefore a primary beneficiary of the SIRT3–IDH2–NADPH axis in the auditory system.

## Clinical relevance

Because hair cell loss is permanent, strategies that preserve hair cell redox homeostasis — such as SIRT3 activation — are promising for delaying AHL onset. SIRT3 has been proposed as both a therapeutic target and a candidate biomarker for AHL susceptibility.
