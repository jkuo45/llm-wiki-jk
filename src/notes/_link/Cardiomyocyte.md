# Cardiomyocyte

**Overview:** [[Cardiomyocyte|Cardiomyocytes]] are the striated, terminally differentiated muscle cells of the [[Myocardium]] that generate the contractile force of the heart. They are among the most energetically demanding cells in the body and are largely post-mitotic in adults, with limited regenerative capacity.

**Structure and Organization:** Each cardiomyocyte contains a single central nucleus (or occasionally two), densely packed [[Mitochondria]] (≈25–35% of cell volume), and a highly organized sarcomere of actin and myosin filaments producing the striated appearance. Intercalated discs bearing gap junctions ([[Connexin|connexins]]) and desmosomes electrically and mechanically couple cells into a functional syncytium. Excitation–contraction coupling relies on [[Calcium]] flux through ryanodine receptors and the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

**Physiological Function:** Depolarization opens L-type calcium channels, triggering calcium-induced calcium release and myosin–actin cross-bridge cycling that ejects blood. Sustained ATP (from oxidative phosphorylation) is essential; cardiomyocytes are exquisitely sensitive to [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischemia]]. They respond to adrenergic tone via [[Beta-Oxidation|β-adrenergic]] signaling that modulates contractility and heart rate.

**Pathology and Clinical Relevance:** Injury or overload drives [[Cardiomyopathy]], [[Cardiac Hypertrophy]], and ultimately [[Heart Failure]]. Oxidative and metabolic stress, [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], and [[Autophagy]] dysregulation contribute to cell loss. Cardiomyocyte senescence and death are central to aging-related cardiac decline; cardioprotective strategies ([[Rapamycin]], [[Caloric Restriction]], [[Urolithin A|urolithin A]], [[Creatine]]) target mitochondrial quality and stress resistance.
