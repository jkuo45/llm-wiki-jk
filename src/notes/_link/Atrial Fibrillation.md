# Atrial Fibrillation

**Atrial fibrillation** is a supraventricular tachyarrhythmia in which the atria
depolarize chaotically without coordinated contraction, producing an irregularly
irregular ventricular response. It is the most common sustained arrhythmia, affecting
roughly 1–2% of the adult population in high-income countries and rising steeply with
age — under 0.1% below 50, around 4% at 60–70, and 14% or more over 80. Prevalence
grows with each successive decade, which makes AF a cardinal disease of [[Aging]].

## Classification and natural history

- **Paroxysmal** — self-terminating episodes under 7 days.
- **Persistent** — beyond 7 days, or requiring intervention to terminate.
- **Long-standing persistent** — beyond 12 months.
- **Permanent** — accepted as non-revertible.

It often begins as brief paroxysms, or as [[Atrial Flutter|atrial flutter]], and
progresses toward persistence as the atrium remodels. Post-ablation recurrence is
common, which is why rhythm-control interventions are applied to symptomatic patients
rather than prophylactically.

## Mechanism

> [!info] Triggers and substrate
> AF requires both a **trigger** and a **substrate**. The dominant triggers are rapid
> focal activity in the muscle sleeves of the **pulmonary veins**; the dominant substrate
> is atrial structural and electrical remodeling. Catheter isolation of the pulmonary
> veins can restore sinus rhythm precisely because it removes the trigger, and
> ganglionated plexi and the ligament of Marshall can be additional sources.

The remodeled substrate is characterized by:

- **Atrial fibrosis** — activated fibroblasts deposit collagen, producing conduction
  heterogeneity and slow conduction.
- **Atrial dilatation** — stretching the myocardium shortens action potential duration
  and effective refractory period, favoring re-entry.
- **Altered ion currents** — reduced L-type calcium current, increased K+ currents
  (IKs), changes in Na+ channel availability, and increased ryanodine-receptor/SR
  calcium leak producing afterdepolarizations.
- **Inflammation and oxidative stress** — elevated [[C-reactive protein|hs-CRP]] and
  other inflammatory markers, with Mendelian-randomization evidence that inflammation is
  *causally upstream* of AF rather than merely associated.
- **Autonomic remodeling** — both sympathetic and vagal denervation patterns
  (vagal AF in young, sympathetically mediated AF in older and diseased hearts).

Genetic susceptibility is real: 2018 GWAS meta-analysis identified 97 loci, many in
transcription factors governing conduction and ion channels. Family history in a
first-degree relative confers roughly 40% higher risk.

## Risk factors

[[Hypertension]] and valvular heart disease are the most common modifiable drivers;
diastolic dysfunction, [[Heart Failure|heart failure]], coronary artery disease,
cardiomyopathy, [[Obesity]], [[Sleep Apnea|obstructive sleep apnea]], COPD,
[[Diabetes]], [[Thyroid Hormones|thyrotoxicosis]] and subclinical hyperthyroidism, alcohol
(including holiday heart and low-to-moderate intake), [[Smoking]], sedentary lifestyle,
and psychosocial stress are all implicated. Chronic kidney disease and
[[Inflammaging]]-associated immune activation are increasingly emphasized. Long-term
extreme endurance training is associated with a modest increase in risk in older athletes.

> [!warning] Common correction
> [[Caffeine]] consumption has **not** been shown to increase AF risk in cohort data,
> including at high intake — this has been assumed from palpitations and case reports
> but the epidemiological signal is absent. The vault's [[Caffeine]] note links to
> [[Arrhythmias]]; the AF-specific claim is specifically not supported.

## Clinical consequences

Symptoms are highly variable and often absent. The consequences that matter are:

- **Stroke and systemic embolism.** Loss of coordinated atrial contraction produces
  stasis, particularly in the left atrial appendage, the commonest site of thrombus
  formation; AF roughly quintuples ischaemic stroke risk. Prevention is by
  [[Anticoagulant|anticoagulation]], guided by stroke-risk scoring.
- **Heart failure**, worsened by loss of atrial kick and by tachycardia-mediated
  cardiomyopathy.
- **Cognitive decline and dementia**, both from embolism (silent infarcts) and from
  chronic hypoperfusion and inflammation.
- **Reduced quality of life**, independent of stroke risk.

## Management

- **Rate control** — beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, and
  digoxin, aiming for a lenient target in most patients.
- **Rhythm control** — antiarrhythmics (flecainide, propafenone, amiodarone, dronedarone,
  sotalol, dofetilide) and catheter ablation; increasingly, ablation as first-line for
  symptomatic paroxysmal AF. A large US trial (EAST-AFNET 4) showed early rhythm control
  reduces a composite of death, stroke, HF and hospitalization, though the effect is
  largely driven by hospitalization.
- **Stroke prevention** — anticoagulation; left atrial appendage occlusion for patients
  with absolute contraindications.
- **Risk-factor modification** — weight loss, alcohol abstinence, blood-pressure control,
  sleep-apnoea treatment, and exercise. These are the interventions with the strongest
  effect on AF *burden*, and they are the pharmacological-proof-of-concept for
  [[Metabolic Syndrome]] and [[Physical Activity Level]] as AF targets.
