# Hamartoma

A **hamartoma** is a benign, tumor-like malformation composed of cells and tissues that are *native* to the organ of origin but abnormally arranged and often overgrown. Unlike a true neoplasm, a hamartoma is not driven by a classical oncogene-induced arrest; in the mTOR context it is the **direct manifestation of a hyperactive growth program**. It is the canonical "benign tumor that is *not* good for longevity" — the pathological counter-case to the protective [[Oncogene-Induced Senescence|OIS]] lesion (see [[Benign Tumor]]).

## mTORopathy-associated hamartomas

Loss-of-function mutations in [[TSC1]] or [[TSC2]] (the [[TSC]] complex) relieve inhibition of [[Rheb]], driving constitutive [[mTORC1]] hyperactivation. This produces the benign tumors of **Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC)** and related mTORopathies ([Iffland & Crino, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28135561/); [Iffland et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35136953/)):

- **Cortical tubers** — disorganized neurons/glia; cause epilepsy and autism.
- **Subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA)** — intraventricular, can obstruct CSF flow.
- **Renal angiomyolipoma** — vascular smooth-muscle/fat/adipose overgrowth; bleeding risk.
- **Cardiac rhabdomyoma** — often regresses spontaneously.
- **Pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)** — smooth-muscle proliferation.

Other hamartoma syndromes: **Peutz-Jeghers** ([[LKB1]]/STK11 loss) and **Cowden** ([[PTEN]] loss) — both also feature elevated cancer risk, underscoring that these benign overgrowths sit on a dysplasia-to-neoplasia continuum.

## Why hamartomas are not longevity-protective

The distinction from the protective benign lesion is mechanistic:

| Feature | Protective benign lesion (OIS nevus) | Pathological hamartoma |
|---|---|---|
| Driver | Oncogene → [[DNA Damage Response]] → arrest | [[mTORC1]]/growth-signal hyperactivation |
| Cell state | Permanent [[Cell Cycle Arrest]] (senescent) | Ongoing, disorganized proliferation |
| Longevity effect | Caps malignant potential | Causes seizures, organ dysfunction, cancer risk |

Thus hamartomas exemplify the rule that **only *arrested* growth is beneficial**; continuous growth-signal-driven proliferation — even when "benign" — is disease (consistent with the hyperfunction theory of [[Aging]], see [[Hyperfunction Theory of Aging]]).

## Therapeutic relevance

Because a single mutation drives mTOR hyperactivation, hamartomas are exceptionally rapamycin-responsive. [[Rapamycin]]/[[Rapalog|rapalogs]] (everolimus) reduce tuber volume and seizure frequency (EXIST-3 trial; [Zeng et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18389497/)), the prototype for translating mTOR inhibition to the clinic (see [[mTORopathies]]).
