# Mass Drug Administration

**Mass drug administration** (MDA) is the delivery of a full therapeutic course
of a drug to an entire at-risk population — or a defined subset of it — without
individual diagnosis, on a recurrent schedule. It is distinguished from
individual treatment by the absence of case detection as a precondition, and from
vaccination in that it is not immunological and requires
repeated dosing for as long as the infection remains transmissible.

> [!info] The logic of administering to the well
> MDA is counterintuitive from a clinical standpoint: the drug goes to people
> who are healthy. The rationale is that the drug is killing or sterilising the
> parasite inside the host, which removes the reservoir the parasite needs to
> infect the next host via the vector. Success is measured by transmission
> interruption in the community, not by individual cure.

## Mechanism of Elimination

The design logic is a mass-drug-plus-vector-control package:

1. **High population coverage.** Because prevalence in endemic settings is a
   minority of the population but transmission intensity is a function of
   community worm burden, coverage must be geographically and socially
   complete — typically at least 65% of the eligible population treated
   annually — or the parasite reservoir outlasts the campaign.
2. **Interruption of transmission.** With treatment every year and vector
   survival shortened below the parasite's maturation
   time, transmission falls to zero. Adult worms do not die in the human host
   on their own; they are removed by repeated drug exposure.
3. **Stop MDA, sustain surveillance.** WHO recommends MDA cessation once
   transmission is eliminated as a public-health problem, with post-stop
   surveillance to catch resurgence. Elimination as a *disease* (no new cases)
> differs from elimination as a *public-health problem* (transmission stopped),
   and MDA targets the latter.

> [!warning] Failure modes are mostly social, not pharmacological
> Campaigns are defeated by missed populations, seasonal migration, refusal,
> and stock-outs rather than by drug resistance. That is why MDA has almost
> always required community-directed distribution and household coverage
> surveys rather than fixed facility-based delivery.

## Regimens and Targets

| Disease | Regimen |
| --- | --- |
| --- | --- |
| Lymphatic filariasis | Ivermectin (~200 µg/kg) + [[Albendazole]] (400 mg); [[Diethylcarbamazine]]-based triple-drug (IDA) in areas without onchocerciasis or loiasis co-endemicity; [[Albendazole]] alone twice yearly where loiasis is co-endemic |
| Onchocerciasis | [[Ivermectin]] annually or every six months; doxycycline-based regimens targeting symbiotic *Wolbachia* endosymbionts |

The choice within lymphatic filariasis reflects parasitology rather than
pharmacology: diethylcarbamazine kills the adult worms but can provoke severe
[[Inflammation]] reactions in hosts harbouring onchocercal microfilariae, so
it is withheld where loiasis or onchocerciasis co-circulate. Ivermectin is the
workhorse because it covers filariasis, onchocerciasis, and the major
soil-transmitted helminths simultaneously.

> [!info] MDA is the clearest trial of a drug's safety at population scale
> A drug given to millions of healthy people must have an extremely wide safety
> margin. This is why MDA campaigns have been able to use drugs long after
> individual clinical enthusiasm for them faded, and why the safety surveillance
> of MDA programmes has been as influential as their efficacy data.

## Drugs and Resistance

Ivermectin targets parasite glutamate-gated chloride channels, and while
anthelmintic resistance is a real and growing concern in veterinary use,
confirmed reduced-efficacy populations in endemic regions are still
localized and monitoring remains the priority. The broader concern is
[[Drug Resistance|antimicrobial and antiparasitic resistance]] as a class
threat to the elimination agenda, since MDA depends on few alternative
molecules.
