# Zinc

**Zinc** (Zn, atomic number 30, divalent, `[Ar]3d¹⁰4s²`) is the second most abundant trace metal in the human body after iron and the **only metal represented in all six enzyme classes**. Roughly 2–4 g is distributed through the body, concentrated in brain, muscle, bone, kidney, and liver, with the highest concentrations in the prostate and parts of the eye. A 2015 analysis estimated that on the order of 3,000 human proteins (~10% of the proteome) bind zinc.

Chemically, Zn²⁺ is a closed-shell d¹⁰ ion — it is not redox-active in the way iron or copper are, which is precisely why it is such a good *structural* and *Lewis-acidic* cofactor rather than an electron-transport participant. Its coordination geometry is unusually labile (4- or 6-coordinate), letting zinc enzymes switch conformations rapidly during catalysis.

## Enzymatic Roles

> [!info] Catalytic zinc
> As an efficient Lewis acid, Zn²⁺ polarizes water, carbonyl, and other substrates to lower the activation barrier for hydrolysis and hydroxylation. The two founding examples were **carbonic anhydrase** (1940) and **carboxypeptidase** (1955). Without the catalytic zinc, carbonic anhydrase's CO₂/bicarbonate interconversion would run roughly a million times slower at blood pH.

Representative zinc-dependent enzyme classes include oxidoreductases (e.g. alcohol dehydrogenase), hydrolases (carboxypeptidase, matrix metalloproteinases), kinases (protein kinase C is a zinc enzyme), and phosphatases. Many additional enzymes are zinc-*dependent* rather than zinc-catalytic, meaning the metal maintains a required fold.

## Structural Roles

The best-known structural role is the **zinc finger**, where two zinc ions tetrahedrally coordinate cysteine and histidine side chains to hold a small ββα motif in a geometry that presents a DNA-recognizing surface. Nine or ten Zn²⁺ ions can stabilize a single zinc finger array, and thousands of human transcription factors (many of them in the vault's [[Transcription Factor]] and [[Zinc Finger]] notes) are built this way. Because the fold is small and modular, zinc fingers are widely used in engineered DNA-binding proteins.

Zinc also acts as a **second messenger**. It is released from metallothionein-bound stores and from synaptic vesicles of glutamatergic neurons, where it can modulate NMDA-receptor-mediated excitability and synaptic plasticity — a zinc-dependent contribution to learning and memory. Zinc signaling is also used by immune cells, the prostate, salivary gland, and intestine to communicate state locally.

## Redox and Antioxidant Context

Zinc sits at a critical junction with [[Oxidative Stress]], but the direction of its influence is context-dependent and frequently double-edged:

- It supports [[Glutathione Peroxidase]] and related antioxidant systems indirectly, and stabilizes membranes.
- It is the **structural (not catalytic) partner of [[Copper]] in SOD1** — loss of zinc from that site renders the enzyme catalytically dead and releases the copper to a pro-oxidant pool.
- Excess synaptic zinc is itself neurotoxic, via disruption of electron transport chain enzymes (complex I, complex III, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase), disruption of [[Calcium]] homeostasis, and excitotoxicity.

> [!warning] Genotype-dependent effects
> Zinc is not a benign antioxidant. In the CRND8 mouse model of late-onset Alzheimer's disease, **ApoE ε4 carriers showed *worsened* spatial memory and increased soluble and insoluble amyloid-β after dietary zinc supplementation**, attributed to ApoEε4-dependent Zn–Aβ binding and altered zinc transporter expression. This is a concrete demonstration that a supplement can be harmful in a defined genetic context, and it is the reason this vault does not treat zinc supplementation as a general neuroprotective intervention.

## Homeostasis, Nutrition & Requirements

Intestinal absorption is the main regulatory point, mediated by ZIP transporters (notably SLC39A4/ZIP4) on the apical surface. Plasma zinc is carried bound to [[Albumin]] (low affinity) and transferrin, and **plasma zinc concentration stays relatively constant regardless of intake**, which is why the most widely used biomarker has poor sensitivity and specificity — a real limitation for individual assessment. Intestinal and hepatic **metallothionein** acts as a buffer that can adjust absorption by 15–40% and sequesters copper as well, which explains copper deficiency on high zinc intake.

- **US RDAs** (IOM): 8 mg/day adult women, 11 mg/day adult men, 11 mg/day in pregnancy, 12 mg/day in lactation; 3 mg/day for infants. Tolerable upper intake level 40 mg/day (US), 25 mg/day (EFSA).
- **EFSA** population reference intakes scale upward with dietary phytate, from 7.5–9.4 mg/day at low phytate to 12.7–16.3 mg/day at high phytate, because phytate chelates zinc and blocks absorption.
- **Bioavailability is form-dependent**: zinc acetate and zinc sulfate are better absorbed than zinc oxide or zinc carbonate, a finding relevant to supplement formulation but less to food fortification, where the matrix changes the picture.

## Deficiency and Excess

> [!info] Deficiency
> Inadequacy of zinc intake affects a large fraction of the developing world population and is one of the most common micronutrient deficiencies worldwide. In children it causes growth retardation, delayed sexual maturation, impaired immunity with increased infection and diarrheal susceptibility, and skin/eye lesions; the WHO advocates zinc supplementation in severe malnutrition and diarrhea, but cautions against giving it alone in populations with multiple deficiencies. Causes beyond poor intake include malabsorption, chronic liver and kidney disease, and sickle cell disease. Inherited zinc malabsorption presents as acrodermatitis enteropathica.

> [!warning] Excess
> Chronic high intake induces copper deficiency (via metallothionein sequestration and intestinal copper competition) with anemia and myelopathy, and causes nausea, ataxia, and lethargy. Acute zinc poisoning produces nausea, vomiting, and metal fume fever.

## Connections

- [[Selenium]] — zinc and selenium are the two trace elements that most directly determine antioxidant enzyme competence in the vault's redox framework: selenium supplies the catalytic selenocysteine in glutathione peroxidases and thioredoxin reductases, while zinc supplies structure and Lewis acidity in the parallel zinc enzyme set. They are co-administered in essentially every multivitamin and are discussed as a pair in the Nrf2/exercise literature.
- [[Copper]] — copper and zinc are direct nutritional antagonists: they compete for intestinal uptake, share the metallothionein sequestration pool, and cooperate structurally in SOD1. This is the single most mechanistically important zinc relationship in nutrition.
- [[NRF2]] and [[Keap1]] — zinc status modulates the Nrf2 antioxidant response: Keap1's zinc-binding and redox chemistry is part of how the pathway is switched on, so zinc sufficiency is permissive for Nrf2-driven antioxidant gene expression.
- [[Glutathione]] and [[Glutathione Peroxidase]] — zinc supports the glutathione system indirectly (membrane stability, thiol handling, and the selenium-dependent GPx axis) rather than as a GPx cofactor, so the interaction is regulatory rather than direct.
- [[Zinc Finger]] — the structural zinc-finger domain is the mechanism by which thousands of transcription factors read DNA; this is the connection from the inorganic ion to [[Gene Expression]] control.
- [[Transcription Factor]] — zinc fingers are the archetypal DNA-binding domain of a large fraction of human transcription factors, and the reason the element sits at the center of gene-regulatory biology.
- [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] and [[Ferroptosis]] — zinc status modulates ROS generation (via NADPH oxidase) and lipid peroxidation; the interaction with [[Lipid Peroxidation]] and [[Ferroptosis]] is active area of research, though the exact quantitative contribution of zinc to ferroptosis regulation is not settled.
- [[Parkinson's Disease]] — via [[DJ-1]], whose zinc-binding site is required for structural integrity; zinc dyshomeostasis is described in PD and in neurodegenerative disease more broadly, though causality in humans is not established.
- [[Calcium]] — zinc and calcium are divalent cations with overlapping synaptic roles, and zinc modulates calcium entry through NMDA receptors; the two compete and interact at the synapse and in bone.
- [[Mitochondrial Complex I]] — excess synaptic zinc inhibits complex I and complex III, linking zinc dyshomeostasis to [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]] and the [[Electron Transport Chain]].
- [[Metallothionein]] *(suggested)* — the buffer protein that governs intestinal zinc (and copper) absorption and the mechanism of zinc-induced copper deficiency.
- [[Albumin]] and transferrin — the plasma carriers; transferrin's dual role for iron and zinc explains the iron–zinc antagonism as well.
- [[Immune System]] — zinc deficiency is a common cause of impaired immune competence, and zinc supplementation in deficient children reduces diarrheal and infectious morbidity.
- [[Exercise]] — appears in the vault's redox-exercise literature as an intervention context in which zinc status and supplementation interact with Nrf2/BDNF and neuroprotection, with the caveat noted above.
- [[Depression]] and [[Neurodegeneration]] — mood and cognitive claims for zinc supplementation exist in the orthomolecular literature and in animal models; human trial evidence is inconsistent, and this is one of the weaker evidentiary areas for zinc.
