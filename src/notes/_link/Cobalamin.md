# Cobalamin

**Cobalamin** (vitamin B12) is a coordination complex of cobalt held in a
corrin macrocycle, with a dimethylbenzimidazole base ligand and a variable
axial ligand that determines which of the several vitamers is present. It is
the most structurally complex of all vitamins and the only one whose synthesis
is restricted to prokaryotes — no plant, animal, or fungus makes it.

## Why a bacterium makes it

The corrin ring binds a single cobalt ion, and cobalt is nearly unavailable to
anaerobic and microaerophilic organisms. The adaptive story is that
cobalamin-dependent metabolism lets bacteria run 1,2-carbon rearrangements and
radical-mediated methyl transfers that would otherwise be too energetically
demanding. Two enzyme families use it, and they use it in opposite oxidation
states.

> [!info] The two human cofactor reactions
> In humans, cobalamin appears in exactly two enzyme systems.
>
> **Methionine synthase** (cytosolic, 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-dependent) uses
> the **methylcobalamin** form to transfer a methyl group from
> 5-methyltetrahydrofolate onto the cobalamin-bound cobalt, then onto
> [[Homocysteine]] — the terminal reaction of the [[Methylation Cycle]] that
> regenerates [[Methionine]]. This is the link between B12 status,
> methylation, and [[DNA Methylation]].
>
> **Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase** (mitochondrial) uses the
> [[Adenosylcobalamin]] form as a source of a protein-bound carbon-centred
> radical that drives an acyl rearrangement of methylmalonyl-CoA to
> [[Succinyl-CoA]], the entry step of the
> [[Citric Acid Cycle|TCA cycle]] for odd-chain fatty acids, branched-chain
> amino acids, and several cholesterol and porphyrin metabolites.
>
> Everything else cobalamin does in a cell is downstream of those two.

## Microbial and host roles beyond the vitamin

The link from the bacterial cofactor to host defence is direct and
economically important. Gut bacteria biosynthesise cobalamin but, for most
non-cobalamin-synthesising species, do not excrete it — they compete for and
scavenge the vitamin. That competition is the mechanism behind cobalamin
antagonism in [[Salmonella enterica]]: the *cobS*/*cobT* pathway is under
positive selection in *Salmonella* populations exposed to antibiotic or
microbiome pressure, and the bacterial enzyme cobB appears in cobalamin
biosynthesis intermediates. Cobalamin status is thus not a single-host
variable; it is a contested resource in the [[Gut Microbiome|microbial
community]].

The most striking non-vitamin role is in methanogenesis: archaea in the
gastrointestinal tract use cobalamin as a cofactor for methyl-CoM reductase,
the rate-limiting enzyme of the final step of methane production. Human
intestinal methanogens therefore have a direct cobalamin requirement that is
independent of the host's own.

## Absorption and the intrinsic factor bottleneck

Absorption requires gastric [[Intrinsic Factor]], a glycoprotein secreted by
parietal cells that binds B12 in the duodenum and delivers it to cubilin
receptors in the distal ileum. Absorption is inefficient at normal intakes
(about 1–2% of an oral dose by passive diffusion), which is why oral
supplements work only at the 1000 µg/day scale. B12 binds plasma
transcobalamin II for tissue delivery and haptocorrin (transcobalamin I) for
circulating storage; the hepatic half-life is extraordinarily long, on the
order of 400 days, and total body stores of 2–5 mg last several years.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> The most common cause of clinically significant B12 deficiency in developed
> countries is not dietary intake but loss of intrinsic factor or gastric acid —
> pernicious anaemia, atrophic gastritis, proton-pump inhibitor or H2-blocker
> use, and metformin. Because liver stores last years, deficiency presents
> late, after irreversible neurological damage is possible. Diagnosis rests on
> elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine rather than serum B12 alone,
> which lags tissue depletion; MMA is also raised in renal insufficiency and
> homocysteine in folate deficiency, so neither is conclusive in isolation.
> The methylmalonic acid to succinyl-CoA link is also why deficiency
> masquerades as a mitochondrial problem — the elevated
> [[D-lactate]] and propionate-derived metabolites in B12 deficiency are
> products of the blocked mutase step.
>
> [[Nitrous Oxide]] is a clinically underappreciated trap: it irreversibly
> oxidises the cobalt in methylcobalamin, functionally inactivating
> methionine synthase without any change in measured serum B12.

## Connections

- [[Methylation Cycle]] — cobalamin's methylcobalamin form is the cofactor for
  the methionine synthase reaction that closes the cycle; B12 deficiency and
  folate deficiency both produce homocysteine elevation at the same step.
- [[Betaine]] — the betaine-dependent remethylase pathway is a partial bypass
  for methionine synthase, so adequate choline and betaine can blunt the
  homocysteine rise of B12 deficiency without correcting the
  methylmalonyl-CoA branch.
- [[Choline]] — choline is oxidised to betaine, making the choline axis a
  second-line defence when the folate- and B12-dependent remethylation route
  is impaired.
- [[CobB]] — the bacterial nicotinamidase that generates a cobalamin
  precursor in the *Salmonella* biosynthesis pathway; the genetic node
  under selection in the note's source document.
- [[Salmonella enterica]] — carries both cobalamin synthesis genes and
  cobalamin-scavenging systems, making it the vault's exemplar organism for
  the microbiome side of B12 biology.
- [[Homocysteine]] — the substrate whose plasma level is the standard
  functional readout of the methionine synthase step and therefore of
  functional B12 status.
- [[Hydroxocobalamin]] — the parenteral vitamer used both to correct
  deficiency and as a cyanide antidote: the cyanide ion displaces its
  hydroxide ligand, forming non-toxic cyanocobalamin excreted in urine.
