# Could this enzyme help remove "zombie" cells from our tissues?

**Salk Institute researchers discover that the enzyme [[Acid ceramidase]] can make aging cells more vulnerable to the cell death pathway [[Ferroptosis]] and may be a promising target for supporting healthy aging.**

## Highlights

- Salk scientists identify two cellular programs linked to age-related dysfunction, [[Ferroptosis]] and [[Senescence|senescence]], are mechanistically linked through the enzyme [[Acid ceramidase]].
- The team finds that [[Senescent Cells|senescent "zombie" cells]] are more vulnerable to regulated cell death through [[Ferroptosis]] and that they can pass that sensitivity on to neighboring cells.
- The observations suggest [[Acid ceramidase]] could be a powerful therapeutic target for supporting healthy aging and preventing age-related diseases and disorders such as [[Osteoarthritis|arthritis]], [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|dementia]], and [[Cancer]].

LA JOLLA—Our population is aging. The United Nations reports that by 2050, the number of people over the age of 85 on our planet is set to *triple.* But a longer life span isn't the same as a longer [[Healthspan|healthspan]] (the number of years we spend healthy). Salk Institute scientists are working to change that through foundational research that explores the cellular mechanisms driving age-related dysfunction.

> [!info] Core finding
> Elevated levels of the enzyme [[Acid ceramidase]] make [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] more susceptible to [[Ferroptosis]] and can pass that vulnerability to neighboring cells. Because [[Acid ceramidase]] is already a druggable target (inhibitors exist for other indications), it represents a near-term, testable [[Senolytic|senotherapeutic]] strategy for clearing senescent cells.

The latest efforts to understand cellular aging look at two processes prevalent in aged cells: 1) [[Senescence|senescence]], in which cells stop dividing but don't die, and 2) [[Ferroptosis]], in which cells lose their ability to regulate fats and eventually die. While studying human lung cells, the Salk team discovered that elevated levels of the enzyme *[[Acid ceramidase]]* make [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] more susceptible to [[Ferroptosis]] and can pass that vulnerability to neighboring cells.

Experimental drugs that target [[Acid ceramidase]] have already been developed for other therapeutic purposes, demonstrating that [[Acid ceramidase]] is targetable and lighting the way to future innovation that could extend healthspans.

The study was published in [*Cell Death and Disease*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41419-026-09108-y#article-info) on July 10, 2026. (See the companion primary paper: [[_document_ - Acid_ceramidase_modulates_the_lipid_profile_and_ex|Acid ceramidase modulates the lipid profile and exacerbates sensitivity to ferroptosis in WI-38 replicative senescent cells]].)

> [!quote] Pam Maher, PhD (senior author)
> "[[Senescent Cells|Senescent cells]] are linked to many age-related conditions, including [[Osteoarthritis|arthritis]], poor wound healing, and [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|neurodegenerative diseases]] like [[Alzheimer's Disease|Alzheimer's]] or [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's]]. Our findings are a big milestone in the ongoing public health effort to support healthy aging, as we now have a novel target for developing new therapeutics that could be applied to a multitude of diseases and disorders."

## What are ferroptosis and senescence?

Maher is the person to ask about [[Ferroptosis]]—she discovered the cellular pathway and named it ("oxytosis" originally) in 2001. [[Ferroptosis]] is an [[Iron|iron]]-dependent cell death pathway in which the accumulation of [[Lipid Peroxidation|lipid peroxides]] becomes toxic to cells. Healthy cells keep [[Lipid Peroxidation|lipid peroxides]] at steady levels with the help of [[Glutathione]], an [[Antioxidant]] that can stave off [[Ferroptosis]].

> [!note] Links to neurodegeneration
> Recent studies have reinforced links between [[Ferroptosis]] and [[Alzheimer's Disease|Alzheimer's]] and [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's]] diseases. In a recent *Cell Death Discovery* paper, Maher's lab discovered that chronic [[Iron|iron]] overexposure in neurons makes them less resilient over time and more vulnerable to [[Neurodegeneration|neurodegeneration]].

This study is one of many to link [[Ferroptosis]] to [[Neurodegeneration|neurodegeneration]], as targeting the pathway has become a promising therapeutic strategy for age-related diseases, alongside another growing area of interest: *[[Senescence|senescence]].*

[[Cellular Senescence|Cellular senescence]] is a biological process in which cells don't completely die but also aren't quite alive. Often referred to as "zombie" cells, [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] are a hallmark of aging. Though sometimes harmless, other times they release harmful factors (the [[SASP|senescence-associated secretory phenotype]]) into surrounding tissues and can promote the development of diseases or disorders, including [[Cancer]].

## Are ferroptosis and senescence connected during aging?

Since [[Ferroptosis]] and [[Senescence|senescence]] have been independently linked to age-related dysfunction, and both have become promising therapeutic targets for age-related diseases, the Salk team wondered whether there was any overlap in the two cellular processes.

To find an answer, the researchers cultured [[Senescent Cells|senescent human lung cells]] in the lab. Then, they induced [[Ferroptosis]] in the [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] to find they were far more sensitive to the cell death pathway than young, healthy cells. The sensitivity began as [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] expressed progressively higher levels of an enzyme called *[[Acid ceramidase]].*

> [!info] Mechanism in one line
> [[Acid ceramidase]] breaks down [[Ceramide|ceramides]] into sphingosine and free fatty acids, reshaping membrane [[Lipid Metabolism|lipid composition]] toward a pro-ferroptotic profile rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids ([[PUFA|PUFAs]])—the preferred substrates for [[Lipid Peroxidation]].

After discovering that excessive [[Acid ceramidase]] drives cell sensitivity to [[Ferroptosis]], the researchers removed the enzyme from senescent and non-senescent cells to see whether its absence would make cells more resilient to induced [[Ferroptosis]]. *And it did.* Removal of [[Acid ceramidase]] from cells protected both young and old cells from [[Ferroptosis]].

> [!warning] A novel, GPX4/GSH-independent axis
> "This is an entirely novel pathway that is independent of the mechanisms usually associated with [[Ferroptosis]]-induced cell death, as it doesn't involve any changes in [[Iron|iron]] or [[Glutathione]] levels but instead modulates the [[Lipid Metabolism|lipid metabolism]]," says first author David Soriano-Castell, PhD.

The Salk team also observed something else: Vulnerable [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] were able to pass their vulnerability on to neighboring cells. This helps explain why only a few [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] can turn into more, ultimately impacting a larger tissue, as time goes on.

> [!quote] David Soriano-Castell, PhD (first author)
> "We have unraveled a new mechanistic connection between [[Ferroptosis]] and [[Senescence|senescence]]. This gives us a clear target that would eliminate two birds with one stone, eliminating [[Senescent Cells|senescent cells]] and keeping neighboring cells healthy for longer."

## How does this advance healthy aging?

Unrelated to the Salk study, scientists have already developed drugs to target [[Acid ceramidase]] for the treatment of other diseases in which this enzyme also plays a role. The Salk findings bring forward a new potential use case for these drugs, and the fact that they are already in development is a great proof-of-concept that drugs can, in fact, be designed to target the enzyme.

> [!tip] Path to the clinic
> "This is only the beginning," says Soriano-Castell. "The next step is finding a more complete pathway and translating into animals and tissues, then clinical trials of our own, and so on. But what is important is that [[Acid ceramidase]] is targetable, and other labs have already gotten that work started—later on, we can hopefully build on what they learn."

> [!quote] Pam Maher, PhD
> "The more we learn about [[Ferroptosis]], the more possibilities there are for healthy aging innovations. This is an exciting time to be studying aging, resilience, and longevity, and I look forward to continuing to see how [[Ferroptosis]] can be a useful target."
