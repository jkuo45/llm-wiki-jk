# Flow-Mediated Dilation

**Flow-mediated dilation (FMD)** measures how much a conduit artery widens in response to a shear-stress challenge. A pressure cuff around the upper arm is inflated to roughly 200 mmHg for five minutes, occluding flow and producing local ischemia; on release, downstream resistance vessels dilate and the resulting surge in flow creates a transient physiologic shear stress stimulus on the brachial artery endothelium. Brachial artery diameter is tracked continuously by high-resolution ultrasound, and FMD is computed as the percentage increase from baseline to peak diameter.

Introduced in 1992, FMD became the reference non-invasive test of endothelial function because endothelial dysfunction precedes any structural or symptomatic sign of [[Atherosclerosis]] — it interrogates a stage of disease that angiography and blood pressure cannot see.

> [!info] What the number means
> An FMD of 6% means the artery widened from 4.0 mm to 4.24 mm. It is an **endothelium-dependent, largely nitric-oxide-mediated** response; the parallel test of nitroglycerin-induced dilation assesses endothelium-*independent* smooth muscle function, and reporting both as an FMD/NID ratio is the current refinement.

## Mechanism

Shear stress is transduced by the endothelium through mechanosensors including glycocalyx and PECAM-1, activating [[eNOS]] via Akt-dependent phosphorylation and, in some beds, through shear-sensitive ion channels. The resulting [[Nitric Oxide]] diffuses into adjacent smooth muscle, activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, raises cGMP and relaxes the vessel. Any step in this chain — reduced NO production, oxidative scavenging of NO by superoxide, [[Arginase]] competition for substrate, [[ADMA]] accumulation, [[Tetrahydrobiopterin]] depletion — lowers FMD.

Because FMD is NO-dependent, it is tightly coupled to the vault's redox chemistry: reactive oxygen species quench NO and depress FMD directly.

## Prognostic Value

Meta-analysis of 35 FMD studies covering over 17,000 participants found each **1% absolute increase** in FMD associated with roughly 12% lower relative risk of cardiovascular events, with a 1-standard-deviation deterioration roughly doubling risk. This magnitude is comparable to reactive hyperemia–peripheral arterial tonometry (RH-PAT), the simpler alternative measuring finger pulse amplitude rather than brachial diameter; the two correlate poorly in the Framingham cohort, suggesting they sample partly distinct aspects of vascular health.

FMD is measurably reduced in [[Hypertension]], [[Diabetes Mellitus]] and its complications, [[Dyslipidemia]], [[Obesity]], [[Chronic Kidney Disease]], metabolic syndrome and [[Aging]], and improves after statins, antihypertensives, [[Exercise]], dietary weight loss and — in several trials — SGLT2 inhibitors.

## Methodological Caveats

> [!warning] The technique's weak points are structural, not incidental
> FMD is technically demanding and highly operator-dependent. Baseline brachial diameter is a potent confounder of the percentage index — because FMD% scales inversely with baseline diameter, the metric can generate **spurious** correlations between shear rate, artery size and endothelial function. Much of the published debate over FMD% is a critique of this scaling problem rather than of the underlying biology. Consensus recommendations include:
> - continuous diameter measurement for at least 180 s after cuff release, with most peaks in the first 120 s — calculating FMD from the fixed 60-s value underestimates the true peak by 25–40%
> - reporting the shear-rate stimulus alongside the dilation
> - recognising that time-to-peak diameter is **not** an independent marker of NO bioavailability and should not be used as an endothelial-health adjunct
> - acknowledging normal values vary from 5% to 15% across laboratories

Consequently the 2010 ACCF/AHA guideline did not recommend FMD for risk assessment in asymptomatic adults, and no universal reference values have been agreed. Its strength remains in **within-subject serial measurement** in research settings using a standardised protocol.
