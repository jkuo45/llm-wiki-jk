# Methylene Blue

Adam Ostrovsky; Muriam Afzal.

Last Update: January 2, 2026.

## Continuing Education Activity

[[Methylene blue]] is a multifaceted therapeutic agent used across a wide range of clinical settings, with its well-established role being the [[Food and Drug Administration]] (FDA)–approved treatment for acquired [[Methemoglobinemia]]. The unique redox properties of methylene blue enable the restoration of functional [[Hemoglobin]] and enhanced oxygen delivery, making it a critical intervention in toxicologic emergencies. Beyond this indication, methylene blue has demonstrated clinical utility in perioperative care, critical illness, and select neurologic and oncologic procedures, underscoring the importance of a thorough understanding of its mechanisms, benefits, and potential risks for clinicians across specialties.

This activity provides a comprehensive review of methylene blue, including FDA-approved and commonly recognized off-label uses such as [[Vasoplegic Shock]], [[Ifosfamide]]-induced encephalopathy, and intraoperative staining, as well as emerging applications in neuroprotective and psychiatric research. Learners will explore evidence-based dosing and administration strategies, key contraindications, and clinically significant adverse effects, including [[Hemolysis]], [[Serotonin Syndrome]], [[Anaphylaxis]], and interference with diagnostic monitoring. Emphasis is placed on patient selection, safety monitoring, and interprofessional collaboration to support informed decision-making and optimize patient outcomes in diverse clinical environments.

**Objectives:**

- Identify approved and commonly used off-label indications for methylene blue.
- Apply knowledge of methylene blue's pharmacologic mechanisms to guide safe and effective clinical decision-making.
- Implement evidence-based monitoring strategies in patients receiving methylene blue to detect adverse effects such as hemolysis, serotonin syndrome, anaphylaxis, and neurologic toxicity.
- Collaborate with pharmacists, nurses, and other healthcare providers to ensure safe prescribing, administration, and monitoring of methylene blue.

## Indications

Methylene blue is approved by the FDA as a treatment for acquired [[Methemoglobinemia]], a condition in which hemoglobin iron is oxidized from the ferrous (Fe2+) to ferric (Fe3+) state. Ferric iron is unable to bind oxygen effectively, resulting in impaired oxygen delivery, reduced oxygen saturation, and subsequent tissue [[Hypoxia]].

Common causative agents of acquired methemoglobinemia include pharmaceuticals such as [[Dapsone]], [[Benzocaine]], and [[Phenazopyridine]], as well as [[Nitrates]] and [[Nitrites]]. Patients typically present with [[Cyanosis]], [[Dyspnea]], and fatigue, with a characteristic saturation gap between [[Pulse Oximetry]] and [[Arterial Blood Gas]] measurements. Patients may also exhibit chocolate-brown discoloration of blood, and symptoms correlate with methemoglobin levels—cyanosis appears above 10%, anxiety and tachycardia occur at 20% to 50%, and levels exceeding 50% to 70% can be fatal.

> [!info] Methemoglobinemia severity
> Symptoms correlate with methemoglobin percentage: cyanosis above 10%, anxiety/tachycardia at 20–50%, and fatal risk at 50–70%.

Methylene blue has several well-established off-label applications beyond its FDA-approved indication for methemoglobinemia, including:

- **Intraoperative staining:** Utilized to enhance visualization of structures such as the ureters, parathyroid glands, and sentinel lymph nodes during surgery, including sentinel lymph node mapping in [[Breast Cancer]] and other malignancies.
- **Ifosfamide-induced encephalopathy:** Widely used off-label for the treatment of neurotoxicity associated with ifosfamide administration.
- **Refractory vasoplegic shock** (including septic and anaphylactic shock): Employed as adjunctive therapy to increase systemic vascular resistance in cases refractory to standard [[Vasopressors]], particularly in the setting of cardiac surgery (e.g., [[Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting]]).
- **Antimalarial agent:** Historically used for this indication; however, it is no longer considered standard therapy due to resistance and the availability of more effective agents.
- **Neuroprotective agent:** Investigational use in neurologic conditions, such as [[Alzheimer Disease]], [[Parkinson's Disease]], [[Traumatic Brain Injury]], and [[Ischemic Stroke]], based on preclinical and early clinical data.
- **Psychiatric disorders:** Studied as adjunctive therapy in [[Bipolar Disorder]], [[Major Depressive Disorder]], and [[Schizophrenia]]; not FDA-approved for these indications.
- **Cyanide poisoning:** Occasionally cited as an adjunctive treatment, though not an FDA-approved or first-line therapy.
- **COVID-19** and [[Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome]]: Proposed for its potential anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties; clinical efficacy has not been established, and use is not FDA-approved.

## Mechanism of Action

Methylene blue exerts its pharmacological effect primarily as a [[Redox]] agent and alternative electron carrier. In the treatment of methemoglobinemia, methylene blue is reduced by [[NADPH]]-dependent [[Methemoglobin Reductase]] to leucomethylene blue, which then reduces Fe3+ in methemoglobin back to the ferrous state Fe2+, restoring normal hemoglobin function. This nonenzymatic redox conversion is the primary mechanism by which methylene blue reverses methemoglobinemia.

[[Vitamin C]] exhibits similar reducing properties and can serve as an alternative treatment for methemoglobinemia, though with significant limitations. Ascorbic acid reduces methemoglobin through a slower, nonenzymatic pathway that often requires multiple doses over several hours to achieve a significant effect. Although case reports demonstrate successful treatment with high-dose vitamin C, it is unlikely to be effective in a resuscitation situation. Vitamin C is most appropriate when methylene blue is contraindicated—such as in patients with [[Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase]] (G6PD) deficiency—or when methylene blue is unavailable.

> [!tip] Alternative to methylene blue
> [[Vitamin C]] (ascorbic acid) reduces methemoglobin via a slower nonenzymatic pathway and is preferred when methylene blue is contraindicated (e.g., G6PD deficiency) or unavailable.

At the cellular level, methylene blue can bypass dysfunctional components of the [[Mitochondrial Electron Transport Chain]] by shuttling electrons from [[NADH]], in the presence of [[Complex I]], and donating them directly to [[Cytochrome c]], effectively bypassing complexes I through III. This alternative electron transfer pathway enhances [[Mitochondrial Respiration]], increases oxygen consumption, reduces [[Mitochondrial Superoxide]] production, and mitigates [[Oxidative Stress]]—mechanisms that underlie its neuroprotective effects in conditions such as Alzheimer disease, Parkinson disease, traumatic brain injury, and stroke. Additionally, methylene blue inhibits [[Nitric Oxide Synthase]] and [[Guanylate Cyclase]], contributing to its vasoconstrictive effects in the management of vasoplegic shock.

Methylene blue inhibits both nitric oxide synthase and guanylate cyclase, contributing to its vasoconstrictive effects in vasoplegic shock. By inhibiting the [[Nitric Oxide]]–guanylate cyclase–[[Cyclic Guanosine Monophosphate]] pathway, methylene blue attenuates the excessive vasodilation mediated by inflammatory cytokines in septic and post-cardiopulmonary bypass vasoplegic shock.

## Administration

The dosing and administration of methylene blue vary depending on the indication, patient characteristics, and clinical setting. Although standardized regimens exist for the treatment of acquired methemoglobinemia, many other uses are off-label and supported primarily by clinical experience, case series, and institutional protocols. Careful attention to dosing, infusion timing, and patient-specific factors is essential to optimize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

In both pediatric and adult patients, methylene blue is administered intravenously at a dose of 1 mg/kg over 5 to 30 minutes. A repeat dose of 1 mg/kg may be given after 1 hour if methemoglobin levels remain above 30% or symptoms persist. If the condition does not resolve after 2 doses, alternative treatment strategies should be considered.

In patients with moderate-to-severe renal impairment (estimated [[Glomerular Filtration Rate]] 15–59 mL/min/1.73 m²), the recommended regimen is a single intravenous dose of 1 mg/kg. If methemoglobin levels remain elevated or symptoms persist after 1 hour, alternative therapies should be pursued.

For intraoperative lymph node mapping, 1 to 5 mL of 1% methylene blue is injected peritumorally, subareolarly, or pericervically, depending on the tumor type and anatomical site. The dye migrates through lymphatic channels, allowing visual identification of sentinel lymph nodes to guide surgical staging and resection.

Methylene blue has been evaluated at a dose of 50 mg (2 mL of a 2.5% solution) administered intravenously approximately 45 seconds before [[Propofol]] injection. Although this approach has demonstrated efficacy in reducing injection-related pain, it is not standard practice, and [[Lidocaine]] remains the preferred agent.

For the treatment of ifosfamide-induced neurotoxicity, methylene blue is typically administered intravenously at a dosage of 50 mg every 4 hours until symptom resolution. This regimen is supported by case series and institutional experience; however, dosing is individualized based on clinical response, with symptom resolution often occurring within 10 minutes to 24 hours.

In vasoplegic shock, methylene blue is commonly administered as a single intravenous bolus of 2 mg/kg over 20 to 30 minutes. Some protocols incorporate a bolus followed by continuous infusion; however, evidence supporting routine preoperative or postoperative prophylactic use to reduce mortality remains limited, and this practice is not considered standard of care.

Methylene blue, used for parathyroid gland identification during [[Parathyroidectomy]], may be administered at a dose of 0.4 mg/kg intravenously 1 hour before surgery. However, this practice has fallen out of favor at many institutions due to concerns about neurotoxicity, particularly at higher cumulative doses (>5 mg/kg). Lower doses are increasingly preferred.

All dosing should be individualized based on patient-specific factors and institutional protocols. Caution is warranted in patients with G6PD deficiency and in those receiving serotonergic medications due to the risk of hemolysis and serotonin syndrome, respectively.

## Adverse Effects

Methylene blue is generally well-tolerated but carries several critical adverse effects and contraindications that clinicians must consider when treating methemoglobinemia or using it for off-label indications. The most commonly reported adverse reactions include headache, [[Hypokalemia]], diarrhea, [[Hypomagnesemia]], [[Myoclonus]], nausea, and seizure-like phenomena. Additional adverse effects include hemolytic anemia; cardiovascular effects, such as palpitations, tachycardia, and [[Hypertension]]; gastrointestinal disturbances; and blue-green discoloration of urine, which is expected and benign.

**Serotonin Syndrome**

Serotonin syndrome has been reported following administration of methylene blue, particularly when used concomitantly with medications that increase serotonergic activity, including [[Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors]], [[Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors]], and [[Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors]]; certain [[Opioids]]; and [[Dextromethorphan]]. The risk is increased with concurrent use, and fatal cases have been described.

Clinical manifestations include the following:

- Altered mental status: agitation, hallucinations, delirium, or coma
- Autonomic instability: tachycardia, labile blood pressure, dizziness, diaphoresis, flushing, and hyperthermia
- Neuromuscular abnormalities: tremor, rigidity, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, and incoordination
- Seizures
- Gastrointestinal symptoms: nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea

Concomitant use of methylene blue with serotonergic agents and opioids should be avoided. Patients receiving methylene blue should be closely monitored for signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome; if suspected, methylene blue should be discontinued and supportive care initiated promptly. Patients should be counseled regarding this risk and advised to avoid serotonergic medications for at least 72 hours following the final dose of methylene blue.

> [!warning] Serotonin syndrome boxed warning
> The FDA labeling carries a boxed warning that methylene blue may cause serious or fatal serotonin syndrome when combined with serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs) and opioids; concomitant use should be avoided entirely.

**Anaphylaxis**

Anaphylactic reactions have been reported in patients receiving methylene blue. Patients should be closely monitored for signs of hypersensitivity, including [[Angioedema]], [[Urticaria]], and [[Bronchospasm]]. If anaphylaxis or other severe hypersensitivity reactions occur, methylene blue should be discontinued immediately and appropriate supportive care initiated.

**Hemolytic Anemia**

Hemolysis may occur during the treatment of methemoglobinemia with methylene blue. Laboratory findings can include [[Heinz Bodies]], elevated indirect [[Bilirubin]], decreased [[Haptoglobin]], and a negative [[Direct Antiglobulin Test]] (Coombs test). Anemia may develop 1 or more days after administration and, in severe cases, may require [[Red Blood Cell Transfusion]]. To minimize risk, the lowest effective number of methylene blue doses should be used, and the drug should be discontinued if significant hemolysis develops. Alternative therapies for methemoglobinemia should be considered.

Hemolysis associated with methylene blue therapy is mechanistically linked to its oxidative properties, which can overwhelm erythrocyte antioxidant defenses, particularly in susceptible individuals. The American Heart Association emphasizes that patients with G6PD deficiency are at markedly increased risk, as impaired NADPH generation limits [[Glutathione]] regeneration, rendering red blood cells unable to counteract oxidative stress and predisposing to rapid hemolysis and worsening anemia.

**Encephalopathy**

In clinical trials, methylene blue treatment has been associated with central nervous system adverse effects, including confusion, dizziness, and visual disturbances. Patients should be advised to avoid driving, operating heavy or potentially hazardous machinery, or engaging in activities requiring full alertness until these symptoms have fully resolved, as such effects may impair safe task performance.

**Interference with Clinical Monitoring and Laboratory Assays**

The presence of methylene blue in the bloodstream can cause pulse oximetry to significantly underestimate true oxygen saturation by interfering with the device's measurement wavelengths. During and shortly after methylene blue infusion, arterial blood gas analysis with direct measurement of oxygen saturation (SaO2) should be used instead of pulse oximetry to accurately assess oxygenation.

Methylene blue is excreted in the urine and, because of its blue coloration, may interfere with urinalysis methods that rely on colorimetric indicators, such as dipstick testing for [[Leukocyte Esterase]], potentially resulting in false-negative or uninterpretable results. When accurate urine testing is clinically necessary, alternative diagnostic approaches or repeat testing after dye clearance should be considered.

## Contraindications

Due to the risk of hemolysis and worsening anemia, methylene blue is contraindicated in patients with [[Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase]] deficiency. In addition, it is contraindicated in individuals with a known history of anaphylaxis or other severe hypersensitivity reactions to methylene blue or related thiazine dye products, given reports of life-threatening allergic reactions.

Although not an absolute contraindication, methylene blue may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. Intra-amniotic administration of methylene blue–containing products, particularly during the second trimester, has been associated with serious fetal adverse outcomes, including [[Intestinal Atresia]] and fetal demise. When methylene blue is administered hours to days before delivery, reported neonatal complications include [[Hyperbilirubinemia]], hemolytic anemia, blue skin discoloration, methemoglobinemia, [[Respiratory Distress]], and [[Photosensitivity]].

Newborns exposed to methylene blue in utero, especially near term, should be closely monitored for these adverse effects. Management is primarily supportive and may include [[Phototherapy]] for hyperbilirubinemia, red blood cell transfusion for hemolytic anemia, supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support for respiratory distress, and protection from light to reduce photosensitivity. Serial laboratory monitoring of bilirubin, hemoglobin, and methemoglobin levels is recommended to guide clinical management.

## Monitoring

Methylene blue is generally well tolerated at therapeutic doses below 2 mg/kg; however, doses exceeding 7 mg/kg are associated with a markedly increased risk of adverse effects. The FDA labeling carries a boxed warning stating that methylene blue may cause serious or fatal serotonin syndrome when used in combination with serotonergic drugs—including SSRIs, SNRIs, and MAOIs—and opioids, and recommends avoiding concomitant use entirely. Caution is also warranted in patients with renal impairment, as methylene blue may reduce renal blood flow.

## Toxicity

Methylene blue demonstrates clear dose-dependent toxicity thresholds. At single doses of 3 mg/kg or more, hypotension, wheezing, and reduced oxygenation have been reported. Cumulative doses of 7 mg/kg or more can cause significant toxicity, including nausea, vomiting, precordial pain, dyspnea, tachypnea, chest tightness, tachycardia, apprehension, tremor, [[Mydriasis]], abdominal pain, dizziness, paresthesia, headache, confusion, and mild methemoglobinemia (up to 7%), with effects lasting 2 to 12 hours. Severe overdosage (single dose of 20 mg/kg or more) has caused severe intravascular hemolysis, hyperbilirubinemia, and death.

> [!danger] Dose-dependent toxicity
> - < 2 mg/kg: generally well tolerated
> - 3 mg/kg single: hypotension, wheezing, reduced oxygenation
> - ≥ 7 mg/kg cumulative: marked adverse-effect risk
> - ≥ 20 mg/kg single: severe hemolysis, hyperbilirubinemia, death

There is no specific antidote for methylene blue toxicity; management is entirely supportive, with close observation and monitoring for cardiopulmonary, hematologic, and neurologic toxicities. Continuous clinical assessment and appropriate supportive interventions should be implemented based on symptom severity and progression.

## Enhancing Healthcare Team Outcomes

Ethically, methylene blue use should reflect beneficence and nonmaleficence—balancing urgency against known risks, avoiding preventable interactions, and ensuring equitable, evidence-informed decisions, especially for off-label indications supported by limited data. Teams should incorporate patient preferences when feasible, document rationale for off-label use, and follow institutional policies for high-alert medications. Care coordination should include discharge and follow-up planning when relevant, such as counseling on avoiding serotonergic agents for the recommended interval, return precautions for delayed hemolysis symptoms, and communication with primary care and specialty teams. Through standardized protocols, medication-safety checks, vigilant monitoring, and closed-loop interprofessional communication, the healthcare team can optimize patient-centered outcomes, enhance safety, and improve overall team performance during methylene blue administration.

Pharmacists play a key role in medication reconciliation, identifying high-risk drug interactions, verifying weight-based dosing, and advising on infusion parameters and alternative therapies when methylene blue is contraindicated. Nursing staff and anesthesia professionals are essential for safe administration, standardized double-checks, and close monitoring for adverse effects, including serotonin syndrome, hemolysis, and hypersensitivity reactions. Laboratory and respiratory therapy teams support accurate assessment by facilitating [[Co-oximetry]] or arterial blood gas analysis when pulse oximetry is unreliable.

Effective communication, clear role delineation, and rapid escalation of concerns are critical to patient safety. Ethical use requires balancing potential benefits against known risks, particularly for off-label indications, and documenting the clinical rationale and patient counseling when feasible. Through structured collaboration, vigilant monitoring, and shared accountability, interprofessional teams enhance patient-centered care, improve outcomes, and minimize preventable harm associated with methylene blue administration.
