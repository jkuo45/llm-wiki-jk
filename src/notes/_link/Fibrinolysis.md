# Fibrinolysis

Fibrinolysis is the enzymatic dissolution of [[Fibrinogen|fibrin]] clots — the physiological counterpart of coagulation, and the reason a clot that has served its purpose does not persist. It is executed by [[Plasmin]], generated from its zymogen [[Plasminogen]], and controlled almost entirely by the balance between its two activators and their single dominant inhibitor.

> [!info] The pathway in one line
> Plasminogen → (tPA, uPA) → plasmin → fibrin → fibrin degradation products, including D-dimer. PAI-1 inhibits the activators, α2-antiplasmin inhibits plasmin directly, and α2-macroglobulin is the final plasma sink.

## The Regulatory Triad

**Activators.**

- [[Tissue Plasminogen Activator]] (tPA) — a serine protease secreted by vascular endothelium. Its activity is highly local: it binds to fibrin through its kringle domains, and fibrin binding allosterically accelerates plasminogen activation, so tPA is preferentially active *on a clot* rather than in free plasma. It also clears circulating plasminogen-activator activity at physiological tPA concentrations.
- Urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) — broadly active, and central to tissue remodelling rather than to vascular emergency thrombolysis.

**The inhibitor that decides everything.**

[[PAI-1]] is the physiological inhibitor of both activators, present in plasma in molar excess over tPA and forming covalent complexes with it. It is produced by endothelium, hepatocytes, adipocytes and muscle, and its level — not the level of tPA — is the dominant determinant of fibrinolytic capacity. PAI-1 rises in obesity, metabolic syndrome, [[Atherosclerosis]], type 2 diabetes, [[Inflammation]] and acute-phase states, which is one of the strongest mechanistic explanations for the prothrombotic phenotype of metabolic disease.

**Direct plasmin inhibition.** α2-antiplasmin inactivates plasmin in circulation, and is consumed when fibrinolysis becomes systemic. That consumption is the basis of the fibrinogen-degradation pattern that distinguishes systemic from localised thrombolysis.

**The fibrin scaffolding effect.** Fibrinogen and fibrin enhance tPA activity against plasminogen while partially protecting tPA from PAI-1 — so the clot is both the substrate and the preferred site of activation.

## Compartmentalisation: The Key Concept

> [!info] Why fibrinolysis is normally invisible
> Physiological fibrinolysis is surface-bound and clot-localised, because tPA is released by endothelium and retained on fibrin. It is only when PAI-1 is exhausted or overwhelmed, or a pharmacological agent is given at high concentration, that plasmin activity becomes free-floating and degrades circulating fibrinogen and other proteins. This distinction is the whole basis of safe thrombolysis.

Thrombolytic agents:

- **tPA (alteplase, reteplase, tenecteplase)** — fibrin-selective, so bleeding risk is lower than with non-selective agents.
- **Urokinase** — non-fibrin-selective.
- **Streptokinase** — bacterial, non-selective, strongly allergenic.

## Clinical Relevance

| Context | Role of fibrinolysis |
|---|---|
| [[Stroke]] | Thrombolysis with tPA within the therapeutic window; alteplase for eligible patients |
| [[Myocardial infarction]] | Reduced fibrinolysis raises infarct size and mortality; PAI-1 activity is a prognostic marker |
| Venous thromboembolism | Impaired fibrinolysis underlies DVT and pulmonary embolism risk; [[D-dimer]] reflects cross-linked fibrin turnover |
| [[Atherosclerosis]] | Local plaque fibrinolysis imbalance contributes to plaque rupture and thrombosis |
| Deep vein thrombosis | Impaired fibrinolysis underlies DVT and pulmonary embolism risk; [[D-dimer]] reflects cross-linked fibrin turnover |
| [[Chronic Kidney Disease]] | Both impaired activation and elevated PAI-1 |
| Cancer | Tumours and stroma co-opt fibrinolytic enzymes for invasion and matrix remodelling |
| [[Cardiac Fibrosis]] and tissue repair | Plasmin is required for collagen turnover, so antifibrinolytics impair scar formation |

> [!warning] Antifibrinolytics as a two-edged intervention
> Tranexamic acid and ε-aminocaproic acid reduce mortality in postpartum haemorrhage and in major trauma, but prolonged suppression of fibrinolysis has been associated with ischaemic and thrombotic complications, including in conditions such as subarachnoid haemorrhage and infective endocarditis. Reduced fibrinolysis is also one proposed contributor to the increased thrombotic risk in [[Long COVID]].
> Uncertain: the extent to which PAI-1 and tPA ratios add prognostic value beyond established thrombotic markers.

## Connections

- [[Plasmin]] — the protease that executes fibrinolysis by degrading fibrin; the functional effector of the whole pathway.
- [[Plasminogen]] — the zymogen substrate of tPA and uPA and the source of extravascular plasminogen used in tissue remodelling.
- [[Tissue Plasminogen Activator]] — the principal physiological activator and the basis of fibrin-selective thrombolytic therapy.
- [[PAI-1]] — the physiological inhibitor of tPA and uPA, and the dominant control point of the system; elevated in metabolic disease and inflammation.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — the pharmacological opposite of fibrinolysis: anticoagulants prevent clot formation, fibrinolytics dissolve clots that have already formed.
- [[Fibrinogen]] — the soluble precursor of the fibrin substrate, consumed by fibrinolysis and therefore a direct readout of systemic activity.
- [[Factor XIIIa]] — crosslinks fibrin to stabilise the clot and paradoxically makes it more resistant to plasmin, defining clot age.
- [[D-dimer]] — the cross-linked fibrin degradation product, and the clinical test that couples fibrinolysis to diagnosis.
- [[Thrombin]] — the coagulation protease whose generation drives fibrin formation and against which fibrinolysis is the counterweight.
- [[Myocardial infarction]] — the clinical setting in which fibrinolytic capacity directly determines infarct size and outcome.
- [[Stroke]] — the principal indication for pharmacological thrombolysis.
- [[Ischemia]] — the tissue consequence of both inadequate fibrinolysis and, conversely, of bleeding induced by excessive activation.
- [[Atherosclerosis]] — the disease in which a local fibrinolytic imbalance within the plaque contributes to rupture and thrombosis.
- [[Inflammation]] — a two-way relation: acute-phase reaction raises PAI-1, which suppresses fibrinolysis and worsens thrombotic outcomes.
- [[Cardiac Fibrosis]] — tissue remodelling in which plasmin-mediated matrix turnover is required, so antifibrinolytics can impair scar formation.
- [[Acute Kidney Injury]] — a state of markedly impaired fibrinolysis and increased PAI-1, relevant to the microvascular thrombosis seen in critical illness.
