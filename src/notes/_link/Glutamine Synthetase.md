# Glutamine Synthetase

Glutamine synthetase (GS) catalyses the ATP-dependent condensation of glutamate with ammonia to yield glutamine, ADP and inorganic phosphate. It is the only enzyme that performs this reaction in mammals, and it operates in three anatomical compartments with different jobs: the cytosol (nitrogen assimilation and nucleotide/nucleotide-sugar synthesis), the mitochondrial matrix (the last step of the [[urea cycle]], where it scavenges the ammonia released by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I), and the astrocyte perinuclear sheath (the brain's ammonia sink and its glutamate-to-glutamine cycle).

> [!info] Mechanism
> The reaction proceeds in two steps through a covalent enzyme intermediate. ATP first phosphorylates the γ-carboxylate of glutamate to form **γ-glutamyl phosphate**; ammonia then attacks that acyl-phosphate to give glutamine and release Pi. ADP and Pi remain bound until ammonia is bound, so the intermediate is not released. A divalent metal ion (Mn²⁺ or Mg²⁺) is required — the low-affinity site stabilises the active site and binds the Mn-ATP chelate. Because the ammonia site is polar and solvent-exposed, the substrate is **ammonium ion, not neutral NH₃**, which is why the enzyme shows a steep pH dependence.

Human GS is a dodecamer of ~373 kDa subunits and is among the most tightly regulated enzymes in metabolism. It is controlled by covalent modification, by subcellular localisation, and by the supply of its own substrates.

> [!info] Regulation
> - **Adenylation** by adenylyl transferase (ATase) adds AMP to a conserved Tyr in a flexible activation loop, opening the active site; deadenylylation closes it. This is the dominant mammalian control point.
> - **Subcellular localisation** matters as much as modification: cytosolic GS turns over rapidly (hours), whereas mitochondrial and astrocytic GS is far more stable. Redistribution of the enzyme between compartments therefore changes whole-body nitrogen flux without changing total GS abundance.
> - GS is itself an oxidative-stress target: the catalytic tyrosine and other residues are susceptible to [[Peroxynitrite|nitration]] and carbonylation, which lowers activity.

## Physiological function

Glutamine is the most abundant free amino acid in the human body and the obligatory nitrogen donor for biosynthesis of purines, pyrimidines, amino sugars, NAD and glutathione. GS supplies it in two ways: as a biosynthetic step, and as a **detoxification step**. In brain, astrocytic GS is the only substantial route for clearing synaptic glutamate (via the glutamate-glutamine cycle) and the main route for ammonia clearance after the rest of the urea cycle machinery is exhausted. Perfusion of the brain with hyperammonemic blood in acute liver failure can overwhelm this capacity, which is the basis of hepatic encephalopathy.

> [!info] Clinical relevance
> - **GLUL deficiency (congenital glutamine deficiency, GLND)** is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive disorder with neonatal encephalopathy, absent psychomotor development, seizures and progressive brain atrophy (OMIM 610015).
> - **Loss of GS in mouse brain** causes a 14-fold fall in cortical glutamine, a 7-fold fall in alanine, and a catastrophic oxidative-stress phenotype with neurodegeneration — GS is required to keep brain glutathione synthesis supplied, and its loss triggers [[Mitochondrial ROS|mitochondrial oxidative stress]] and a ribotoxic stress response.
> - Reduced GS levels or activity have been reported across neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory conditions, though whether this is cause or consequence is not settled.
> - **GS inhibition as therapy.** Glutamine is a fuel for tumour cells, so the classic inhibitors [[Acivicin]] and [[Telatinib]]-derived compounds and the antibiotic [[DON]] (6-diazo-5-oxo-L-norleucine) have been explored as anti-tumour agents. Systemic GS blockade is limited by the same brain ammonia handling described above.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> Because astrocytic GS is the brain's ammonia sink, any strategy that lowers GS activity systemically carries a real risk of hyperammonaemia. Conversely, the enzyme is a tempting target for augmenting ammonia clearance, but simply raising GS activity does not help if the portal supply of ammonia is the limiting factor.

## Connections

- [[Glutamine]] — GS is the enzyme that makes it. Every molecule of free glutamine in the cytosol of a mammalian cell passes through this reaction, and the reverse (glutaminase) reaction is what fuels the [[Glutaminolysis|glutaminase]] step in rapidly dividing cells.
- [[Astrocytes]] — astrocytic GS sits on the inner surface of the astrocyte endfeet facing the capillary and is the rate-limiting step of the glutamate-glutamine cycle; it is also why astrocytic GS immunostaining (GS+ astrocytes) is used as a neuropathological marker.
- [[Peroxynitrite]] — nitration of GS catalytic residues inactivates the enzyme, providing a link between oxidative/nitrosative stress and impaired nitrogen handling.
- [[Glutaminase]] — the opposing arm of the glutamine cycle. GS and glutaminase together form a cycle whose net direction determines whether a cell is using or producing glutamine.
- [[ATP]] — ATP is an obligate substrate; the enzyme couples nitrogen assimilation directly to the cell's energy budget.
- [[Neurodegeneration]] — GS deficiency produces neurodegeneration in both humans and mice, and reduced GS is a recurring finding in neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation.
