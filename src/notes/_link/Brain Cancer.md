# Brain Cancer

Brain cancer covers a heterogeneous set of malignant neoplasms arising inside the
cranial vault (primary brain and CNS tumours) and tumours that spread there from
elsewhere (brain metastases). The two are separated clinically and biologically: a
metastasis is a systemic disease with a CNS endpoint, a primary brain tumour is a
disease of the organ.

## Epidemiology

Primary brain tumours occur in roughly 250,000 people per year worldwide and make up
under 2% of all cancers, but they cause disproportionate mortality. Brain metastases
are about four times as common as primary brain tumours, with roughly half originating
from [[Lung Cancer|lung cancer]]; the remainder come from breast, melanoma, renal and
colorectal primaries. In children under 15, brain and CNS tumours are the second most
common cancer after acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. US five-year survival for all
malignant brain tumours is around 33%.

## Presentation and diagnosis

Symptoms depend on location more than size, and reflect raised intracranial pressure
(focal headache worse in the morning or on Valsalva, vomiting, papilloedema) plus focal
deficits — personality change and executive failure from frontal lesions, aphasia from
dominant-hemisphere lesions, hemianopia from occipital lesions, ataxia from cerebellar
lesions. Seizures are common at presentation, particularly in low-grade cortical
tumours. Diagnosis is by contrast-enhanced [[Magnetic Resonance Imaging|MRI]] with
perfusion and spectroscopy, followed by stereotactic or open biopsy for histologic
grading and molecular profiling (IDH, 1p/19q codeletion, MGMT promoter methylation,
H3K27M, ATRX, TP53, TERT).

> [!info] Molecular classification has replaced histology alone
> The 2016 WHO and 2021 WHO5 updates define glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma
> and ependymoma by integrated histo-molecular criteria. An IDH-mutant diffuse
> astrocytoma with grade 4 histology is now "astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4"
> — a genuinely different prognosis from IDH-wildtype glioblastoma — which is why
> methylation class is now requested on every adult diffuse glioma biopsy.

## Management

Maximum safe resection followed by radiotherapy with concurrent temozolomide
chemotherapy remains the backbone for glioblastoma; MGMT promoter methylation
predicts greater temozolomide benefit. Tumour-treating fields add modestly. Anti-angiogenic
bevacizumab is used for symptom control (oedema, corticosteroid sparing) rather than
for survival. Corticosteroids and, when raised pressure is severe, osmotherapy manage
peritumoural oedema; antiepileptics are used if seizures occur. Immunotherapy has not
delivered the durable responses seen in melanoma or lung cancer, which remains one of
the field's most conspicuous negative results.

## Viral associations

> [!warning] Evidence is contested — do not assert causation
> The vault's [[SV40]] link to brain cancer comes from a decades-old hypothesis
> (brain tumours as childhood orphans of families exposed to contaminated polio vaccine
> batches), supported by early reports of SV40 T-antigen and viral DNA in tumour tissue.
> Larger, better-controlled studies — including careful work using modern viral
> quantification methods — have generally failed to find reproducible SV40 sequence in
> human brain tumours, and major epidemiological reviews do not support a causal link.
> The association should be described as historical and unresolved, not established.
> Simian virus 40 is a real and still-useful tool in the laboratory, and its
> transformation assays (large T antigen inactivating [[p53]] and [[RB1]]) are a
> legitimate reason to keep the note in the graph.

## Connections

- [[SV40]] — Large T antigen of SV40 inactivates p53 and RB1 and immortalises human cells, which is the mechanistic rationale for linking the virus to tumourigenesis. The clinical claim that SV40 causes human brain cancer is not supported by current evidence and should be treated as historical.
- [[Glioblastoma]] — Glioblastoma is the commonest and most lethal primary brain tumour in adults and the entity against which most primary brain cancer therapy research is benchmarked.
- [[Glioma]] — Gliomas comprise the majority of adult primary brain tumours; molecular subclassification (IDH status, 1p/19q codeletion) has become the primary axis of diagnosis and prognosis.
- [[Blood-Brain Barrier]] — The blood–brain barrier is the reason systemic drugs achieve poor intracranial concentrations, restricting chemotherapeutic options and requiring agents that are lipophilic or actively transported.
- [[Lung Cancer]] — Roughly half of all brain metastases originate from lung primaries, making brain surveillance part of lung cancer staging and management.
- [[Metastasis]] — Brain metastasis is four times more common than primary brain tumour, and represents a different problem: systemic disease control plus local therapy, not resection of a confined malignancy.
- [[Ionizing Radiation]] — Ionising radiation is the only established environmental risk factor for primary brain tumours, with risk related to dose and inversely to age at exposure; therapeutic radiation is a secondary risk.
- [[Neurodegeneration]] — Brain cancer is not a degenerative disease, but the two share cell types (neurons, glia), the same microenvironment, and overlapping therapeutics, which is why the vault graph links them.
