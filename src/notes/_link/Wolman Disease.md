# Wolman Disease

**Wolman disease** is the infantile-severity end of the spectrum of **lysosomal acid lipase deficiency (LAL-D)**, an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism caused by biallelic mutations in *LIPA*, which encodes [[Lysosomal Acid Lipase]] (the enzyme historically called LipA in the mouse and sometimes LipA in human literature). Historically the disorder was split into two named diseases — Wolman disease (infants) and cholesteryl ester storage disease (older children and adults) — but since both are the same enzymatic defect, the modern convention is to use LAL-D for both and reserve "Wolman disease" for the infantile presentation.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

> [!info] The enzymology
> LAL is a lysosomal acid lipase (a GDSL-type lipase) that hydrolyzes **cholesteryl esters and triglycerides** arriving in the lysosome by receptor-mediated endocytosis of [[Low-Density Lipoprotein]] and by [[Lipophagy]] of intracellular [[Lipid Droplet|Lipid Droplets]]. Loss of activity means the lysosome cannot clear its lipid cargo. The result is massive accumulation of cholesteryl esters in hepatocytes, macrophages, adrenal cortex, and intestinal mucosa — the bright yellow-orange, lipid-laden tissue seen on biopsy.

Because the defect is in **catabolism of arriving lipid** rather than in synthesis, the phenotype is a combination of:

- **Hepatic steatosis progressing to fibrosis and cirrhosis**, with markedly elevated transaminases
- **Dyslipidemia that mimics familial hypercholesterolemia** — high total and LDL cholesterol, low HDL — because uncleared cholesteryl esters are not returned to plasma efficiently
- **Malabsorption** from lipid-laden intestinal mucosa, with steatorrhea and vomiting
- **Adrenal calcification** in ~50% of affected infants, the classic imaging finding (and the origin of the name via Moshe Wolman's 1956 description of "generalized xanthomatosis with calcified adrenals")

Additional features include anemia, xanthomatous skin deposits (notably periorbital), hepatosplenomegaly, and a persistent low-grade fever.

## Clinical Presentation & Biomarkers

Infants present within weeks to months of birth with feeding difficulty, vomiting, diarrhea, distended abdomen, and failure to thrive. Untreated, the disease is **almost uniformly fatal within 6–12 months** from multiorgan failure. Later-onset patients present with the same features at lower intensity, frequently undiagnosed, and may die young of hepatic failure or premature cardiovascular events — the vascular consequences being a direct extrapolation of the dyslipidemia rather than a separate atherosclerotic process.

Diagnostic workup: elevated transaminases, low HDL with raised LDL and total cholesterol, imaging for adrenal calcification, and liver biopsy showing lipid-laden hepatocytes and Kupffer cells with micro- and macrovesicular steatosis. Only genetic confirmation of *LIPA* is definitive.

## Therapeutic Landscape

- **Enzyme replacement therapy**

> [!info] Approved therapy
> **Sebelipase alfa** (Kanuma; recombinant human LAL) received FDA and EMA approval in 2015 and Japan approval in 2016, and is the first disease-modifying therapy. It is dosed by intravenous infusion — weekly for infants with rapidly progressive disease in the first six months of life, every other week for less aggressive presentations. In the pivotal Phase 3 trial (Burton et al., *NEJM* 2015) infants followed for one year had improved survival and reduced abnormal lipid levels relative to historical controls. Its list price has been reported around US$375,000/year, which is the central access barrier.

- **Dietary and supportive measures** — low-fat or specialized formula, intravenous nutrition, treatment of intercurrent infection, and steroid replacement where adrenal insufficiency is suspected. These were the mainstays before 2015.
- **Statins** lowered cholesterol but did not slow hepatic progression; most patients still required liver transplantation.
- **Hematopoietic stem cell (bone marrow) transplantation** has been attempted experimentally with the rationale that enzyme-replacement-secreting donor monocytes can supply LAL cross-correctorily. Evidence is sparse and complication rates (including graft-versus-host disease and death) have been high, so it is not standard of care.

## Epidemiology

Autosomal recessive, with a carrier frequency giving a 25% recurrence risk per pregnancy for affected parents. Prevalence estimates range from about 1 in 40,000 to 1 in 300,000 depending on population, which almost certainly means **substantial underdiagnosis**. The highest reported incidence is in infants of Iraqi or Iranian Jewish ancestry in Los Angeles (~1 in 4,200), reflecting a founder variant.

## Connections

- [[Lysosomal Acid Lipase]] — This is the causal entity: Wolman disease is simply the null (or near-null) phenotype of LAL deficiency. Understanding the enzyme's normal role in clearing endocytosed cholesteryl esters and [[Lipophagy]]-delivered triglycerides is what explains the entire phenotype, from steatosis to adrenal calcification.
- [[LipA]] — The murine ortholog of *LIPA*; *LipA* knockout mice are the standard model for this disease and underpin most mechanistic work on lysosomal lipid catabolism.
- [[Atg15]] — Lysosomal lipophagy machinery interfaces directly with LAL function: the lipid reaching LAL arrives substantially via autophagic lipophagy, so autophagic flux and LAL activity are mechanistically coupled, and this is why LAL deficiency is discussed in autophagy/lipophagy notes rather than purely in enzyme notes.
- [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases]] — Wolman disease is a lysosomal storage disease in the lipid (rather than sphingolipid) category; the general storage-disease framework — enzyme deficiency, undegraded substrate accumulation, organomegaly, and progressive organ failure — applies directly.
- [[Lipophagy]] — Delivers triglyceride substrate to LAL; a failure of lipophagy produces the same downstream problem (cytosolic and lysosomal lipid overload) by a different route.
- [[Lysosome]] — The site of LAL action and of lipid accumulation; the acidic pH dependence of the enzyme ties its activity to lysosomal maturation.
- [[Lipid Droplet]] — Lipid droplets are both a source of lipophagy substrate and a marker of the pathological lipid overload seen in hepatocytes and adrenal cortex.
- [[Cholesterol]] and [[Low-Density Lipoprotein]] — The cholesterol handling arm of the phenotype: LAL deficiency produces a dyslipidemia that looks like familial hypercholesterolemia but responds to statins only symptomatically.
- [[Liver]] and [[Adrenal gland]] — The two organs that fail fastest: progressive hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis drives the mortality, and the adrenal cortex is where the characteristic calcification appears.
- [[Anemia]] and [[Erythropoietin]] — Anemia is common in LAL-D; the marrow and EPO axis is a plausible downstream contributor, though the vault has no dedicated note on the mechanism and the causal chain is not fully established.
- [[Hyperlipidemia]] and [[Statins]] — Clinically relevant because LAL-D masquerades as a primary lipid disorder; statins lower cholesterol but do not prevent hepatic decompensation.
- [[Autophagy]] — Upstream of lipophagy and lysosomal delivery; the reason this disease belongs in an autophagy vault at all.
