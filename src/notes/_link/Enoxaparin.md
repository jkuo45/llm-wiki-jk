# Enoxaparin

Enoxaparin is the most widely used low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and, in
most health systems, the reference LMWH against which other LMWHs are compared.
It is a mixture of fragments of porcine heparin with a mean molecular weight of
about 4,500 Da, produced by depolymerisation with heparinase and subsequent
fractionation of the resulting low- and high-molecular-weight pools.

## Mechanism of action

Enoxaparin is an indirect anticoagulant. It binds to a conformational
site-specific region of [[Antithrombin III]] and, in doing so, exposes the
reactive arginine, accelerating inactivation of clotting enzymes by roughly
1000-fold.

> [!info] Why LMWHs are Xa-selective
> Antithrombin inhibits thrombin only after thrombin first binds heparin.
> This requires a heparin chain of at least 18 saccharide units. A 4.5 kDa
> fragment is physically too short to bridge antithrombin to thrombin, so
> it retains full anti-Xa activity while having little to no anti-thrombin
> activity. That is the entire pharmacologic difference between LMWH and
> unfractionated [[Heparin]] - chain length.

Two other consequences follow. First, because the drug does not inhibit
[[Thrombin]] well, it cannot be reversed by [[Protamine]] at any dose - a
practical disadvantage in urgent procedures. Second, and the origin of the
class's defining complication, heparin-binding antibodies formed against
heparin- PF4 complexes cross-react with the platelet [[Fibrinogen]] receptor
and cause immune platelet activation.

## Pharmacokinetics

- **Absorption**: subcutaneous, near-complete bioavailability.
- **Peak anti-Xa**: 3-5 hours.
- **Half-life**: approximately 4-5 hours by anti-Xa assay, notably longer by
  thrombin time - a reminder that anti-Xa and anti-IIa assays do not track each
  other for LMWHs.
- **Clearance**: almost entirely renal, via filtration and, for the smaller
  chains, some tubular handling. Elimination half-life lengthens progressively
  with degree of renal impairment and with day of therapy, so accumulation
  develops over the first several days.
- **Not dialysable** - a dose given just before haemodialysis is largely lost.

> [!warning] Clinical caveats
> - **Renal impairment** is the dominant dose-modifying variable. Common
>   practice is to reduce to 1 mg/kg once daily, or to about 50-65% of the
>   full dose, at creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min; dose reduction to
>   roughly 75-85% is used at 30-60 mL/min. Evidence is largely from
>   anti-Xa levels rather than from bleeding or thrombotic outcomes.
> - **Obesity**: dosing on actual body weight with 1 mg/kg twice daily
>   achieves therapeutic trough anti-Xa in most obese patients with normal
>   renal function. Very large patients may need higher doses or trough
>   anti-Xa monitoring.
> - **Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)**: cross-reactivity is lower than
>   with unfractionated heparin but not zero. Prior HIT is a strong reason to
>   choose [[Fondaparinux]] or a direct agent instead.
> - **No routine monitoring** is needed in the average patient. Trough anti-Xa
>   (target < 0.5 IU/mL), drawn 30 minutes before the next dose, is used in
>   renal impairment, extremes of body weight, pregnancy, and unexplained
>   bleeding or thrombosis.
> - **Osteoporosis** with prolonged therapeutic (>3 month) exposure is a real
>   under-recognised cost of the drug.
> - **Pregnancy**: enoxaparin is generally preferred over warfarin because it
>   does not cross the placenta, but it is not licensed for prevention of
>   recurrent pregnancy loss.

## Indications

Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis after surgery or in immobilised medical
patients; treatment of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism (1 mg/kg
twice daily or 1.5 mg/kg once daily); acute coronary syndrome without
ST-elevation; and treatment of acute deep vein thrombosis in cancer, where the
sustained outpatient schedule is the main practical advantage over
unfractionated heparin.

## Connections

- [[Heparin]] — enoxaparin is a defined fragment population derived from
  unfractionated heparin, and inherits both its antithrombin-dependent mechanism
  and its HIT cross-reactivity while losing its anti-thrombin activity.
- [[Antithrombin III]] — the obligatory cofactor; the drug has no direct
  anticoagulant action of its own, and its activity collapses in antithrombin
  deficiency.
- [[Fondaparinux]] — the other indirect factor Xa inhibitor, fully synthetic,
  with essentially no HIT risk and a harder renal profile; the usual
  alternative when enoxaparin is unsuitable.
- [[Warfarin]] — the standard oral anticoagulant for long-term therapy after
  enoxaparin induction; warfarin has no parenteral overlap option, which is
  exactly why enoxaparin is used.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — enoxaparin is a member of this class; note that it is not
  a direct-acting agent and is not reversible with a specific antidote.
- [[Thrombin]] — the enzyme enoxaparin cannot effectively inhibit, the reason
  protamine reversal fails.
- [[Platelet]] — the target of the immune-mediated mechanism behind HIT, which
  enoxaparin can still trigger.
- [[Pulmonary Embolism]] and [[Stroke]] — two of the main treatment
  indications; the class also reduces recurrence after acute coronary
  syndromes.
- [[Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia]] — a class complication; the chain-length
  dependence of PF4 binding explains why risk falls with molecular weight.
