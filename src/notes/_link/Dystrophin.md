# Dystrophin

**Dystrophin** is a 427 kDa, 3,685-residue rod-shaped cytoskeletal protein encoded by the *DMD* gene, the largest human gene (~2.4 Mb, 79 exons) at Xp21. It is a normal constituent of the muscle fibre: a protein located between the sarcolemma and the outermost layer of myofilaments, linking actin filaments to support proteins on the inner membrane surface, which in turn link to proteins in the extracellular matrix.

> [!info] Why the gene is so large
> A large fraction of *DMD* mutations are deletions, and because the gene is so large and lies in a region of high recombination rate on the female X chromosome, deletions (and duplications) predominate over point mutations. This is why genetic counselling in DMD is framed around deletion/duplication genotyping rather than sequencing alone. The gene's size also means that in-frame exon skipping is a viable therapeutic strategy — see below.

## Structure and domains

Dystrophin is modular, with four functional regions:

1. **N-terminal domain.** Binds actin and, critically, couples the sarcolemma to the signalling machinery. It binds [[Akt]]-associated proteins and the protein product of a gene mutated in A-betalipoproteinaemia, anchoring signalling at the membrane.
2. **Central rod domain.** 24 spectrin-like repeats, interrupted by four hinge regions. This is the bulk of the molecule and the source of its flexibility and its ability to act as a mechanical shock absorber between basal lamina and actin cytoskeleton.
3. **C-terminal domain.** Contains the EF hand–like and ZZ domains, both zinc-binding, and binds the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex (DGC).
4. **C-terminal tail.** Binds syntrophins, dystrobrevin and — via a conserved spectrin-repeat site (around repeats 20–21, the nNOS-binding site) — neuronal nitric oxide synthase.

A deletion removing spectrin repeats 17–22 produces the "missing nNOS-binding domain" mutation, which is a distinct disease class because it disconnects NO-mediated signalling without removing the whole protein.

## The dystrophin-glycoprotein complex

Dystrophin does not function alone. It is the organising core of the DGC, an assembly of sarcolemmal proteins and glycoproteins first described by Campbell and demonstrated through the dystrophin-deficient *mdx* mouse:

- **Dystroglycans (α- and β-dystroglycan).** The transmembrane anchor. α-dystroglycan is a heavily glycosylated, laminin-binding extracellular protein whose carbohydrate moieties are essential for ECM binding; glycosylation defects (LARGE, POMGnT1) cause muscular dystrophy without dystrophin loss, which defines a genetically distinct disease group.
- **Sarcoglycans (α, β, γ, δ).** A four-subunit assembly required for stable complex assembly. Mutations cause limb-girdle and other sarcoglycanopathies with a *secondary* dystrophin deficiency.
- **Syntrophins.** Modular membrane adaptors, each with a PDZ domain, a PH domain and a C2 domain, and a ZZ domain binding dystrophin. They recruit signalling proteins — nNOS, [[Calcium]] channels (including aquaporin-4) and kinases — to the membrane, which is what makes the DGC a signal-transduction platform and not just a mechanical anchor.
- **Dystrobrevin**, linking dystrophin to the syntrophins.

## Function

- **Mechanical.** The DGC links basal lamina to actin, spanning the entire myofibre, and functions as a molecular shock absorber. Dystrophin is not the primary load-bearing element — the sarcomere is — but its absence means contraction-induced damage goes unrepaired.
- **Signalling.** Membrane-localised Akt signalling, NO/cGMP signalling via nNOS, and mechanosensing depend on dystrophin anchoring these complexes.
- **Sarcolemma stability.** Dystrophin protects the sarcolemma against contraction-induced tears. In its absence, the membrane becomes permeable to calcium, triggering protease activation, mitochondrial involvement, myonecrosis and cycles of degeneration and regeneration.

## Clinical and pathology

Loss of dystrophin is the primary molecular lesion in [[Muscular Dystrophies (MD)]]:

- **Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD).** Out-of-frame deletions give no functional protein. Onset ages 2–5 years, progressive proximal weakness, loss of ambulation typically in the early teens, dilated cardiomyopathy and respiratory failure, with death usually in the third decade. Calf pseudohypertrophy, Gower's sign and elevated serum creatine kinase (10–100× normal) are characteristic.
- **Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD).** In-frame mutations give a shortened but partly functional protein, with milder, later-onset disease.
- **X-linked dilated cardiomyopathy**, from dystrophin mutations restricted to cardiac tissue.

> [!warning] Clinical caveat
> The genotype–phenotype correlation is imperfect. Estimating severity from mutation class alone (out-of-frame vs in-frame) is unreliable, and modifier genes (and the reading of "pseudo-hypertrophy" as unearned muscle) explain a substantial share of variance between siblings with identical mutations. Inflammatory histopathology drives much of the disease progression, which is why glucocorticoid therapy — targeting inflammation, not the missing protein — has been the standard of care for decades and why gene-based therapies must be assessed on top of that background.

## Therapeutic approaches

- **Exon-skipping antisense oligonucleotides.** [[Eteplirsen]] (skipping exon 51, addressing ~13% of patients) was approved by the FDA in 2016 on the basis of dystrophin levels as a surrogate endpoint; the European Medicines Agency refused it, and the clinical-benefit evidence remains contested. [[Golodirsen]] and [[Viltolarsen]] (exon 53) and [[Casimersen]] (exon 45) followed. Caseimersen carries a thrombotic microangiopathy warning.
- **Micro-dystrophin gene therapy.** Miniaturised transgenes deliver a largely functional protein without a genomic-integration event.
- **Delandistrogene moxeparvovec.** A micro-dystrophin AAV-based therapy with accelerated approval for DMD in ambulatory patients aged 4–5 years and older.
- **Viral vectors, utrophin upregulation, and read-through compounds** remain investigational.
- **GalNAc-conjugated siRNA** approaches to exon skipping are in clinical development.

> [!warning] The ceiling on current therapies
> Exon skipping addresses at most a minority of patients per agent, converts a severe phenotype into a milder rather than a normal one, and requires lifelong weekly injections. Micro-dystrophin AAV therapy is one-time but has transgene-immunity and hepatotoxicity problems and, to date, durability concerns. None of these restores normal dystrophin to every patient, and gene therapy has so far not produced a cure.

## Connections

- [[Muscular Dystrophies (MD)]] — dystrophin is the defining protein of this disease group. The Duchenne/Becker distinction is *quantitative and qualitative* respectively, and the whole exon-skipping therapeutic field is a rationalisation of the reading-frame rule.
- [[Skeletal Muscle]] — the myofibre is where the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex functions, and repeated contraction–induced membrane damage and regeneration failure are the pathological engine in dystrophin loss.
- [[Sarcomere]] — clarification of division of labour: dystrophin is the mechanical tether and shock absorber linking sarcolemma to sarcomere, not a contractile element. Confusing these produced the early "missing contractile protein" models of DMD that have since been superseded.
- [[Laminin]] — laminin-α2 in basal lamina binds α-dystroglycan, completing the mechanical chain extracellular matrix → dystroglycan → dystrophin → actin. Adhesion to the matrix is what prevents the fibre from being torn away on contraction.
- [[Calcium]] — the common final pathway of dystrophin loss. Sarcolemmal tears admit calcium, activating proteases and mitochondrial permeability transition, which is why calpain activation, mitochondrial involvement and necrosis feature so prominently in the pathogenesis.
- [[Akt]] — dystrophin anchors Akt-regulated signalling at the sarcolemma via syntrophins, and this loss contributes to impaired muscle regeneration independently of mechanical fragility.
- [[Nitric Oxide Synthase]] — the nNOS-binding site in dystrophin couples NO production to muscle contraction and exercise hyperaemia; its loss in the missing-exon-20/21 mutants underlies a distinct, NO-deficient disease class.
- [[Sarcoglycanopathies]] — sarcoglycan mutations cause dystrophin *secondary* deficiency, which is why immunohistochemistry for dystrophin must be interpreted alongside sarcoglycan and dystroglycan staining in any dystrophy workup.
- [[Myostatin]] — the key modulator of muscle mass, relevant to the pathological hypertrophy and fibrosis of DMD muscle and to the muscle wasting seen in chronic disease generally.
