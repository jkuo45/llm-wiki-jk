# Aquaporins

**Overview:** Aquaporins (AQPs) are a conserved family of small integral membrane proteins that form selective channels for the rapid, passive movement of water across biological membranes down osmotic gradients. Their discovery (Peter Agre, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2003) explained how epithelia achieve water permeabilities far exceeding simple lipid diffusion.

**Structure and mechanism:** Each aquaporin monomer contains six transmembrane helices surrounding a narrow central pore, and functional channels assemble as tetramers. Two signature asparagine-proline-alanine (NPA) motifs and an aromatic/arginine (ar/R) constriction form the selectivity filter, permitting single-file water transport while excluding protons and other ions—preserving the membrane's electrochemical gradient. A subset called "aquaglyceroporins" (e.g., AQP3, AQP7, AQP9) also conduct glycerol and other small neutral solutes.

**Tissue distribution and physiology:** Thirteen mammalian aquaporins (AQP0–AQP12) show tissue-specific expression. AQP1 mediates fluid handling in the kidney proximal tubule and vasculature; AQP2 in the collecting duct is trafficked to the apical membrane under vasopressin control to concentrate urine; AQP4 dominates in astrocyte end-feet and governs brain water homeostasis; AQP0 maintains lens transparency. They also contribute to secretion, cell migration, and volume regulation across membranes.

**Clinical relevance:** Aquaporin dysfunction underlies several diseases: mutations in AQP2 cause nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, AQP0 mutations cause congenital cataract, and autoantibodies against AQP4 define neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder. AQP4 also influences edema formation after stroke and traumatic brain injury, and several AQPs are implicated in tumor angiogenesis and metastasis, making them therapeutic targets.
