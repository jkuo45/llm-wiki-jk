# Gout

**Etiology and pathophysiology:** [[Gout]] is a metabolic inflammatory arthritis driven by persistent hyperuricemia—elevated [[Uric Acid]] in blood—which promotes the crystallization of monosodium urate (MSU) in joints, cartilage, and soft tissue. Hyperuricemia arises from increased purine turnover (diet, cell turnover, myeloproliferative disease), overproduction via [[Xanthine Oxidase]], or reduced renal excretion (kidney disease, diuretics, genetics). MSU crystals are phagocytosed by [[Macrophages]] and neutrophils, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and releasing IL-1β, which ignites acute [[Inflammation]].

**Clinical presentation and biomarkers:** Attacks present as sudden, intensely painful, red, swollen monoarthritis—classically the first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra). Tophi, urate deposits in chronic disease, cause deformities. Diagnosis combines clinical picture, serum urate, and identification of crystals in synovial fluid; imaging (ultrasound, dual-energy CT) detects deposits.

**Therapeutic landscape:** Acute flares are treated with NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids. Urate-lowering therapy—[[Xanthine Oxidase]] inhibitors (allopurinol, febuxostat) or uricosurics (probenecid)—prevents recurrence. Lifestyle measures address [[Obesity]], alcohol, fructose, and purine intake. Comorbidities include [[Metabolism|metabolic syndrome]] and cardiovascular disease.
