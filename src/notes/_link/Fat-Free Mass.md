# Fat-Free Mass

**Fat-Free Mass (FFM)**, also termed lean body mass, is the mass of all body components excluding stored fat: [[Skeletal Muscle|skeletal muscle]], bone, internal organs, and connective tissue. Because metabolically active tissues (muscle, liver, brain, kidneys) dominate energy turnover, FFM is the principal determinant of [[Resting Metabolic Rate|resting metabolic rate]] (RMR), explaining between 50 and 70 % of the inter-individual variation in RMR (Westerterp _et al_. 1992; Heshka _et al_. 1993; Albu _et al_. 1997; Zhang _et al_. 2002).

## Relation to Resting Metabolic Rate

- RMR scales strongly with FFM: a regression of RMR on fat-free mass shows that two individuals of identical FFM can still differ in residual RMR by up to 3 MJ/d, so FFM is necessary but not sufficient to predict resting metabolism (Weyer _et al_. 2000).
- The metabolic intensity of the tissues comprising FFM is not uniform: training can raise organ- and muscle-specific metabolic output (e.g., via induction of aerobic enzymes), so RMR can change per unit FFM.

> [!info] Source: [[_document_ - speakman2003_Resting Metabolic Rate|Speakman & Selman (2003)]]
> Exercise training generally increases RMR in animals despite declining body and fat mass, attributed to both increases in lean tissue and higher metabolic output per unit lean tissue. In the Byrne & Wilmore (2001) human resistance/aerobic trial, however, RMR declined even though participants gained ~1·6 kg fat-free mass — showing training intensity/overload can override the FFM effect.

## Clinical Relevance

Preserving FFM during weight loss (diet + exercise vs. diet alone) protects against the fall in RMR that otherwise accompanies caloric restriction, which is why resistance training is emphasised for weight-loss maintenance. FFM is routinely estimated by bioelectrical impedance, DXA, and air-displacement plethysmography.
