# NSAIDs

**NSAIDs** (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) are among the most widely used drugs in the world — more than 30 million people take them daily for headache, arthritis and other pain. They share analgesic, antipyretic and anti-inflammatory activity through a single mechanism: inhibition of **[[Cyclooxygenase]]**, the rate-limiting enzyme that converts arachidonic acid into the prostaglandin endoperoxide PGH2 and thence into bioactive prostanoids — PGI2 (prostacyclin), PGD2, [[Prostaglandin E2]], PGF2α and thromboxane A2. Vane and Piper established this in 1971; the COX isoforms were separated in 1976 and the COX-2 gene cloned in 1991.

## Mechanism of Action

The therapeutic/adverse split is conventionally assigned to the two isoforms, though this framing is now considered an oversimplification:

- **COX-1 is constitutively expressed** and generates the "housekeeping" prostanoids: gastric mucosal protection, renal afferent arteriolar vasodilation, and platelet thromboxane A2.
- **COX-2 is inducible**, upregulated by cytokines and inflammatory mediators, and generates the prostanoids mediating pain, inflammation and fever.
- Consequently, **COX-1 inhibition → GI and renal toxicity**, while **COX-2 inhibition → anti-inflammatory benefit**. The once-hoped-for "COX-2 hypothesis" (a clean therapeutic separation) proved false: COX-2-selective agents retain the anti-inflammatory effect but shifted the prostacyclin/thromboxane balance.

Most NSAIDs inhibit both isoforms reversibly. **[[Aspirin]] is the exception**, acetylating the COX-1 active site irreversibly; because platelets cannot resynthesise COX, its antiplatelet effect lasts the platelet's ~7–10 day lifespan, which is why low-dose (75–150 mg) aspirin is an antiplatelet agent rather than an analgesic.

> [!info] The prothrombotic mechanism
> Haemostasis depends on the balance between endothelial **prostacyclin** (vasodilator, platelet-inhibitory, natriuretic — largely COX-2-derived) and platelet **thromboxane A2** (vasoconstrictor, pro-aggregatory, pro-inflammatory — COX-1-derived). Inhibiting COX-2 without blocking platelet COX-1 tips this balance towards thrombosis. This is the mechanistic reason coxibs failed, and it also explains why non-selective agents with strong COX-2 inhibition (notably **diclofenac**) carry similar vascular risk.

## Adverse Effects & Clinical Use

**Gastrointestinal.** Endoscopic ulcers occur in up to 40% of chronic users, though most never become clinically significant. Symptomatic ulcers and ulcer complications run at 2–4% annually in average-risk users and up to 10% in high-risk patients; serious complications (haemorrhage, perforation, death) aggregate to ~2%/year. Risk factors: high dose, older age, *H. pylori*, prior ulcer, and concomitant low-dose aspirin, anticoagulants, SSRIs or corticosteroids. COX-2 selectivity gives a real 2–3-fold vs 3–4-fold reduction in upper GI complications — but concomitant low-dose aspirin cancels that advantage (RR 1.9 vs 0.6 for a COX-2 agent alone). Proton-pump-inhibitor co-therapy is an alternative with comparable GI safety. Notably, 75% of NSAID users develop lower GI adverse events, and PPIs do not protect the small bowel.

**Renal.** Loss of prostaglandin-mediated afferent arteriolar dilation reduces glomerular filtration, impairs natriuresis, and antagonises the haemodynamic benefit of ACE inhibitors. Consequences: fluid retention, hypertension, acute kidney injury, and precipitation of volume overload in heart failure. Avoid in creatinine clearance <30 mL/min.

**Cardiovascular.** Increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, thromboembolism, [[Atrial Fibrillation]] and heart failure. Risk peaks early — myocardial infarction risk has been observed with as little as **7 days** of use, and remains elevated for weeks after discontinuation — so there is no safe window by duration alone. Nearly all non-aspirin NSAIDs roughly double heart failure risk. Individual agents differ: diclofenac carries the highest reported CV risk; naproxen shows the lowest and is the recommended NSAID where one is needed in a patient with CV risk. The 2016 **PRECISION** trial found moderate-dose celecoxib non-inferior to ibuprofen and naproxen for CV safety, and low-dose celecoxib (400 mg/day) showed non-significant risk in pooled analysis. Risk is further increased by tobacco, alcohol and baseline CV risk factors.

**Other.** Hepatic toxicity, and CNS effects including drowsiness, confusion and tinnitus.

> [!warning] Practical guidance
> Professional guidelines recommend the **lowest effective dose for the shortest duration**, avoidance where possible in patients with hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, angina, oedema, recent bypass surgery or prior MI, and use only when other analgesia is insufficient. Use [[Aspirin]] (not another NSAID) if the indication is vascular. Beware two interactions: **ibuprofen and naproxen can occupy the COX-1 site and reduce aspirin's irreversible antiplatelet effect**, and combining NSAIDs with anticoagulants, SSRIs or glucocorticoics multiplies bleeding risk.

## Connections
- [[Cyclooxygenase]] — the inhibited enzyme; NSAID pharmacology is entirely a story of differentially inhibiting the COX-1 and COX-2 isoforms, which is where both the benefits and the toxicities come from.
- [[Prostaglandin E2]] — the prostanoid whose suppression produces analgesia and inflammation control; this is the direct link from NSAID mechanism to the vault's prostaglandin notes.
- [[COX-2]] — the inducible isoform that COX-2-selective NSAIDs target, and the reason coxibs were developed and then restricted for cardiovascular rather than gastrointestinal reasons.
- [[Aspirin]] — the one irreversible member of the class, whose antiplatelet duration outlasts dosing because platelets cannot resynthesise COX-1; also the agent with a net *cardiovascular benefit* at low dose, opposite to the class effect.
- [[Anti-inflammatory]] — NSAIDs are the pharmacological mainstay of anti-inflammatory therapy, alongside corticosteroids, DMARDs and biologics.
- [[Inflammation]] — the process being suppressed, through COX-2-dependent prostanoid generation in inflamed tissue.
- [[Colorectal Cancer]] — [[Aspirin]] has chemopreventive benefit here, and the class appears as chemoprevention in the colorectal neoplasia pathway.
- [[Chronic Kidney Disease]] — a contraindication context: prostaglandin-dependent renal perfusion means NSAIDs accelerate decline, which is why they are avoided in reduced eGFR.
- [[Myocardial infarction]] — the most serious class cardiovascular harm, with elevated risk detectable within the first week of use.
- [[Stroke]] — a class thrombotic risk alongside myocardial infarction, the two sharing the prostacyclin/thromboxane imbalance mechanism.
- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] — NSAID use is associated with increased AF risk, adding a second cardiovascular mechanism to the vascular one.
- [[Cancer]] — COX-2 overexpression is a feature of many tumours and the rationale for coxib use in FAP chemoprevention, so the class sits in cancer biology as well as analgesia.
- [[p53]] — NSAIDs have reported chemopreventive and pro-apoptotic effects mediated in part through COX-independent p53-dependent pathways, a mechanism distinct from prostaglandin synthesis.
