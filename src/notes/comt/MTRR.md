# MTRR

**[[MTRR]]** (methionine synthase reductase) is a flavoprotein reductase that sustains the activity of [[Methionine Synthase]] ([[MTR]]), the [[Vitamin B12]]-dependent enzyme that remethylates [[Homocysteine]] to [[Methionine]]. Without continuous reduction by MTRR, the cobalt center of MTR's methylcobalamin cofactor becomes oxidized to an inactive form, halting methyl transfer.

## Mechanism of Action

MTRR uses FAD and NADPH to reduce the oxidized (Co²⁺/Co³⁺) form of methionine synthase back to its active Co¹⁺ state, allowing the enzyme to accept a methyl group from [[Methylfolate]] and transfer it to homocysteine. This reductase step is essential because every catalytic cycle of MTR carries a risk of oxidative inaccivation of the B12 cofactor; MTRR "rescues" the enzyme, preventing futile cycling and preserving methylation flux.

## Role in the Methylation Cycle

By keeping [[Methionine Synthase]] functional, [[MTRR]] ensures a steady supply of [[Methionine]] and, downstream, [[S-Adenosylmethionine]] ([[SAMe]]) — the universal methyl donor. SAMe is consumed by [[COMT]] ([[Catechol-O-methyltransferase]]) to O-methylate [[Dopamine]], [[Epinephrine]], and [[Norepinephrine]]. MTRR activity therefore indirectly governs the methyl-group availability that sets the pace of catecholamine clearance in the [[Prefrontal Cortex]] and [[Striatum]].

## Genetic Variants

The common MTRR A66G (Ile22Met) polymorphism reduces reductase efficiency and is associated with elevated homocysteine, particularly when compounded by [[MTHFR]] or [[MethylB12]] insufficiency. Genotyping MTRR helps identify individuals who benefit from active B12 forms ([[MethylB12]], [[Hydroxocobalamin]]) and who may be vulnerable to methylation deficits that exacerbate slow-[[COMT]] sensitivity.

## Clinical and Research Relevance

MTRR dysfunction contributes to hyperhomocysteinemia, a risk factor for cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline, and to impaired fetal development. In psychiatric contexts, reduced MTRR activity can lower SAMe and worsen COMT-dependent catecholamine dysregulation implicated in [[Schizophrenia]] and mood disorders.
