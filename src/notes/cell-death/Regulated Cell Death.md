## Overview

Regulated cell death (RCD) encompasses genetically encoded pathways by which cells die in a controlled manner, in contrast to unregulated necrotic death. Major forms include [[Apoptosis]], [[Necroptosis]], [[Pyroptosis]], [[Autophagic Cell Death]], and [[Ferroptosis]], each with distinct molecular machinery and physiological functions.

## Ferroptosis

[[Ferroptosis]] is a regulated, iron-dependent cell death pathway in which cells lose their ability to regulate fats and eventually die, distinct from unregulated necrosis or other programmed death programs. It was discovered and named (originally "oxytosis") by Pam Maher in 2001. The hallmark mechanism is accumulation of lipid peroxides to toxic levels, normally kept in check by [[Glutathione]] via glutathione peroxidase 4.

> [!info] Druggable and regulatable
> Because ferroptosis is druggable and genetically regulatable, it has become a promising therapeutic target alongside cellular senescence for supporting healthy aging and clearing dysfunctional cells.

## Context & Clinical Relevance

RCD is increasingly linked to neurodegeneration and age-related diseases. The relationship between cell death programs and [[Cellular Senescence]] is central to tissue homeostasis: senescence arrests potentially damaged cells, while regulated death clears them. In cancer, evading RCD is a hallmark, and reactivating death programs — through BH3 mimetics, necroptosis inducers, or ferroptosis sensitizers — is a major therapeutic strategy.

## Therapeutic Directions

The druggability of ferroptosis (e.g., via GPX4 or system xc− inhibition) and its interplay with senescence and immunotherapy make it an active area of translational research for clearing dysfunctional cells in aging and disease.
