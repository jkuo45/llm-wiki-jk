## Overview

A single nucleotide variation (SNV) is a variation at a single nucleotide position in the genome — the most common type of genetic variation, occurring roughly every few hundred bases. When a SNV reaches a frequency of at least 1% in a population, it is conventionally termed a single nucleotide polymorphism ([[SNP]]).

## Types & Effects

Most SNVs are neutral, falling in non-coding or degenerate positions. Functional variants can, however, alter protein function, [[Gene Expression|gene expression]], or drug response. A paradigmatic example is the [[COMT]] Val158Met ([[Val158Met]]/rs4680) variant, which changes an amino acid in catechol-O-methyltransferase and modulates enzyme activity.

> [!info]
> SNPs such as COMT Val158Met can alter protein function, gene expression, or drug response, making them the primary markers used in pharmacogenetic and behavioral genetics studies.

## Applications

SNVs are the foundation of modern association studies, including genome-wide association studies (GWAS) linking variants to disease risk, and pharmacogenetics relating variants to drug metabolism and efficacy. Because SNVs are inherited as [[Genotype|genotypes]], they are stable individual-level biomarkers usable across the lifespan.

## Relevance to Epigenetics

While SNVs are sequence-level variations, they interact with the [[Epigenome]]: CpG-containing variants can create or destroy methylation sites (CpG-SNPs), and genotype can influence local DNA methylation. This makes SNVs relevant to interpreting epigenetic marks and epigenetic-clock associations.
