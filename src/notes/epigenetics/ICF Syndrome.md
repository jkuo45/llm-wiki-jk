## Overview

ICF syndrome (Immunodeficiency, Centromere instability, and Facial anomalies) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder most often caused by mutations in the [[DNMT3b]] gene, which encodes a de novo [[DNA Methylation|DNA methyltransferase]]. The defining feature is impaired de novo methylation during development, with the most severe epigenetic consequences occurring at repetitive DNA.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

Loss of DNMT3b activity leads to hypomethylation of [[Pericentromeric Satellite Repeats]] and juxtacentromeric regions. Because these repeats normally carry dense methylation required for constitutive [[Heterochromatin]] formation, their demethylation results in centromeric decondensation, chromosome instability, and characteristic chromosomal abnormalities in stimulated lymphocytes — including multibranched chromosomes and premature centromere separation.

> [!important]
> ICF syndrome is a natural experiment demonstrating that DNMT3b-mediated methylation of pericentromeric satellites is essential for centromere stability and normal immune function.

## Clinical Presentation

The disease manifests as combined immunodeficiency with reduced or absent immunoglobulin levels (hypogammaglobulinemia), recurrent infections, facial dysmorphism, and developmental delay. Variable features include growth retardation and neurological impairment. Centromeric instability is visible in cultured lymphocytes and is a diagnostic hallmark.

## Therapeutic Landscape

Treatment is largely supportive: immunoglobulin replacement therapy and infection prophylaxis. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation has been attempted with variable success. Because the epigenetic defect is intrinsic, no curative therapy currently exists.
