# G1 Phase

The [[G1 Phase]] (Gap 1 phase) is the first gap phase of the [[Cell Cycle]] in eukaryotic cell division. Following mitosis and cytokinesis (or exit from [[Quiescence]]/G0), the cell enters G1, grows in size, synthesizes RNAs and proteins, and monitors both its external environment and internal state to decide whether to commit to another round of division. G1 is the principal period during which the cell assesses nutrient availability, growth-factor signaling, DNA integrity, and cell–cell contacts before passing the [[Restriction Point]] (R point) and becoming irreversibly committed to DNA replication in S phase.

## Regulation & the Restriction Point

Progression through G1 is driven by [[Cyclin-Dependent Kinase]] (CDK) complexes, principally cyclin D–CDK4/6 and cyclin E–CDK2. Mitogenic signals activate these complexes, which phosphorylate the [[RB1]] (retinoblastoma) tumor suppressor. In its hypophosphorylated state, RB1 binds and represses E2F transcription factors, withholding expression of S-phase genes. Phosphorylation by cyclin D–CDK4/6 progressively inactivates RB1, releasing E2F to induce cyclin E and the transcription program required for S-phase entry. Once the R point is passed, CDK activity becomes self-sustaining and largely independent of external cues.

## Checkpoints & Surveillance

G1 contains a key DNA-damage checkpoint. Stress or genome damage activates [[p53]], which induces p21^CIP1^, a CDK inhibitor that halts cyclin–CDK activity and arrests the cell, allowing repair or triggering [[Senescence]]/apoptosis. Measurement of cell size and biomass via nutrient-sensing pathways (mTOR, AMPK) likewise gates G1 progression, linking metabolism to proliferation.

## Physiological Function

G1 is the dominant phase of the somatic cell cycle and the principal point of entry into [[Quiescence]] (G0), a reversible arrest state. Differentiated tissues (e.g., hepatocytes, lymphocytes) reside in G0 and can re-enter G1 in response to injury or mitogenic stimulation. Stem and [[Progenitor Cell]] populations modulate G1 length to balance self-renewal against differentiation; asymmetric division and fate specification are often coupled to G1 duration.

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

Deregulation of G1 control is a hallmark of [[Cancer]]. Loss of [[RB1]], overexpression of cyclin D or CDK4, amplification of upstream receptor signaling, and inactivation of CDK inhibitors (p16^INK4a^, p21, p27) all drive unrestrained G1 progression. Conversely, pharmacological CDK4/6 inhibitors (e.g., palbociclib) exploit G1 dependence to arrest tumor cells, a validated strategy in breast cancer. G1 length is also a focus in cellular reprogramming and senescence research, where prolonged G1 favors pluripotency and stress-induced arrest respectively.
