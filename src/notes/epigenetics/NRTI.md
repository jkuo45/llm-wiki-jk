## Overview

Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) are a class of drugs developed as antiretroviral agents for [[HIV]], targeting the viral reverse transcriptase. They are chain-terminating nucleoside analogs that are incorporated into nascent DNA by reverse transcriptase, halting synthesis of the cDNA copy.

## Mechanism

Beyond their antiviral role, NRTIs also inhibit the reverse transcription of endogenous retroelements, notably [[LINE-1]] and [[HERV]]. When these elements are derepressed during aging — due to loss of [[DNA Methylation]] and [[Heterochromatin]] — their RNA is reverse transcribed into cDNA in the cytoplasm. NRTIs block this step, preventing the generation of the cytoplasmic cDNA that otherwise activates the [[cGAS]]-[[STING]] pathway.

> [!tip]
> By blocking LINE-1 and HERV reverse transcription, NRTIs reduce the cGAS-STING-dependent inflammation and [[SASP]] that arise from retrotransposon derepression during aging. They are therefore being explored as repurposed anti-aging and senomorphic interventions.

## Applications

- **HIV therapy** — established antiretroviral backbone drugs (e.g., [[Lamivudine]]).
- **Aging research** — experimental suppression of retrotransposon-driven inflammation in senescence, with evidence of reduced SASP and improved aging-related phenotypes in models.
- **Potential senomorphic therapy** — reducing chronic inflammation ([[Inflammaging]]) without killing senescent cells.

## Considerations

NRTIs carry mitochondrial toxicity risks and off-target effects that must be weighed in long-term aging applications. Whether partial inhibition of retrotransposition is sufficient and safe for healthy aging remains an open clinical question.
