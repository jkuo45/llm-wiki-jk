# 5-Azacytidine

**5-Azacytidine** (5-aza-CR; trade name Vidaza) is a ribose nucleoside analog of cytidine that functions as a dual [[DNA Methylation|DNA methyltransferase (DNMT)]] inhibitor and [[RNA]] incorporation agent. It is the prototypical [[Epigenetic Modifiers|epidrug]] and a first-line therapy for [[Myelodysplastic Syndrome|myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)]]. Beyond its epigenetic effects, it is also a potent inducer of [[Stress-Induced Premature Senescence]] in vitro.

## Mechanism of Action

The therapeutic efficacy of 5-Azacytidine arises from its ability to interfere with both DNA and RNA metabolism through its incorporation as a nucleotide analog.

1. **DNA Methylation Inhibition**: 5-Azacytidine is phosphorylated by [[Uridine-cytidine kinase]] to its triphosphate form. During [[DNA Replication]], it is incorporated into the nascent DNA strand in place of cytidine. When the enzyme [[DNMT1]] attempts to methylate the DNA at these sites, it forms a covalent bond with the 5-azacytidine residue. This interaction irreversibly traps the DNMT enzyme, leading to its depletion via proteasomal degradation. As the cell undergoes subsequent divisions, the lack of available DNMT leads to passive [[DNA Demethylation]] at CpG sites, potentially reactivating silenced genes.

2. **RNA Incorporation and Epigenetic Modulation**: Unlike its deoxyribose counterpart [[Decitabine]], 5-Azacytidine is also incorporated into [[RNA]] during transcription. This incorporation disrupts various aspects of RNA metabolism, including:
    - Inhibition of [[tRNA]] methylation, which can impact translation efficiency.
    - Impairment of [[rRNA]] processing.
    - Alteration of [[mRNA]] splicing patterns.
    These RNA-level effects contribute to the overall cytotoxicity and may play a role in its unique epigenetic profile.

3. **Induction of Senescence and DNA Damage**: The incorporation of 5-Azacytidine into DNA can trigger a [[DNA Damage Response]] due to replication fork stalling at the trapped DNMT-DNA adducts. This stress, combined with the re-expression of hypermethylated tumor suppressor genes (such as [[CDKN2B|p15^INK4b^]] and [[CDH1]]), can drive cells into [[Stress-Induced Premature Senescence]]. This senescence is often characterized by the elaboration of a [[SASP|Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype]] (Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype), including the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines like [[IL-6]] and [[IL-8]].

## Clinical Use

5-Azacytidine is primarily indicated for the treatment of high-risk MDS:

- **Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS)**: The FDA-approved regimen involves 75 mg/m² administered via subcutaneous or intravenous injection daily for 7 consecutive days in a 28-day cycle. It is particularly effective in patients with [[TET2]] mutations.
- **Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML)**: It is also used in certain subtypes of AML, often in combination with other agents.

The clinical response in MDS involves the reactivation of silenced tumor suppressor genes, which can lead to improved hematopoiesis or the induction of terminal differentiation and senescence in leukemic cells.

## Limitations and Resistance

Several factors limit the clinical utility and contribute to resistance against 5-Azacytidine:

- **Chemical Instability**: 5-Azacytidine is highly unstable in aqueous solutions, with a half-life of approximately 4 hours at 37°C and pH 7.4. This requires it to be freshly prepared for clinical administration.
- **Metabolic Inactivation**: It is rapidly inactivated in the plasma by [[Cytidine deaminase]] (CDA), with a half-life of approximately 20 minutes.
- **Resistance Mechanisms**: Resistance can arise through the downregulation of the activating enzyme [[Deoxycytidine kinase]] (dCK), the upregulation of [[Cytidine deaminase]] (CDA), or compensatory methylation by [[DNMT3a]] and [[DNMT3b]].
