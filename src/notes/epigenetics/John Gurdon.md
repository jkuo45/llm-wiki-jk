# John Gurdon

**Sir John Bertrand Gurdon** (2 October 1933 – 7 October 2025) was a British
developmental biologist at the University of Oxford and the Gurdon Institute at
Cambridge. His nuclear transplantation work established that cell specialisation is
reversible at the level of the genome, and he shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in
Physiology or Medicine with [[Shinya Yamanaka]] "for the discovery that mature,
differentiated cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent".

## The Experiments

- **Background** — Gurdon trained in zoology at Christ Church, Oxford, and completed a
  DPhil with Michael Fischberg on nuclear transplantation in *Xenopus* frogs. Earlier
  attempts by Briggs and King in *Rana pipiens* had failed to produce normal development
  from transplanted adult-cell nuclei; Gurdon chose *Xenopus laevis*, which grows fast
  and supports an unambiguous cell-tracing system for distinguishing donor-derived from
  host-derived tissue.
- **The key result** — Following the first successful cloning of a vertebrate from an
  adult somatic cell in 1958, Gurdon transplanted nuclei from *differentiated* tadpole
  intestinal epithelium into enucleated eggs and obtained a small number of swimming,
  normally structured tadpoles (1962). **Serial transplantation** — successive rounds of
  nuclear transfer — raised the efficiency substantially, showing that a large fraction of
  intestinal epithelial nuclei still contain the complete genetic instructions for
  building an entire organism.
- **The conclusion that changed biology** — Differentiation does not involve loss of
  genetic material. Specialised cells keep the same genome; their nuclei merely sit in a
  state that an oocyte cytoplasm can reset. This overturned the prevailing assumption
  that specialised cell fates were permanently locked, and it is the conceptual
  foundation of [[Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer]].

## Scientific Impact

- **Nuclear reprogramming precedes pluripotency** — Gurdon's demonstration that egg
  cytoplasm can erase a differentiated nucleus supplied the mechanistic rationale that
  [[Yamanaka Factors|transcription factors]] later exploited directly in
  [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]]. His Nobel Lecture was titled "The Egg and the
  Nucleus: A Battle for Supremacy".
- **Practical consequences** — The work birthed modern cloning and stem cell biology, and
  ultimately contributed to [[Regenerative Medicine]], including clinical [[Disease
  Modeling]] and patient-specific pluripotent lines.
- **Controversy and ethics** — Gurdon publicly defended the scientific legitimacy of
  nuclear transplantation while insisting that therapeutic cloning, which does not
  involve creating an individual, should not be treated as morally equivalent to
  reproductive cloning.

## Recognition

Fellow of the Royal Society (1971), Royal Medal (1985), knighted in 1995, Copley Medal
(2003), Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research (2009), and Master of Magdalene
College, Cambridge (1995–2002). The Nobel Prize in 2012 made him, at 79, the oldest
recipient of the physiology prize at that time. He died on 7 October 2025, aged 92.

## Connections
- [[Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer]] — Gurdon's 1962 *Xenopus* transplantations are the
  foundational demonstration of the technique; everything from Dolly onward depends on
  his finding that a differentiated nucleus retains totipotency once placed in an oocyte.
- [[Shinya Yamanaka]] — Shared the 2012 Nobel Prize. Yamanaka's transcription-factor
  reprogramming is the chemical and molecular counterpart of what Gurdon achieved by
  transferring nuclei into egg cytoplasm.
- [[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells]] — iPSC technology is the practical descendant of
  Gurdon's proof: if the differentiated genome is intact, pluripotency should be
  reachable by resetting the nucleus.
- [[Reprogramming]] — Nuclear transfer was the first demonstration that differentiated
  cellular identity is plastic rather than terminal, making it the founding experiment
  of modern reprogramming research.
- [[Pluripotency]] — Gurdon's serial-transplant frogs were whole organisms built from a
  specialised donor nucleus, demonstrating the genomic basis of pluripotency decades
  before embryonic stem cell lines existed.
- [[Kazutoshi Takahashi]] — As first author of the Yamanaka iPSC papers, Takahashi is
  credited as co-discoverer of the transcription-factor route that completes the argument
  Gurdon began.
