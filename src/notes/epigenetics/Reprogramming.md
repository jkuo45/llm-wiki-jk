# Reprogramming

**Reprogramming** is the experimental resetting of a cell's epigenetic state and identity. It ranges from complete nuclear reprogramming — [[Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer]] (SCNT), where an oocyte erases somatic identity to restore totipotency — through factor-driven reprogramming to pluripotency ([[Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells|iPSCs]], the [[OSKM]] factors of [[Shinya Yamanaka]]), to **partial reprogramming**, which interrupts the process early enough to erase epigenetic age marks without losing cell identity.

## The three regimes

1. **Complete reprogramming (SCNT)**: John Gurdon's 1962 frog cloning and Dolly the sheep (1996) proved the differentiated genome remains totipotent — the conceptual foundation for everything that followed. SCNT remains the gold standard for developmental reprogramming studies and for patient-genetically-matched pluripotent lines without exogenous-factor integration.
2. **Pluripotent reprogramming (iPSCs)**: transient OSKM expression erases identity and yields induced pluripotent stem cells — enabling patient-specific [[Disease Modeling]], human-relevant [[Drug Discovery]], and regenerative applications (the first iPSC clinical trial for macular degeneration, led by Masayo Takahashi). Reprogramming kinetics, the role of the [[Epigenetic Clock|epigenetic reset]], and barrier factors (p53, epigenomic roadblocks) are active mechanistic fronts.
3. **Partial reprogramming**: cyclic, short OSKM pulses in vivo reverse epigenetic age without teratoma risk or identity loss — the translational arm linking reprogramming to [[Rejuvenation]] and the [[Information Theory of Aging]].

## Why reprogramming matters for aging biology

Reprogramming is the only intervention that demonstrably resets multiple aging markers simultaneously: DNA methylation age (clock reversal), chromatin organization, mitochondrial function, and senescence burden all return toward youthful states in partially reprogrammed tissues. It is also the proof that aging is substantially **epigenetic** — information loss rather than damage accumulation — which is why the [[ICE Mouse]] experiments and reprogramming share a mechanistic frame.
