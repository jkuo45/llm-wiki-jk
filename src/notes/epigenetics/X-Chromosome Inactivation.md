## Overview

X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) is the epigenetic process by which female (XX) cells transcriptionally silence one of the two X chromosomes, ensuring equal X-linked gene dosage between sexes. It is random and clonally heritable, meaning each cell and its descendants inactivate the same X chromosome.

## Mechanism

The primary driver is [[XIST]], a long [[Non-coding RNA|non-coding RNA]] expressed from the future inactive X. XIST coats the inactive X chromosome and recruits chromatin-modifying enzymes that establish repressive marks, compacting the chromosome into a transcriptionally silent Barr body.

> [!info]
> This silencing is heritable through cell divisions, making XIST a paradigm for long-range, RNA-mediated [[Gene Silencing|epigenetic gene silencing]].

The process is reinforced by several layers: recruitment of [[Polycomb Group Proteins]] (including [[EZH2]]) that deposit H3K27me3, accumulation of [[macroH2A]] histone variants, and DNA methylation at CpG islands of X-linked genes.

## Role in Aging & Reprogramming

XCI erodes with age in some tissues — for example, the inactive X can partially reactivate or lose silencing of escape genes — contributing to age-related transcriptional noise. XCI state is also a sensitive marker of cellular epigenetic identity and is perturbed during reprogramming and in cancer.

## Clinical Relevance

Skewed XCI underlies X-linked disease manifestation in female carriers, and loss of XCI maintenance is observed in aging and malignancy. XIST-based silencing is also a research tool for dosage-compensation studies and therapeutic gene silencing.
