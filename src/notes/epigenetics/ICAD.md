## Overview

ICAD (also known as DFF45) is the inhibitor of the caspase-activated DNase (CAD/DFF40). In living cells, ICAD binds CAD and keeps it inactive and in the [[Nucleus|cytoplasm/nucleus]] compartment. During [[Apoptosis]], ICAD is cleaved by executioner caspases, releasing CAD to fragment nuclear DNA — the classic internucleosomal ladder observed in dying cells.

## Mechanism

[[Caspase-3]] is the principal executioner caspase responsible for cleaving ICAD. The relationship is directional: Caspase-3 acts as the operative agent upon ICAD, and ICAD does not act back on the protease. Upon [[Caspase-3]] cleavage, the released CAD enters the nucleus and cuts DNA between nucleosomes, producing the oligonucleosomal fragments that are a hallmark of apoptosis.

> [!info]
> The directness of this molecular event makes it a precise, often druggable point of control: perturbing ICAD cleavage can decisively shift cell fate, with relevance to cancer where evasion of apoptosis is a hallmark.

## Physiological & Pathological Roles

ICAD keeps CAD poised but inactive, preventing inappropriate DNA fragmentation during normal cell function. In cancer, reduced apoptosis execution — sometimes via altered ICAD/CAD regulation — allows survival of genomically damaged cells. Conversely, therapeutic strategies that engage executioner caspases depend on intact ICAD cleavage to complete cell death.

## Research Notes

Because DNA fragmentation is a late, definitive step in apoptosis, the ICAD/CAD system is widely used experimentally as a biochemical marker of apoptosis and as an assay readout in cell death studies.
