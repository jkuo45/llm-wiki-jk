### Overview

Arterial aging refers to the progressive structural and functional deterioration of large elastic arteries with age, most prominently manifesting as arterial stiffening and impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation. It is a central contributor to systolic hypertension, isolated systolic hypertension, and the rising cardiovascular risk of aging populations. Mechanistically, arterial aging is driven by the accumulation of oxidative stress, low-grade inflammation, and cellular senescence within the vessel wall, and is held in check by competent [[Autophagy]].

### Mechanism / Structure

With age, autophagic flux in vascular cells declines. Impaired autophagy permits damaged mitochondria, misfolded proteins, and lipid aggregates to accumulate, promoting endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and vascular stiffening. The loss of nitric-oxide (NO)-mediated relaxation follows from this oxidative and inflammatory burden, as endothelial NO bioavailability falls.

> [!info] Autophagy as the defender of vascular youth
> [[Autophagy]] prevents arterial aging by clearing damaged mitochondria and aggregates that would otherwise drive endothelial dysfunction and stiffening. Enhancing autophagy — pharmacologically or via caloric restriction — rescues endothelial function in preclinical models.

### Role of Autophagy and Spermidine

[[Spermidine]] reverses features of arterial aging, including large elastic artery stiffening, and restores NO-mediated endothelial function in aged vessels. These vascular benefits are linked to spermidine-induced autophagy, which clears damaged mitochondria and reduces vascular oxidative stress and inflammation. By improving endothelium-dependent vasodilation, spermidine counteracts the stiffening that propagates systolic hypertension and cardiovascular risk with age, supporting it as a candidate vascular-rejuvenation intervention.

### Clinical & Research Significance

Arterial aging underlies much of the systolic hypertension and arterial stiffness seen in older adults and predicts adverse cardiovascular events independently of traditional risk factors. Interventions that restore autophagic competence — such as spermidine, caloric restriction, or other autophagy enhancers — are promising but remain largely preclinical. The key translational challenge is demonstrating durable human vascular benefit and defining the optimal timing and intensity of autophagy modulation.
