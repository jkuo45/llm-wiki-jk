# Nucleophagy

**Nucleophagy** is the selective [[Autophagy]] of nuclear material: the delivery of parts of the [[Nucleus]] — including patches of [[Nuclear Envelope|nuclear envelope]], nucleoplasmic protein aggregates, or the organelle itself — to autophagosomes for lysosomal turnover. It is a relatively young concept, formally named in 2009, and it has been recognised as an important quality-control route for the largest organelle in the cell.

## Overview

Nucleophagy is a form of [[Selective Autophagy]] in which the cargo is nuclear. Mechanistically it requires three ingredients common to all selective autophagy: a receptor that recognises the cargo, an [[Autophagosome]] that engulfs it, and a route of delivery to the lysosome. Unlike bulk autophagy of cytoplasm, nucleophagy is cargo-selective and receptor-dependent.

Two broad flavours are described:

- **Selective / canonical nucleophagy** — receptor-mediated, in which individual pieces of the nuclear envelope or specific nuclear bodies are engulfed in a double-membrane autophagosome that fuses with the lysosome.
- **Bulk nucleophagy** — more extensive, in which the whole nucleus is progressively engulfed (nuclear envelope rupture, or "nucleophagy of the dying cell"), usually in the context of cell death, and sometimes executed by the death receptor [[Fas]] or by [[Caspase-8]]-dependent, caspase-independent pathways.

## Mechanism

- **Receptors.** The outer mitochondrial membrane protein [[BNIP3]] (and its family member [[BNIP3L]]), the nuclear receptor [[NIX]], and the hypoxia-sensitive [[FUNDC1]] act as selective-autophagy receptors. Each has an LIR motif that binds LC3 on the autophagosomal membrane ([[LIR Motif]]), and NIX additionally has a BH3 domain and can sequester [[BCL2L13]]/Bcl-2 family members to activate the autophagy machinery.
- **Nuclear envelope recognition.** In yeast, the nuclear-envelope protein Atg39 and the outer-membrane protein Atg32 drive this step; in mammals, the ER-phagy receptor families (SEC24, ATL3, SEC62, TEX264) perform the analogous function at the ER, and the nuclear envelope, being continuous with the ER, is degraded by the same machinery — the basis of the "nucleophagy by ER-phagy" model.
- **Canonical pathway.** A nuclear body such as a nucleolus or an aggregated nuclear protein is enclosed by a double-membrane autophagosome; the autophagosome then fuses with lysosomes via the HOPS/SNARE machinery, with [[LC3]] and [[Atg]] proteins on the autophagosome and [[p62]] serving as cargo adaptors where appropriate.
- **LAP and nuCyp2a4.** LC3-associated phagocytosis (LAP) is a non-canonical, Atg-independent route in which LC3 is recruited to single-membrane phagocytic cups; a nuclear-envelope-derived variant, nuCyp2a4, has been described in which nuclear material is delivered to lysosomes through a single-membrane, partly endosomal route, bypassing classic autophagosome formation.
- **Link to mitophagy.** Because NIX and FUNDC1 are shared between the nuclear and mitochondrial compartments, nucleophagy and [[Mitophagy]] are mechanistically intertwined: the same receptors are deployed at different locations, and a block in one route often affects the other.

## Physiological Role

- **Nuclear quality control.** Selective removal of misfolded or aggregated nuclear proteins (e.g. polyglutamine-expanded huntingtin, TDP-43, or nuclear inclusions in disease) protects transcriptional integrity.
- **Nuclear envelope repair.** Localised nucleophagy helps remove damaged or oxidised nuclear-envelope segments, and it contributes to the biogenesis and turnover of the nuclear envelope during cell division and in nuclear reconstruction after damage.
- **Chromatin and transcriptional regulation.** Degradation of nuclear bodies, including the [[Nucleolus]] and stress granules that translocate to the nucleus, modulates transcription and ribosome biogenesis.
- **Developmental and disease roles.** Nucleophagy is required in embryogenesis and in stem-cell differentiation, and its failure is implicated in neurodegeneration, in myopathy (where nuclear-envelope damage drives repair), and in cancer, where altered nuclear-envelope turnover supports tumour growth.

## Clinical Relevance

- **Cancer.** Many tumours up-regulate NIX, BNIP3 or FUNDC1 to survive hypoxia and to maintain flux, and others disable nuclear quality control to protect oncogenic transcription factors. Nuclear-envelope integrity is also emerging as a readout of tumour aggressiveness.
- **Neurodegeneration.** Defective nucleophagy contributes to accumulation of nuclear aggregates in [[Parkinson's Disease]] and in [[Huntington's Disease]], and the NIX/mitophagy axis is a therapeutic target in these conditions.
- **Ageing.** Nucleophagy capacity declines with age along with the rest of the autophagy machinery, adding to the loss of proteostasis; restoring it via [[Rapamycin]] or spermidine-like interventions remains experimental.
- **Inflammation and infection.** Pathogens that invade the nucleus or that damage the nuclear envelope (e.g. DNA viruses, and bacteria that trigger [[Lysosomal Membrane Permeabilization]]-adjacent pathways) are handled in part by nucleophagy.
