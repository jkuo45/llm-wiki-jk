## **Targeting mitophagy for neuroprotection: mechanisms and therapeutic opportunities**

**Received: 25 July 2025 Accepted: 27 May 2026**

**Abstract:**

[[Mitochondria]] are essential for neuronal energy production, cellular homeostasis, and overall neuronal function. Due to their high metabolic demands and limited regenerative capacity, [[Neuron|neurons]] are particularly vulnerable to [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction|mitochondrial dysfunction]], which leads to [[ATP]] depletion, excessive [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species (ROS)]] production, and [[Calcium|calcium imbalance]]—ultimately causing [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], metabolic disruption, and neuronal death. [[Mitophagy]] is a selective process that removes damaged mitochondria through the [[Autophagy]]-[[Lysosome|lysosome pathway]]. As a key mechanism of [[Mitochondrial Quality Control|mitochondrial quality control]], mitophagy preserves energy production, limits oxidative damage, and maintains mitochondrial network integrity. This process is regulated by pathways such as [[PINK1]]-[[Parkin]] and receptor-mediated mechanisms involving [[BNIP3]] and [[FUNDC1]], all of which help sustain cellular health by preventing mitochondrial dysfunction. Impaired mitophagy is a common feature of several [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|neurodegenerative diseases]], including [[Alzheimer's Disease|Alzheimer's]], [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's]], [[ALS|amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)]], and [[Huntington's Disease|Huntington's disease]], exacerbating mitochondrial damage and neuronal stress. Emerging therapeutic strategies that target mitophagy—ranging from pharmacological agents and [[Gene Therapy|gene therapies]] to dietary interventions—show promise in restoring mitochondrial quality and protecting neurons from degeneration. Nevertheless, challenges remain in translating these findings into effective clinical treatments. Mitophagy represents a critical mechanism for preserving neuronal integrity and offers a compelling target for innovative therapies against neurodegenerative disorders.

## 1. Introduction

[[Mitochondria]] are crucial organelles that regulate energy homeostasis, cellular signaling, and cell death. They also serve as the cellular energy centers of all eukaryotic cells. Through [[Beta-Oxidation|beta oxidation]], the [[TCA cycle|tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle)]], and [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative phosphorylation]] of the [[Electron Transport Chain|electron transport chain (ETC)]], they supply the necessary "fuel" for maintaining the fundamental functions of cells. [[Mitochondrial Quality Control|Mitochondrial quality control (MQC)]] sustains the homeostasis and functionality of mitochondria. Among these, [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]]—the selective autophagic degradation of mitochondria—eliminates dysfunctional or damaged mitochondria. It also coordinates with [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis|mitochondrial biogenesis]] and [[Mitochondrial Dynamics|dynamics]] to regulate mitochondrial quality and quantity, thereby determining cell fate.

[[Neuron|Neurons]] are highly metabolic cells that demand substantial energy to maintain processes such as membrane potential, [[Neurotransmitters|neurotransmitter]] release, and reuptake. Hence, neuronal mitochondria must generate [[ATP]] efficiently to fulfill this high-energy requirement. Neurons possess complex structures, including long [[Axon Degeneration|axons]] and [[Dendritic cells|dendrites]]. Mitochondria within neurons must undergo dynamic distribution through mitochondrial dynamic processes, such as [[Mitochondrial Dynamics|mitochondrial fission, fusion, and transportation]], to ensure the timely provision of energy, particularly in synapses and other regions with high energy demands. Mitochondria in neurons are not only responsible for energy generation but also play a key role in regulating intracellular [[Calcium|calcium (Ca²⁺) concentration]]. By absorbing and releasing calcium ions, mitochondria act as buffers in the regulation of neuronal excitability and signal transduction. Compared with other cell types, neurons typically have longer lifespans; consequently, their mitochondria must also maintain long-term functionality. This makes [[Mitochondrial Quality Control|MQC]] particularly significant in neurons to prevent the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which can lead to neuronal dysfunction and [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs)]]. These characteristics render mitochondria vitally important in the normal function and health of neurons. In damaged mature neurons, mitochondria can also be injured, resulting in local mitochondrial depolarization and [[ATP]] loss, causing local energy deficits in axons and thereby restricting the regeneration of damaged neurons. Not only that, both neural development and the maintenance of long-term neuronal health require the effective removal of aggregated proteins and aged or defective organelles. Selective mitophagy can clear aged and dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis. Excessive mitophagy or insufficient mitophagy can lead to [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction|mitochondrial dysfunction]] and disruption of cellular homeostasis. Not only can it fail to provide sufficient energy for the normal physiological activities of neurons, but it can also cause the excessive accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria within neurons, thereby inducing neuronal injury processes. This is also an important cause for the occurrence and development of neurodegenerative disease.

Currently, functional impairments resulting from mitophagy disorders have become an important factor in the progression and development of [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]]. However, the precise mechanism by which mitophagy specifically regulates neuronal functions and thereby affects NDDs still requires in-depth research.

> [!important] Core Concept
> Mitophagy is a double-edged sword in neurodegeneration: insufficient clearance allows damaged mitochondria to accumulate and trigger inflammation/death, while excessive mitophagy depletes healthy mitochondria, causing bioenergetic failure.

## 2. Mitophagy

[[Mitophagy]], a specialized form of [[Selective Autophagy|selective autophagy]], is an evolutionarily conserved process dedicated to the degradation of damaged or dysfunctional [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]], thereby ensuring mitochondrial quality control and cellular homeostasis. Under physiological or pathological stress, such as excessive [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]], nutrient deficiency, or cellular aging, mitochondria undergo depolarization damage. To maintain a stable mitochondrial network, cells utilize the mitophagy machinery to selectively encapsulate and eliminate these abnormal organelles through four sequential steps: 1) Cells accurately recognize damaged mitochondria targeted for removal and activate the corresponding signaling pathways. 2) The initial damage signal is amplified, leading to the recruitment of adaptor proteins or receptors that link mitochondria to the autophagy machinery. This step involves both [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]]-mediated and receptor-mediated pathways, ultimately enabling engagement of the autophagic system. 3) The autophagy machinery is recruited to the vicinity of the targeted mitochondria, where an isolation membrane forms and progressively engulfs the organelle. 4) The mature [[Autophagosome]] subsequently fuses with a [[Lysosome]], resulting in the final degradation of the mitochondria.

By integrating these pathways, mitophagy effectively prevents the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and maintains the integrity of the intracellular environment.

### 2.1 Ubiquitin-dependent Mitophagy

The [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]]-mediated ubiquitin-dependent pathway is currently the most extensively studied mitophagy pathway. [[PINK1]] is a kinase encoded by the [[PINK1|PARK6]] gene, and Parkin is an [[Ubiquitin|E3 ubiquitin ligase]] encoded by the [[PARK2]] gene. This pathway consists of three key components: PINK1 as a mitochondrial damage sensor, Parkin as a signal amplifier, and [[Ubiquitin|ubiquitin chains]] as a signal effector. They work together to activate mitophagy of damaged mitochondria.

The process begins with the loss of [[Mitochondrial Membrane Potential|mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)]]. This signal prevents PINK1 from entering the inner membrane for degradation. Instead, PINK1 stabilizes and accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM). It then recruits the cytosolic E3 ubiquitin ligase, Parkin, to the surface of the damaged mitochondrion. Crucially, this recruitment process is not autonomous but is facilitated by the [[FBXO7|F-box protein FBXO7]] (encoded by the PARK15 gene). FBXO7 functions as a scaffolding protein that interacts with both PINK1 and Parkin, thereby markedly accelerating Parkin translocation from the cytosol to damaged mitochondria. In addition, FBXO7 promotes the aggregation of damaged mitochondria, clustering them to ensure efficient downstream clearance. These ubiquitin chains serve as "eat-me" signals, recruiting autophagy receptors such as [[p62]] and [[NBR1]]. Biochemical and genetic analyses confirm that PINK1 acts upstream of Parkin. PINK1's kinase activity and mitochondrial localization are prerequisites for recruiting Parkin and initiating the autophagy cascade. Once recruited, Parkin "tags" the damaged mitochondrion by ubiquitinating key proteins on the OMM. Research shows that Parkin mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of the mitochondrial fusion proteins [[MFN1|Mitofusin 1]] and [[MFN2|Mitofusin 2 (Mfn1/2)]]. This not only prevents the damaged mitochondrion from re-fusing with the healthy mitochondrial network but also promotes its fragmentation for clearance. Furthermore, Parkin ubiquitinates the mitochondrial motor adaptor protein [[Miro1|Miro]], leading to its rapid degradation. This severs the mitochondrion's connection to microtubules, causing motility arrest. This ensures the damaged organelle is anchored in place, ready for engulfment by the [[Autophagosome|autophagosome]].

> [!info] Primary Autophagy Receptors
> In PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, [[NDP52]] and [[OPTN]] serve as the primary autophagy receptors. They are recruited to the ubiquitin-coated mitochondrial surface. Through their [[LIR Motif|LC3-interacting region (LIR) motifs]], they bind to [[LC3]] proteins on the expanding phagophore, effectively guiding the isolation membrane to enwrap the mitochondrion. The presence of ubiquitin chains alone is not sufficient. Downstream receptors must efficiently recognize these chains, a process aided by the kinase [[TBK1]]. After Parkin ubiquitinates the mitochondrion, TBK1 is recruited and activated. TBK1 phosphorylates the autophagy receptors OPTN and NDP52, significantly enhancing their binding affinity for ubiquitin chains. This creates a positive feedback loop that accelerates mitochondrial engulfment.

PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy is closely linked to [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]]. [[Amyloid Beta|Amyloid-beta (Aβ)]] can directly inhibit the activation of PINK1 and the recruitment of Parkin to mitochondria, resulting in the obstruction of mitophagy. At the same time, [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] produced by damaged mitochondria will promote the abnormal cleavage of [[Amyloid Precursor Protein|Aβ precursor protein]], accelerating the generation of Aβ, forming a vicious cycle. PINK1 and Parkin play a crucial role in immune cells, such as [[Dendritic cells|dendritic cells]]. They promote mitophagy, which prevents mitochondrial antigens from being presented on the cell surface via [[MHC type II|MHC class I]] molecules. When the PINK1/Parkin pathway fails, mitochondrial antigens are mistakenly presented to cytotoxic [[T Lymphocyte|T cells]]. This can lead the immune system to attack the body's own neurons. This mechanism may explain why gut infections—which can trigger mitochondrial antigen presentation—could be a potential trigger for [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's disease]]. In addition, there are ubiquitin-dependent mitophagy pathways that do not rely on Parkin. These pathways complement the Parkin pathway, working together to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis. [[MUL1]] is an E3 ubiquitin ligase located on the outer mitochondrial membrane. Unlike Parkin, it does not need to be "recruited"; it continuously monitors mitochondrial quality by ubiquitinating [[MFN2|mitofusin 2 (Mfn2)]], thereby functioning as a parallel and compensatory pathway to Parkin-mediated mitophagy. Studies in both [[Drosophila melanogaster|Drosophila]] and mammalian cell models have demonstrated that MUL1 expression rescues mitochondrial accumulation as well as muscle and neurodegenerative phenotypes caused by Parkin deficiency. When PINK1 or Parkin function is impaired, MUL1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of Mfn2 suppress mitochondrial fusion while simultaneously tagging the mitochondria with a ubiquitin label, promoting their clearance via autophagy. After [[RNF185]] ubiquitinates [[BNIP1]], [[p62]] recognizes the ubiquitin chains and triggers mitophagy. [[Ambra-1]] functions as a platform to recruit [[HUWE1]] to ubiquitinate OMM proteins, enabling the recruitment of p62 and [[OPTN]] for mitochondrial clearance.

In contrast, under conditions of excessive mitochondrial fission in the absence of Parkin, excessive activation of the fission protein [[DRP1|Drp1 (dynamin-related protein 1)]] can cause physical rupture of the OMM, exposing inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) proteins. These exposed IMM proteins, such as [[PHB2|Prohibitin 2 (PHB2)]], can then be ubiquitinated by non-Parkin E3 ligases, generating signals that recruit the autophagy machinery to complete mitochondrial clearance. This mechanism provides an explanation for the persistence of basal mitophagy observed in Parkin-deficient [[Neuron|neurons]] or [[Cardiomyocyte|cardiomyocytes]].

> [!tip] Compensatory Mitophagy Pathways
> When the canonical PINK1/Parkin pathway is impaired (as in familial [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]]), multiple backup systems—including [[MUL1]], [[Ambra-1]]/[[HUWE1]], [[DRP1|Drp1]]-mediated OMM rupture, and receptor-mediated pathways via [[NIX]] and [[BCL2L13]]—can maintain basal mitochondrial clearance.

### 2.2 Ubiquitin-independent pathway

In mitophagy, there also exists a ubiquitin-independent pathway in which mitochondrial outer membrane proteins are directly recognized by autophagy receptors. This receptor-mediated mitophagy primarily involves [[FUNDC1]], [[BNIP3]], and [[BNIP3L|NIX (BNIP3L)]]. These pathways play a crucial regulatory role in [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]], and their abnormalities are closely related to the imbalance of mitochondrial homeostasis in neurons. Abnormalities in [[MERCS|Mitochondria-Associated Endoplasmic Reticulum Membranes (MAMs)]] are an early pathological feature of [[Alzheimer's Disease|Alzheimer's disease (AD)]]. FUNDC1 is also localized to MAMs and interacts with the [[IP3R2|IP3R2 receptor]] on the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum|endoplasmic reticulum]]. The presence of FUNDC1 helps maintain the structural stability of MAMs. When FUNDC1 is absent, the MAM structure is disrupted, leading to impaired mitochondrial fission and abnormal [[Calcium|calcium signaling]]. Given that MAMs are often over-strengthened or dysfunctional in AD, FUNDC1's fine-tuning of MAMs provides a new mechanism for understanding calcium homeostasis imbalance in NDDs. Other research shows that when neurons suffer from [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischemia/hypoxia-reperfusion injury]], the mitochondrial kinase [[CK2]] is inhibited. This leads to the dephosphorylation of FUNDC1 at the Ser13 site. This dephosphorylation activates FUNDC1, enabling it to recruit [[LC3]] and initiate mitophagy. Data indicate that activating FUNDC1-mediated autophagy can clear damaged mitochondria, significantly reducing neuronal [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] and infarct volume. Conversely, knocking out FUNDC1 significantly worsens neural damage.

[[BNIP3]] (BCL2 Interacting Protein 3) is a single-pass transmembrane protein located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, belonging to the [[Bcl-2 family|BH3-only subfamily]] of the BCL-2 family. It is induced by [[HIF-1α]] under hypoxic stress and acts as an important receptor-mediated autophagy receptor. BNIP3 directly interacts with the autophagy-related protein [[LC3]] (via its [[LIR Motif|LIR motif]]), triggering the specific clearance of mitochondria. Mitophagy dysfunction has been reported in neurons derived from patients with genetic forms of [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's disease]], particularly those carrying mutations in [[PINK1]] (PARK6) or [[PARK2]] (PRKN), which disrupt the canonical PINK1/Parkin pathway. Interestingly, overexpression of the mitochondrial outer membrane receptor [[BNIP3L|NIX (BNIP3L)]] has been shown to restore mitophagy in these neurons by activating a receptor-mediated pathway independent of PINK1/Parkin signaling. However, whether this compensatory mechanism operates in other genetic forms of PD or in idiopathic PD remains to be determined. NIX does not require phosphorylation by PINK1 nor the ubiquitination activity of Parkin. It can directly bind to [[LC3]] to pull damaged mitochondria into the autophagosome. This discovery provides theoretical support for the idea that the NIX-mediated pathway can compensate for the loss of the Parkin pathway, protecting neurons from degenerative death.

> [!info] Recently Identified Mitophagy Receptors
> [[BCL2L13]] is the functional homolog of the yeast mitophagy receptor Atg32 in mammals and operates independently of Parkin. Located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, it contains two key domains: a [[LIR Motif|LIR motif]] (W-x-x-I positions 270–273) for binding [[LC3]], and a BH domain. BCL2L13 can simultaneously induce mitochondrial fragmentation and recruit autophagosomes. Unlike other receptors, [[FKBP8]] possesses a unique "escape mechanism" and preferentially binds to the LC3A isoform. While most OMM proteins are ubiquitinated and degraded by the [[Proteasome]] during autophagy, FKBP8 avoids this process. It strongly recruits LC3A via its N-terminal LIR motif (F-E-V-I at positions 22–25) and directly mediates Parkin-independent mitophagy. [[PHB2]] (Prohibitin 2) is normally located on the inner mitochondrial membrane and remains "invisible" to cytosolic LC3. When mitochondria are damaged and the outer membrane ruptures or is degraded by the proteasome, PHB2 becomes exposed. Its LIR motif (Y-V-L-P at positions 121–124) then binds LC3 to initiate autophagy. These findings reveal the complexity and precision of the autophagy mechanism and offer insights into how cells can still maintain mitochondrial homeostasis even when Parkin function is impaired, as in [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's disease]].

## 3. The bidirectional regulatory effect of mitophagy on neurons

[[Mitophagy]] acts as a "double-edged sword." Due to their extremely high energy demands and complex morphology, [[Neuron|neurons]] are highly susceptible to dysfunctional [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]]. While mitophagy plays a protective role by removing damaged mitochondria, excessive mitophagy may lead to mitochondrial depletion and subsequent bioenergetic failure, ultimately contributing to neuronal death. Therefore, striking a balance in mitophagy is of crucial importance to ensure the timely clearance of damaged mitochondria while preventing the adverse consequences of excessive activation.

> [!warning] The Double-Edged Sword
> Mitophagy must be precisely balanced: insufficient clearance causes accumulation of damaged mitochondria driving [[Neuroinflammation]] and cell death, while excessive mitophagy strips neurons of essential functional mitochondria, causing bioenergetic collapse.

### 3.1 Appropriate mitophagy maintains the stability of neuronal function

Moderate to mild autophagy serves as a survival-promoting mechanism for neurons by removing protein aggregates and damaged organelles, as well as regulating apoptosis and [[Inflammation]]. Consequently, this process maintains [[Central Nervous System|central nervous system]] homeostasis.

#### 3.1.1 Mitophagy Maintains Neuronal Bioenergetic Homeostasis

[[Neuron|Neurons]] require a continuous supply of [[ATP]] to support electrical signaling and [[Synaptic plasticity|synaptic plasticity]]. The majority of this ATP is generated by mitochondria through [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)]]. [[Mitophagy]] is essential for maintaining bioenergetic homeostasis because it selectively removes dysfunctional mitochondria. Damaged mitochondria are inefficient and may reverse [[ATP Synthase|ATP synthase]] activity, consuming rather than producing ATP—a process termed "substrate wasting." When the [[Mitochondrial Membrane Potential|mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)]] decreases, the [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]] pathway initiates the degradation of these low-efficiency organelles. In [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]] models, activation of this pathway stabilizes energy supply and reduces [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neuron]] loss. Specific mitophagy receptors regulate mitochondrial clearance under different metabolic stresses. For instance, [[FUNDC1]] mediates mitophagy during [[Hypoxia|hypoxia]]. Under normal conditions, [[Src|Src kinase]] inhibits FUNDC1; however, during hypoxia, FUNDC1 is dephosphorylated, allowing it to bind [[LC3]] and reduce mitochondrial fragmentation. This selective removal ensures that only functional mitochondria contribute to the cellular metabolic pool.

The balance between mitochondrial clearance and energy demand is particularly critical in [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]]. In HsAPP Tg mice, stimulating OXPHOS without restoring mitophagy leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, creating a metabolic "bottleneck" that exacerbates synaptic loss. Enhancing lysosomal function can clear these damaged organelles and restore metabolic function. Furthermore, mitophagy works synergistically with [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis|mitochondrial biogenesis]] via the [[SIRT3]]/[[AMPK]]/[[PGC-1α]] axis. The "clearance-renewal" cycle ensures that degraded mitochondria are replaced by healthy ones, thereby maintaining ATP efficiency and long-term bioenergetic stability.

#### 3.1.2 Mitophagy Suppresses Neuroinflammation

In the pathological landscape of [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|neurodegenerative diseases]], mitochondria serve not only as metabolic hubs but also as critical rheostats of the [[Innate Immune System|innate immune response]]. The accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria is a primary trigger for chronic [[Neuroinflammation]]. When [[Mitochondrial Quality Control|mitochondrial quality control]] fails, compromised mitochondria undergo outer membrane permeabilization or rupture, releasing a barrage of [[Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns|Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs)]]—most notably [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] and [[mtDNA|mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)]]—into the cytosol.

Specifically, excessive mitochondrial ROS facilitates activation of the [[NLRP3]] inflammasome, leading to proteolytic cleavage of pro-[[Caspase-1]] and subsequent maturation and secretion of pro-inflammatory [[Cytokines|cytokines]], such as [[IL-1β]]. Simultaneously, the leakage of mtDNA into the cytoplasm triggers the [[cGAS-STING Pathway|cGAS-STING pathway]], further driving the expression of [[Type I Interferon|Type I interferons]] and other inflammatory mediators, which exacerbate the neuroinflammatory microenvironment.

Mitophagy serves as a vital "quality control" checkpoint by selectively sequestering and degrading these damaged organelles via the [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]] or receptor-mediated pathways. By removing dysfunctional mitochondria before they can release [[Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns|DAMPs]], mitophagy effectively extinguishes the inflammatory fire at its source. Consequently, defects in mitophagic flux are increasingly recognized as central drivers of the neuroinflammatory "storm" observed in [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]] and [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]], suggesting that pharmacological enhancement of mitophagy may offer a robust therapeutic strategy to suppress chronic brain inflammation.

#### 3.1.3 Mitophagy Maintains Synaptic Function and Plasticity

[[Mitophagy]] is indispensable for preserving mitochondrial quality at distal neuronal processes, thereby directly supporting synaptic architecture and plasticity. The importance of this regulation is underscored by the specialized role of [[PINK1]] in mitochondrial dynamics and dendritic spine development. In Pink1 knockout [[Neuron|neurons]], mitochondria in dendrites and [[Axon Degeneration|axons]] are significantly elongated and reduced in number due to defective fission. This morphological shift results in a "supply deficit" at synapses, where fewer presynaptic boutons contain mitochondria, and those present are often abnormally large. Consequently, Pink1 knockout neurons exhibit a lower density of mature dendritic spines and excitatory synapses, characterized by reduced [[Homer1]] expression and impaired [[Long-term potentiation|long-term potentiation]]. Mechanistically, PINK1 maintains synaptic integrity by phosphorylating [[DRP1|Drp1]] at the S616 site, which promotes the mitochondrial fission necessary for their recruitment into dendritic spines. Whether alternative pathways compensate for this fission defect remains an area for future investigation.

Beyond local dynamics, synaptic health requires the efficient clearance of damaged organelles, a process heavily dependent on retrograde transport. Han et al. demonstrated that synaptic mitochondrial integrity is maintained through the coordination of Parkin-mediated mitophagy and [[SNAPIN]]-driven retrograde transport. In [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]] models, an imbalance occurs where increased mitophagy initiation is coupled with defective retrograde transport, leading to the accumulation of partially degraded mitochondria within synapses. Notably, enhancing SNAPIN-mediated transport facilitates the removal of these damaged organelles from the synapse to the soma, effectively alleviating mitophagy stress and rescuing synaptic deficits in AD mice.

Complementing this transport mechanism, the pro-apoptotic protein [[BOK]] has emerged as a key regulator of mitophagy flux in [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]]. BOK facilitates [[Parkin]] translocation to damaged mitochondria by competing with the [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]]/Parkin complex, thereby promoting the initiation of clearance. In APPswe/PS1dE9 models, the loss of BOK is closely linked to mitochondrial accumulation; however, hippocampal overexpression of BOK can restore mitophagy flux and improve cognitive function by alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction. In summary, the integration of [[PINK1]]-[[DRP1|Drp1]] mediated fission, BOK-facilitated Parkin recruitment, and [[SNAPIN]]-dependent retrograde transport constitutes a multi-layered quality control system. These pathways ensure that synapses are populated only by healthy, functional mitochondria, offering promising therapeutic targets for restoring synaptic plasticity in NDDs.

### 3.2 Excessive mitophagy leads to neuronal dysfunction

In recent years, evidence has emerged suggesting that excessive activation of [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]] may drive neuronal degeneration and [[Neuroinflammation]] by compromising mitochondrial network integrity, exacerbating bioenergetic dysfunction, or triggering cytotoxicity.

#### 3.2.1 Normal mitochondria are mistakenly cleared

The primary function of mitophagy is to selectively clear damaged mitochondria to maintain cellular energy homeostasis. However, excessive activation or malfunction of mitophagic recognition mechanisms can lead to the inappropriate elimination of healthy mitochondria, contributing to neurodegenerative pathology. This pathological overactivation is primarily mediated by dysregulation of the [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]] pathway. Under normal conditions, PINK1 recruits E3 [[Ubiquitin|ubiquitin]] ligase Parkin to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), where Parkin catalyzes the ubiquitination of OMM proteins, such as [[MFN2]] and [[VDAC1]]. This ubiquitination promotes the recruitment of autophagy receptors (e.g., [[p62]]/SQSTM1) and initiates clearance of damaged mitochondria. Under pathological conditions, however, excessive ubiquitination can target even mildly damaged or healthy mitochondria for degradation.

In [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]] models, aberrant [[MFN2]] ubiquitination blocks mitochondrial fusion and exacerbates fragmentation, contributing to neuronal damage. For instance, in [[MPTP]]-induced PD mice, Mfn2 ubiquitination increased by 50%, correlating with a 30% loss of [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neurons]] in the [[Substantia Nigra|substantia nigra]]. Importantly, functional studies demonstrate that restoring Mfn2 levels via viral-mediated overexpression significantly attenuates MPTP-induced dopaminergic neuron loss and motor deficits, providing direct evidence that Mfn2 degradation is a causal contributor to neuronal degeneration in this model.

> [!info] G6PD-PINK1 Axis
> [[Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase|Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)]] regulates mitophagy by maintaining the stability of [[PINK1]]. In the SH-SY5Y neuronal model, overexpression of G6PD prolonged the half-life of PINK1 protein by two-fold and increased the aggregation of PINK1 on the mitochondrial membrane by 30%, thereby activating the Parkin-mediated ubiquitination cascade, resulting in the erroneous clearance of normal mitochondria (a 40% decrease in [[mtDNA|mitochondrial DNA]] copies). Surprisingly, this effect was independent of the [[Pentose Phosphate Pathway|pentose-phosphate pathway (PPP)]], revealing a non-canonical role for G6PD in mitophagy regulation.

#### 3.2.2 Aggravation of Energy Metabolism Disruption

[[Mitophagy]] is a key mechanism for cells to selectively eliminate damaged mitochondria. However, its excessive activation can lead to excessive consumption of functional mitochondria, thereby exacerbating energy metabolism disruption. [[BNIP3L|NIX]] and [[BNIP3]] act as receptors for mitophagy, mediating selective clearance of mitochondria by directly binding to the autophagosome membrane proteins [[LC3]]/[[GABARAP]]. Excessive or prolonged activation of NIX/BNIP3-mediated mitophagy promotes aberrant mitochondrial clearance, which in turn disrupts neuronal energy metabolism and triggers [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]], contributing to the development of [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]]. Research indicates that following severe metabolic stress (such as [[Hypoxia|hypoxia]]/[[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischemia]]), [[HIF-1α]] within neurons transcriptionally activates BNIP3. BNIP3 then inserts into the mitochondrial membrane, directly causing the loss of [[Mitochondrial Membrane Potential|mitochondrial membrane potential]] and the opening of the [[Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore|mitochondrial permeability transition pore]]. This BNIP3-mediated cell death is delayed neuronal death. Knocking out the BNIP3 gene can significantly improve neuronal survival in the damaged brain regions. This suggests that inhibiting BNIP3 could be a target for preventing neuronal degenerative death.

[[BNIP3L|NIX]] is also a key responder to severe injury stress. Studies have found that under conditions of [[Spinal Cord Injury (SCI)|spinal cord injury]] and hypoxic stress, NIX in spinal cord neurons is highly upregulated. NIX subsequently mediates a burst of mitophagy by binding to [[LC3]]. Other research has shown that in [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]] models, NIX can activate the [[JNK]] pathway via the POSH protein, inducing [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. Furthermore, stimulation with high concentrations of [[Glutamate|glutamate]] leads to [[DRP1|Drp1]]-dependent excessive mitochondrial fission, which subsequently triggers large-scale mitophagy. This excessive autophagic clearance directly causes a catastrophic drop in [[ATP]] levels (ATP depletion) within neurons. Inhibiting mitochondrial fission and subsequent autophagy—whether pharmacologically or genetically—can maintain ATP levels and rescue neurons.

#### 3.2.3 Aggravation of Mitochondrial Fragmentation

[[Mitochondrial Dynamics|Mitochondrial dynamics]], characterized by the balance between fusion and fission, are tightly regulated by a specific set of proteins. Key players include the fission protein [[DRP1|Drp1]] and the fusion proteins [[MFN1]]/[[MFN2]] and [[OPA1]]. The kinase [[PINK1]] exerts context-dependent effects on this balance. While PINK1 is widely reported to promote mitochondrial fission by phosphorylating Drp1 to facilitate [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]], other studies highlight its role in supporting mitochondrial fusion by stabilizing OPA1 and modulating Mfn2 activity.

Normal mitophagy (such as the [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]] pathway) selectively eliminates damaged mitochondria, maintaining mitochondrial network homeostasis; but excessive autophagy breaks this balance, leading to excessive fission and fragmentation of mitochondria. Drp1 is the core regulatory factor for mitochondrial fission. Its excessive activation leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and excessive autophagy, causing neuronal damage in [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]]. For example, the PINK1 kinase directly phosphorylates the S616 site of Drp1, enhancing its mitochondrial recruitment and fission activity. Experimental evidence shows that mutant [[Huntingtin|Huntingtin (mHtt)]] protein not only aggregates in the nucleus but also binds to Drp1 on mitochondria. mHtt acts as a potent activator, significantly increasing the [[GTP|GTPase]] activity of Drp1. This abnormal elevation in enzymatic activity causes the mitochondrial network to fragment. The resulting mitochondrial fragments are too small or too impaired to be transported efficiently, leading to defects in axonal transport and subsequent neuronal death. Pathologically high intraocular pressure can also lead to the phosphorylation of Drp1 at the S616 site by activating [[ERK1/2]], triggering mitochondrial fragmentation and extensive [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] in [[Retinal Ganglion Cells|retinal ganglion cells]], resulting in visual field defects in [[Glaucoma|glaucoma]] patients.

In the [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]] model, [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] also leads to enhanced acetylation at the K711 site of Drp1, promoting oligomerization and mitochondrial division. The [[SIRT3]] deacetylase can rescue mitochondrial dysfunction by inhibiting K711 acetylation and reducing [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neuron]] death. Similar to the phosphorylated S616 site of Drp1 in PD, in the [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]] model, [[Amyloid Beta|Aβ]] triggers Drp1 phosphorylation via [[CDK5]], increasing the mitochondrial division rate by 60% and reducing synaptic mitochondrial density by 45%. In PINK1-deficient models, excessive mitochondrial fission and impaired fusion contribute to mitochondrial fragmentation and dysfunction. Blocking Drp1-dependent fission—either by genetic or pharmacological means—can partially rescue mitochondrial morphology, membrane potential, and function. Additionally, dominant-negative Drp1 mutants or pharmacological inhibitors like [[Mdivi-1]] restore mitochondrial network integrity and have protective effects on neuronal survival and function in PINK1-deficient and PD models.

> [!warning] Therapeutic Paradox of Drp1 Inhibition
> The therapeutic use of [[DRP1|Drp1]] inhibitors like [[Mdivi-1]] raises critical questions: does the suppression of pathological fission also interfere with essential physiological division, such as that required for axonal transport? Prolonged Drp1 inhibition might disrupt the positive regulation of mitochondrial quality control, potentially introducing new pathological risks.

## 4. Abnormal Mitophagy in NDDs

[[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]] such as [[Alzheimer's Disease|Alzheimer's disease]], [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's disease]], and [[Huntington's Disease|Huntington's disease (HD)]] are closely related to the decline and progressive death of [[Neuron|neurons]]. Abnormal [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]] plays a crucial role in this pathological process. Genetic variations, [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], [[Proteinopathy|protein aggregation]], and other factors can interfere with the key signaling pathways of mitophagy (such as [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]], [[mTOR]], [[BNIP3L|NIX]]/[[BNIP3]]), resulting in decreased mitochondrial clearance efficiency or abnormal activation. This leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, excessive production of [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species]], and a cascade of [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. This dynamic imbalance of mitophagy not only exacerbates the metabolic dysfunction of neurons but also forms a vicious cycle with the abnormal deposition of disease-specific pathological proteins (such as [[Alpha-synuclein|α-Syn]], [[Amyloid Beta|amyloid-beta]], [[Tau Protein|Tau protein]]), jointly promoting the progression of NDDs.

### 4.1 Alzheimer's Disease

The pathological features of the brain in [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]] patients are the presence of [[Amyloid Beta|Aβ plaques]] and [[Neurofibrillary tangles|neurofibrillary tangles]] composed of hyperphosphorylated [[Tau Protein|tau (p-tau)]] protein in specific brain regions, leading to extensive neuronal death and brain atrophy. Synaptic damage and [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction|mitochondrial dysfunction]] are considered as the early cellular changes in AD. Specifically, tau accumulation impairs [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]], resulting in the retention of dysfunctional mitochondria and increased neuronal toxicity. Furthermore, N-terminal tau fragments can cause aberrant recruitment of [[Parkin]] and [[UCHL-1]] to mitochondria, triggering excessive organelle removal and synaptic loss, thereby accelerating AD progression.

Beyond tau-mediated damage, [[Amyloid Beta|Aβ]] accumulation induces excessive mitochondrial [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] production, which damages the mitochondrial network and promotes further tau aggregation. Aβ and tau also interfere with mitochondrial transport, leading to further synaptic dysfunction. Notably, Aβ also inhibits the initiation of mitophagy by the [[PINK1]]/[[Parkin]] pathway. Du et al. found that Aβ downregulates the expression or activity of PINK1 and Parkin, hindering the ubiquitination marking of damaged mitochondria. For example, in Aβ-treated [[SH-SY5Y]] cells, the protein level of PINK1 decreased by 40%, and the recruitment of Parkin to mitochondria decreased, resulting in the collapse of [[Mitochondrial Membrane Potential|mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)]] and mitochondrial fragmentation. Moreover, Aβ also downregulates the expression of mitophagy receptor proteins. In the brain tissues of AD patients, the expression levels of [[OPTN]], [[BNIP3]], and [[BNIP3L|NIX]], which are mitophagy-related proteins, are significantly lower than those in normal tissues. This abnormality is closely related to mitochondrial clearance disorders, oxidative stress, and synaptic function deterioration in AD. In the [[Hippocampus|hippocampus]] and [[Prefrontal Cortex|prefrontal cortex]], the [[mRNA]] and protein levels of OPTN decreased by 30%–50% compared to normal controls, and were negatively correlated with the degree of Aβ deposition. This may be related to the activation of [[GSK3|GSK-3β]] phosphorylation of OPTN (Ser473 site) by Aβ oligomers, promoting its [[Ubiquitin|ubiquitin]]-mediated degradation, resulting in reduced protein stability.

In [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]], several studies have demonstrated downregulation of the fusion proteins [[MFN1]] and [[MFN2]] in patient neurons, leading to impaired mitochondrial fusion and abnormal morphology. Loss of Mfn2 can also disrupt [[MERCS|ER–mitochondria contact sites]], disturbing [[Calcium|calcium signaling]] and lipid metabolism. Additionally, [[Amyloid Beta|Aβ]] accumulation reduces [[OPA1]] expression, promoting mitochondrial fragmentation and impairing [[Autophagy|autophagy]], which contributes to tau hyperphosphorylation and [[Neurofibrillary tangles|neurofibrillary tangle]] formation. Recent studies have shown that tau acetylation can exacerbate mitochondrial fragmentation by suppressing mitochondrial biogenesis and fusion proteins (Mfn1/2, OPA1). Importantly, treatment with [[BGP-15]] has been reported to counteract this by activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, enhancing mitophagy, and promoting cognitive recovery in AD models.

> [!important] The Aβ-PINK1 Vicious Cycle
> [[Amyloid Beta|Aβ]] downregulates [[PINK1]] and [[Parkin]], blocking mitophagy initiation. The resulting accumulation of damaged [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] generates more [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]], which promotes further Aβ production from [[Amyloid Precursor Protein|APP]] — creating a self-amplifying spiral of mitochondrial dysfunction and [[Proteinopathy|proteinopathy]].

### 4.2 Parkinson's Disease

The hallmark of [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]] is the degeneration of [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neurons]] within the [[Substantia Nigra|substantia nigra]]. The neurons are vulnerable in part because of [[Dopamine|DA]] oxidation, as the resulting oxidative metabolites directly attack [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and damage [[mtDNA]]. Simultaneously, oxidized dopamine reduces the activity of the lysosomal enzyme [[Glucocerebrosidase|glucocerebrosidase]]. This lysosomal dysfunction impedes the clearance of [[Alpha-synuclein|α-Syn]], causing a synergistic "mitochondrial-lysosomal" dual failure.

Besides this internal toxicity, genetic factors also increase mitochondrial stress. [[Alpha-synuclein|α-Syn]] was first identified in Alzheimer's plaques. Early studies found mutations in the α-Syn gene ([[SNCA]]) in families with early-onset PD. However, toxic α-Syn also damages mitochondria in sporadic PD cases. Di Maio et al. found that toxic α-Syn binds to the [[TOM20]] receptor. This binding blocks protein import into mitochondria by 50%–60%. This blockage triggers [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] production and the accumulation of damaged mitochondria. Furthermore, α-Syn inserts into the inner mitochondrial membrane and interferes with [[Complex I|Complex I activity]], further increasing ROS production.

[[Oxidative Stress|Oxidative stress]] and protein stress eventually overwhelm neuronal defenses. The failure of mitochondrial dynamics is a key driver. This involves fusion proteins [[MFN2]] and [[OPA1]]. First, Mfn2 connects mitochondria to the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum|endoplasmic reticulum]] ([[MERCS|MAMs]]). In PD, excessive loss of Mfn2 breaks this connection. This disrupts [[Calcium|calcium transfer]] and causes energy failure. Additionally, OPA1 maintains the structure of mitochondrial cristae. In PD, the loss of OPA1 causes cristae to collapse. This structural change releases [[Cytochrome c|cytochrome c]] to start [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. It also reduces energy efficiency and causes a burst of [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]].

Neurons also fail to clear these damaged organelles. The protein [[Miro1]] anchors mitochondria to microtubules. Miro1 must be degraded to stop mitochondrial movement, allowing [[Autophagosome|autophagosomes]] to engulf them. However, Hsieh et al. showed that PD neurons fail to remove damaged mitochondria due to Miro1 retention. They found this defect in neurons from 16 out of 17 sporadic PD patients. In healthy neurons, Miro1 levels drop to less than 20% after stress. In contrast, PD neurons keep Miro1 levels high at 80%–100%. Consequently, the failure to degrade Miro1 halts mitophagy initiation, leading to the persistent retention of dysfunctional mitochondria.

The accumulation of damaged mitochondria triggers lethal signals. One major pathway is [[Inflammation|inflammation]]. Retained mitochondria release [[mtDNA]]. This mtDNA acts as a danger signal. It triggers the [[cGAS-STING Pathway|cGAS-STING pathway]]. This causes inflammation and kills [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neurons]]. Another pathway links clearance defects to [[Ferroptosis|ferroptosis]]. Recent work shows that inhibiting mitophagy increases lipid [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]], speeding up neuronal death. Research found that restoring mitophagy via [[USP30]] knockdown reduced lipid ROS by about 40% and reduced neuronal death. This suggests a critical link between mitochondrial control and ferroptosis. Understanding these mechanisms helps researchers find clinical [[Biomarker|biomarkers]]. Many focus on α-Syn. Higher levels of α-Syn oligomers in [[Cerebrospinal Fluid|cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)]] and plasma have been identified as robust biomarkers for PD diagnosis.

### 4.3 Huntington's Disease

[[Huntington's Disease|HD]] is a neurodegenerative disorder caused by the pathological expansion of a trinucleotide (CAG) repeat sequence within exon 1 of the [[HTT|huntingtin (HTT) gene]]. The disease is characterized by the selective death of [[Medium spiny neurons|medium spiny neurons]] in the [[Striatum|striatum]], where a growing body of evidence implicates the dysregulation of [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]] as a key contributor to neuronal damage in HD. The role of mitophagy in HD appears complex and bidirectional. On one hand, studies indicate that the expanded polyQ tracts in [[Huntingtin|mHtt]] can impair the formation of the autophagy initiation complex in striatal cells, thereby disrupting the autophagic process and contributing to mitophagic dysfunction. Simultaneously, mHtt disrupts [[Mitochondrial Dynamics|mitochondrial dynamics]], leading to elevated [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] levels and systemic [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction|mitochondrial dysfunction]]. These interconnected deficits in clearance and function likely create a permissive environment for neuronal toxicity. On the other hand, research also suggests that excessive or untimely mitophagy may play a detrimental role under specific conditions. For instance, the accumulation of [[VCP|Valosin-containing protein (VCP)]], which binds to mHtt, has been shown to induce excessive mitophagy, leading to neuronal death. Furthermore, evidence points to the GTP hydrolase Rhes—highly enriched in the striatum—as another mediator; its upregulation may trigger mitophagy via the [[BNIP3L|NIX]] receptor, contributing to striatal cell death.

> [!warning] The HD Mitophagy Paradox
> In [[Huntington's Disease|HD]], [[Huntingtin|mHtt]] does not simply suppress or enhance mitophagy, but rather uncouples mitochondrial fission from degradation while simultaneously disrupting autophagic flux. Broad-spectrum autophagy activators like [[Rapamycin]] might be insufficient or detrimental if downstream transport and lysosomal fusion remain blocked by mHtt.

Additionally, mitophagy requires not only the formation of autophagosomes but also their transport to the cell body for fusion with [[Lysosome|lysosomes]]. The interaction between the mitophagy receptor [[OPTN|Optineurin]] and its partner protein [[Rab8]] is compromised by mHtt. This impairs the efficient retrograde transport of mitochondria-containing autophagosomes along microtubules to the cell body, where lysosomes are primarily located, thereby preventing the autophagic process from proceeding normally.

### 4.4 Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

[[ALS]], also known as motor neuron disease, is characterized by abnormal aggregation of pathological proteins [[TDP-43]] and [[SOD1]]. The exact cause of ALS remains uncertain, but more and more studies have shown that ALS-related genes are involved in regulating [[Mitophagy|mitophagy]], including TDP-43, SOD1, [[DRP1|Drp1]], and [[OPA1]]. Among them, increased expression of TDP-43 has been proven to induce [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction|mitochondrial dysfunction]], including decreased [[Mitochondrial Membrane Potential|mitochondrial membrane potential]] and increased [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] production. Conversely, inhibiting TDP-43 expression reduces mitochondrial [[Complex I]] activity and diminishes [[ATP]] synthesis.

Intriguingly, there are also studies that have found that inducing strong autophagy in the spinal cord exacerbated the phenotype of WTxQ331K mice with TDP-43 expression, manifested as shortened lifespan, accelerated loss of motor neurons and significant clearance of TDP-43 in the cell nucleus. This is likely related to the excessive clearance of mitochondria in motor neurons. Additionally, Drp1 is dephosphorylated in several ALS models, including those with SOD1 and TDP-43 mutations, and this dephosphorylation is mediated by the pathological induction of [[PP1|protein phosphatase 1 (PP1)]] activity in these models. In primary neuron culture models, [[iPSC|iPSC-derived human motor neurons]] and in vivo [[Zebrafish|zebrafish]] models, inhibiting the PP1-Drp1 cascade reaction effectively prevented ALS-related symptoms, including mitochondrial fragmentation, damage to mitochondrial complex I, axonal degeneration and cell death. Beyond these factors, [[OPTN]] mutations have been identified as pathogenic in ALS, as mitophagy critically depends on OPTN and its kinase [[TBK1]]. Recent studies reveal that OPTN directly interacts with the microtubule and mitochondrial transport complex [[TRAK1]]/[[KIF5B]] in a C-terminal-dependent manner to stabilize them for proper anterograde axonal mitochondrial transport. Notably, the overexpression of OPTN/TRAK1/KIF5B not only prevents neurodegeneration induced by OPTN truncation but also protects against damage from high intraocular pressure while promoting optic nerve regeneration. These findings suggest that OPTN acts as a microtubule-dependent promoter of mitochondrial transport; its absence may constitute a primary molecular mechanism driving neurodegeneration.

## 5. Targeting Key Molecular Factors of Mitophagy for the Treatment of NDDs

[[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]] are closely related to [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction|mitochondrial dysfunction]]. The accumulation of damaged [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] leads to [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], bioenergetic dysfunction, and protein toxicity cascades. [[Mitophagy]] selectively eliminates defective mitochondria. How to achieve a precise balance between "removing dysfunctional" and "preserving healthy" mitochondria remains a core challenge in the development of drugs targeting the ubiquitination pathway. Therefore, regulators that target the regulation of mitophagy can play an important role in treating NDDs by activating the protective pathways of mitophagy or correcting excessive mitophagy.

> [!important] Therapeutic Challenge
> Achieving precise bidirectional regulation of mitophagy—clearing damaged mitochondria without excessive removal or toxic accumulation from transport blockages—is the central challenge for NDD therapeutics.

### 5.1 Alzheimer's Disease

Mitophagy regulators are considered promising therapeutic strategies for enhancing mitochondrial function in the elderly and improving [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]]-related symptoms. In this field, researchers have explored various synthetic compounds and natural products that regulate mitophagy to eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria.

A major class of natural regulators involves the activation of the [[AMPK]]-Sirtuin signaling axis. Preclinical studies (in vitro and AD animal models) have shown that [[Resveratrol]], a non-flavonoid polyphenolic compound, promotes the clearance of [[Amyloid Beta|Aβ]] peptides. Rather than inhibiting Aβ generation, it primarily activates the [[Proteasome]] and the [[AMPK]]-[[SIRT1]]-dependent autophagy pathway to degrade intracellular Aβ deposits. Concurrently, it mitigates [[Neurofibrillary tangles|neurofibrillary tangles]] by reducing [[Tau Protein|Tau]] phosphorylation at Ser396 via [[GSK3|GSK-3β]] modulation and promoting ubiquitin-proteasome degradation of Tau through SIRT1-mediated deacetylation. Similarly, Wang et al. found that [[Aloe-Emodin]] activates mitophagy via the [[AMPK]]/[[PGC-1α]]/[[SIRT3]] pathway. In AD models, Aloe-Emodin enhances mitochondrial function, reducing [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] and restoring membrane potential. Mechanistically, the upregulation of mitochondrial SIRT3 is crucial; this protein regulates mitochondrial proteins and promotes mitophagy, as evidenced by changes in [[Beclin1]], [[LC3]], [[p62]], [[Parkin]], and [[PINK1]] levels.

Other natural compounds function by directly modulating mitophagy receptors or upstream kinases, though some exhibit stage-dependent effects. [[β-Asarone]] (from Acorus tatarinowii) has been confirmed to enhance mitophagy by up-regulating PINK1 and Parkin in neurons, reducing hippocampal Aβ load and improving cognition. However, its role is complex and stage-dependent; in late-stage AD, β-Asarone may exert neuroprotection by inhibiting Beclin-1-dependent autophagy, suggesting that excessive autophagic activation in advanced pathology could be detrimental. Additionally, [[Kaempferol]] (a flavonoid) and [[Rhapontigenin]] act as natural inducers that promote mitochondrial fission and increase mitophagy-related proteins, thereby reducing p-Tau and Aβ levels. Kaempferol is particularly notable for its ability to penetrate the [[Blood-Brain Barrier|blood-brain barrier]].

Emerging research has also highlighted the critical link between lysosomal function and mitophagy. [[Urolithin A]] (UA), a metabolite of ellagic acid, represents a new target for combined intervention. Preclinical studies in AD transgenic mice demonstrate that UA induces mitophagy not just by targeting mitochondria, but by enhancing lysosomal function—specifically by normalizing the activity of lysosomal [[Cathepsin|cathepsins]] (such as cathepsin Z). This restoration of the "lysosomal sink" significantly reduces Aβ and Tau pathology, improves olfactory and cognitive functions, and restores [[Synaptic plasticity|synaptic plasticity]].

Beyond natural products, synthetic strategies are being developed to target specific protein interactions. [[UMI-77]], an [[Mcl-1|MCL-1]] inhibitor, targets the mitophagy receptor MCL-1. By inhibiting the interaction between MCL-1 and [[BAX]], UMI-77 releases free MCL-1, which then binds to [[LC3|LC3A]] to selectively eliminate damaged mitochondria. In AD mouse models, two months of treatment significantly reduced Aβ deposition and [[Neuroinflammation]] while improving cognition. Furthermore, broad lifestyle interventions support these molecular findings; diets rich in [[Antioxidant|antioxidants]] and [[Polyphenols|polyphenols]], alongside regular [[Exercise|exercise]], have been shown to enhance mitochondrial health and may delay AD progression.

Translating these preclinical findings into clinical applications is the next critical step. In a phase II randomized double-blind controlled trial, sustained-release [[Melatonin]] (2 mg daily) significantly improved cognitive performance, sleep efficiency, and, in subgroups with [[Insomnia|insomnia]], daily living ability and MMSE scores. [[Resveratrol]] has also been tested in a phase II trial for mild-to-moderate AD; while it showed an acceptable safety profile and a potential trend toward stabilizing cognitive decline, it did not significantly lower plasma Aβ levels. Finally, [[Urolithin A]] has demonstrated safety, bioavailability, and the ability to induce mitochondrial gene expression in a first-in-human phase I trial in elderly individuals.

> [!tip] Mito-Lysosomal Dual Repair
> The mechanism of [[Urolithin A]] suggests that inducing mitophagy is insufficient if downstream [[Lysosome|lysosomes]] cannot effectively process the sequestered waste. This mismatch creates "autophagic stress," where cellular waste is "packaged" but not "incinerated." Future drug screening should prioritize a "mito-lysosomal dual repair" mechanism.

### 5.2 Parkinson's Disease

[[Mitophagy]] is a crucial pathogenic mechanism underlying [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neuron]] loss in [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]]. Whether in familial or sporadic cases, mitophagy dysfunction is a hallmark of the disease. Proteins encoded by the [[PINK1]] and [[PARK2|PRKN]] (Parkin) genes are central to the mitophagy process. While early-onset PD is often directly linked to defects in these genes, impaired mitophagy is also a consistent feature of sporadic PD. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting the PINK1/Parkin pathway and its regulatory networks have emerged as a major area of drug discovery.

One direct approach involves pharmacological agents that enhance [[Parkin]] activity. For instance, the lipid-lowering drug [[Lovastatin]] functions as an agonist of the phosphatase [[SHP2]]. By binding to SHP2, lovastatin promotes its translocation to mitochondria, where it interacts with Parkin and dephosphorylates the Tyr143 residue. This specific dephosphorylation unlocks Parkin's E3 [[Ubiquitin|ubiquitin]] ligase activity, thereby enhancing mitophagy and alleviating motor deficits in [[MPTP]]-induced PD mouse models. Supporting the translational potential of this mechanism, a recent case-control study in an elderly Japanese population found that [[Statins|statin]] use was associated with a reduced risk of PD. Notably, a lower cumulative statin dose was associated with an increased risk of PD, whereas a higher cumulative dose showed a protective effect against its onset.

Beyond small molecules, novel delivery systems are being developed to overcome bioavailability challenges. Targeted nanomedicines, such as [[Lycopene]] nanodots (TPP coupled with ferritin nanocages), have been designed to specifically target neuronal mitochondria. Due to their high [[Bioavailability|biocompatibility]], these nanodots can effectively protect mitochondrial function in MPTP models, promote PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, and accelerate [[Alpha-synuclein|α-Syn]] excretion, ultimately restoring motor function in mice. In addition to direct PINK1/Parkin targeting, modulating co-regulators of mitophagy offers another therapeutic avenue. Studies indicate that [[Ambra-1]] interacts with Parkin and is crucial for mitochondrial clearance. In in vitro PD models ([[SH-SY5Y]] cells), the overexpression of AMBRA1 was sufficient to promote mitophagy and synergistically reduce apoptotic cell death induced by neurotoxins such as [[Rotenone]] and [[6-hydroxydopamine|6-OHDA]].

Conversely, inhibiting negative regulators of mitophagy, such as the deubiquitinase [[USP30]], represents a parallel strategy. While PINK1 and Parkin orchestrate the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria by inducing ubiquitylation, this process is antagonized by USP30-mediated deubiquitylation. Recent studies demonstrate that the loss of USP30 in [[Knockout mouse|knockout mice]] prevents behavioral deficits, increases mitophagy, and reduces both phosphorylated S129 [[Alpha-synuclein|α-Syn]] levels and α-Syn-induced dopaminergic neuron loss. These findings were validated using [[MTX115325]], a potent, selective, and brain-penetrant USP30 inhibitor. These data strongly support further research into USP30 inhibition as a potential disease-modifying therapy.

> [!info] USP30: A Negative Regulator of Mitophagy
> [[USP30]] is a mitochondrial deubiquitinase that opposes [[Parkin]]-mediated mitophagy by removing ubiquitin chains from OMM proteins. Loss or inhibition of USP30 enhances mitophagy, reduces [[Alpha-synuclein|α-Syn]] pathology, and protects [[Dopaminergic Neurons|dopaminergic neurons]] — positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]].

Additionally, targeting upstream kinases and oxidative stress pathways shows promise in restoring mitochondrial homeostasis. The kinase inhibitor [[GSK3357679A]] inhibits pathogenic [[LRRK2]] activity and has been shown to restore mitophagy defects in LRRK2 G2019S mice. This study is the first to demonstrate that targeting LRRK2 can rescue mitophagy damage in vivo. Furthermore, in MPTP-induced models, [[Sulforaphane]] has been shown to reduce neuronal loss and inhibit [[Microglia|microglial]] activation by modulating the [[NRF2|Nrf2]]-mediated oxidative stress response. Clinical evaluation is currently underway, with phase II trials assessing the safety and efficacy of sulforaphane in improving movement symptoms.

> [!tip] Shift to Precision Regulation
> Current research suggests a shift in treatment strategies for [[Parkinson's Disease|PD]] from non-specific "broad-spectrum autophagy activation" to "precise molecular regulation." While clearing damaged mitochondria is crucial, excessive or uncontrolled autophagy risks mitochondrial depletion or synaptic loss. [[USP30]] inhibition or [[Ambra-1]] overexpression mainly focus on "stepping on the gas." Determining the optimal therapeutic window remains the core challenge.

## 6. Conclusion

[[Mitophagy]] plays a complex, dual role in [[Neurodegenerative Diseases|NDDs]], where both its impairment and overactivation can precipitate neuronal damage and subsequent neurological disorders. Despite the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway, several critical limitations persist.

First, the precise mechanisms distinguishing physiological from pathological mitophagy, along with the specific "damage threshold," remain elusive. For example, in [[Huntington's Disease|HD]], the accumulation of [[VCP|Valosin-containing protein (VCP)]] induces excessive mitophagy, leading to neuronal death. Similarly, in an [[ALS]] mouse model (WTxQ331K), inducing robust autophagy shortened lifespan and accelerated motor neuron loss. In [[Alzheimer's Disease|AD]] research, [[β-Asarone]] may cause adverse effects in the late stages of the disease due to the excessive activation of autophagy. Moreover, effective mitophagy requires not only autophagosome formation but also their successful transport to [[Lysosome|lysosomes]] for degradation. In HD, [[Huntingtin|mHtt]] blocks the retrograde transport of these autophagosomes. Consequently, utilizing broad-spectrum autophagy activators like [[Rapamycin]] could be detrimental if downstream transport remains obstructed, as this would exacerbate the accumulation of toxic aggregates.

Furthermore, most studies rely on cell or animal models. We still need to verify their direct relevance to human disease. Additionally, a major obstacle in treating NDDs is drug delivery. Drugs must effectively cross the [[Blood-Brain Barrier|blood-brain barrier]] and remain stable. Researchers are exploring nanocarrier technology to address this issue, and they have made some progress. For instance, in a mouse model of [[Parkinson's Disease|Parkinson's disease]], scientists constructed sequence-targeted H-ferritin nanocages. These nanocages were loaded with [[Lycopene|lycopene]]. This system successfully crossed the blood-brain barrier and also specifically targeted mitochondria within neurons. This approach not only cleared [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] but also significantly restored the autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria. Additionally, it accelerated the metabolism of [[Alpha-synuclein|α-syn]].

> [!important] Future Directions
> Current research shows a shift from "broad-spectrum autophagy activation" to "precise molecular regulation." Nano-targeted delivery technology offers new solutions for delivering drugs into the brain and mitochondria, addressing a key bottleneck in treatment. Achieving precise bidirectional regulation of mitophagy—controlling the quality and quantity of mitochondria in neurons—remains the central challenge and provides an important direction for future research.
