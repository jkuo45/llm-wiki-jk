## Abstract

Ageing is a plastic process and can be successfully modulated by some biomedical approaches or pharmaceutics. In this manner it is possible to delay or even prevent some age-related pathologies. There are some defined interventions, which give promising results in animal models or even in human studies, resulting in lifespan elongation or healthspan improvement. One of the most promising targets for anti-ageing approaches are proteins belonging to the [[Sirtuins|sirtuin family]]. Sirtuins were originally discovered as transcription repressors in yeast, however, nowadays they are known to occur in bacteria and eukaryotes (including mammals). In humans the family consists of seven members ([[SIRT1|SIRT1-7]]) that possess either mono-ADP ribosyltransferase or deacetylase activity. It is believed that sirtuins play key role during cell response to a variety of stresses, such as oxidative or genotoxic stress and are crucial for cell metabolism. Although some data put in question direct involvement of sirtuins in extending human lifespan, it was documented that proper lifestyle including physical activity and diet can influence healthspan via increasing the level of sirtuins. The search for an activator of sirtuins is one of the most extensive and robust topic of research. Some hopes are put on natural compounds, including [[Curcumin|curcumin]]. In this review we summarize the involvement and usefulness of sirtuins in anti-ageing interventions and discuss the potential role of [[Curcumin|curcumin]] in sirtuins regulation.

**Keywords:** Sirtuins, Ageing, Senescence, Curcumin

## Introduction

In the year 1979 a paper announcing discovery of mating-type regulator 1 (MAR1) in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* was published (Klar et al. [^116]). Lack of this protein resulted in the inhibition of silencing of HM loci, which control the mating type and sterility in yeast. Three more proteins with similar function were discovered later in 1979 and the nomenclature was unified thus creating a family of Sir (silent information regulator) proteins (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). Shortly, it was shown that sirtuins are evolutionarily conserved from bacteria to humans (Vaquero [^212]). We now know a number of processes sirtuins are involved in and we still discover their new functions. In bacteria phosphoribosyltransferases cobT and cobB catalyze the synthesis of the cobalamin biosynthetic intermediate (which transfers a ribose-phosphate moiety from nicotinic acid mononucleotide (NaMN) to dimethyl benzimidazole 2) and in archaea Sir-2-Af1 and Sir2-Af2 participate in transcription regulation (Tsang and Escalente-Semerena [^207]). While in prokaryotes there are usually one or two sirtuin genes, eukaryotes can have multiple sirtuin genes. In yeast, in addition to the chief representative, Sir2, there are four more homologous proteins (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). In mammals there are seven enzymes belonging to the sirtuin family, among which [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] (silent information regulator T1) has the highest sequence homology to Sir2 in yeast and is the best studied family member. Modulation of sirtuin activity in mammals can regulate many processes such as gene expression, cell metabolism, [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]], [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]], cell cycle, development, immune response and neuroprotection (Michan and Sinclair [^149]).

A significant rise in the interest in sirtuins occurred in 1999 when it was reported that Sir2 overexpression can extend yeast lifespan by as much as 70% (Kaeberlein et al. [^104]). The anti-ageing action of sirtuins appears to be conserved from yeast to mammals, however the complexity of their function increases with the complexity of the organism. In yeast, the positive effect of sirtuins activity can be attributed to the increase in genomic stability in two ways. There are from 100 to 200 copies of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) in each yeast cell, however, only half of them are transcriptionally active, the rest remains silent (Sinclair and Guarente [^194]). Together with other proteins, Sir2 participates in silencing of these regions. Such silencing prevents recombination between rDNA repeats and formation and accumulation of extrachromosomal rDNA circles (ERCs), which are a leading cause of yeast ageing (Sinclair and Guarente [^194]). Mutations in Sir2 gene lead to accelerated accumulation of toxic ERCs, whereas Sir2 overexpression extends *S. cerevisiae* lifespan by silencing HML/R loci and inhibiting ERCs formation (Kaeberlein et al. [^104]). Furthermore, along with yeast ageing Sir2 dissociates from HM loci, which results in termination of HM silencing and in sterility, which is a sign of yeast senescence (Sinclair and Guarente [^194]). Therefore, changes in the localization of Sir2 result in epigenetic alterations that favor ageing. It was shown that Sir2 is indispensable for mediating positive effects of [[Caloric Restriction|calorie restriction]] in yeast (Lin et al. [^133]). It was also observed that the level of Sir2 increases during [[Caloric Restriction|calorie restriction]] in *S. cerevisiae* (Bordone and Guarente [^23]).

Further research revealed that sirtuin overexpression leads to lifespan extension also in other model organisms such as *Caenorhabditis elegans* and *Drosophila melanogaster*. In mammals sirtuins regulate numerous signaling pathways (not only those directly involved in ageing and senescence). This complex influence of sirtuins on mammalian ageing is discussed in this review.

### Function, structure and localization

In the early 1990s Braunstein et al. showed that regions silenced by Sir2 were characterized by reduced histone acetylation at the ε-amino group of N-terminal lysine residues (Braunstein et al. [^30]). Some authors also observed that Sir2 overexpression in yeast led to global hypoacetylation. Soon it was discovered that the main activity of sirtuins is deacetylation of lysine residues. This is a two-step reaction—firstly sirtuins cleave nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide ([[NAD+|NAD]]) to [[Nicotinamide|nicotinamide (NAM)]] and, subsequently, an acetyl/acyl group is transferred from the substrate to the ADP-ribose moiety of NAD; this results in the formation of 2′-*O*-acetyl-ADP-ribose and a deacetylated substrate (Tanner et al. [^202]).

Sirtuins belong to class III histone deacetylases (HDAC). A distinguishing feature of this class is that the catalytic activity of the enzymes depends on [[NAD+|NAD+]] and is regulated by dynamic changes in [[NAD+|NAD+]] level and the [[NAD+|NAD+]]/NADH ratio. Such requirement for [[NAD+|NAD+]] as a co-substrate suggests that sirtuins might have evolved as sensors of energy and redox status in the cell (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). There are two pathways of [[NAD+|NAD+]] biosynthesis—de novo production and the so called salvage pathway. In the salvage pathway NAM is converted to nicotinamide mononucleotide ([[NMN|NMN]]) by [[NAMPT|nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT)]], a limiting enzyme for the whole pathway. Subsequently, NMN is converted to [[NAD+|NAD+]] by [[NMNAT|NMN/NaMN adenylyltransferase (NMNAT)]] (Chung et al. [^43]). The level of [[NAMPT|NAMPT]] can influence sirtuin activity. [[NAD+|NAD+]] synthesis is coupled with the circadian/daily cycle due to the fact that [[NAMPT|NAMPT]] is regulated by a complex consisting of [[CLOCK|CLOCK]] (circadian locomotor output cycles kaput) and [[BMAL1|BMAL1]] (brain and muscle aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator-like 1) (Nakagawa and Guarente [^163]). Unlike [[NAMPT|NAMPT]], [[PARP1|PARP1]] activation by DNA damage results in a decrease in the [[NAD+|NAD+]] level ([[PARP1|PARP1]] uses [[NAD+|NAD+]] as a cofactor) and inhibition of sirtuin activity (Zhang [^233]). NAM (another product of the reaction catalyzed by sirtuins) is a non-competitive inhibitor of sirtuin activity (Chung et al. [^43]).

> [!info] NAD+ couples sirtuin activity to cellular energy state
> Because every deacetylation consumes a molecule of [[NAD+|NAD+]] and releases [[Nicotinamide|nicotinamide (NAM)]], sirtuin flux is gated by the [[NAD+|NAD+]]/NAM ratio. [[NAMPT|NAMPT]]-driven salvage raises [[NAD+|NAD+]], whereas [[PARP1|PARP1]] activation during DNA damage and NAM feedback inhibition both suppress sirtuin activity—a key interface between DNA damage and sirtuin-dependent ageing.

Sirtuins deacetylate not only histones but also some transcription factors and cytoplasmic proteins. Recent research shed some new light on sirtuins as it was shown that in addition to deacetylation they can remove some other moieties as well. For example, [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] catalytic activity increases with the size of the aliphatic tail it removes, so that palmitoyl, myristoyl or butyryl are favored over acetyl moiety (Gertler and Cohen [^68]). Therefore, it is now considered that sirtuins are not deacetylases but a more general term is proposed—deacylases (Jiang et al. [^100]). Acetylation is a post-translational protein modification which can affect, among others, catalytic activity, stability and ability to bind to other proteins or chromatin (which is especially important in the case of histones).

In human we can distinguish seven sirtuins ([[SIRT1|SIRT1-7]]). Their catalytic domain consists of 275 amino acids and is common to all family members. Activity of some sirtuins is not limited only to protein deacetylation. ADP-ribosylation is the main activity for [[SIRT4|SIRT4]], which lacks deacetylase activity, and is also characteristic for [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] (Morris [^155]). Moreover, [[SIRT5|SIRT5]] can demalonylate and desuccinylate proteins (Du et al. [^55]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]], [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] and [[SIRT7|SIRT7]] localize mainly in the nucleus. [[SIRT7|SIRT7]] has been found to be a part of the RNA Pol I transcription machinery and is expressed in the nucleoli where it can bind to histones and positively regulate rDNA transcription (Ford et al. [^64]). [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] can be found mostly in the cytoplasm where its main substrate is [[α-tubulin|α-tubulin]] (Li et al. [^128]). Still, a fraction of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] can translocate to the nucleus where it takes part in regulation of the cell cycle (Dryden et al. [^54]). [[SIRT3|SIRT3]], [[SIRT4|SIRT4]] and [[SIRT5|SIRT5]] have been termed mitochondrial sirtuins. [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] is cleaved to its active form by the mitochondrial matrix processing peptidase (Schwer et al. [^190]). Full-length [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] resides in the nucleus, however, in response to stress (such as DNA damage) it translocates to the [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] (Scher et al. [^189]).

### Anti-ageing potential of sirtuins: in vivo and in vitro studies

Ageing is associated with numerous changes at the organismal, tissue as well as cellular level. With age, senescent cells accumulate in many tissues impairing their proper functioning. Senescent cells have a strong impact on surrounding cells. They modify the microenvironment by secreting certain cytokines, chemokines and mediators of inflammation. Such secretory phenotype is one of the causes of a low grade inflammation observed in old individuals and can induce senescence in neighboring cells as well as support tumor progression. Senescent cells, apart from the secretory phenotype, possess a set of features such as increased: level of cell cycle inhibitors, activity of senescence associated β-galactosidase, granularity and DNA damage. The elevation of DNA damage with age is the result of impaired efficiency of [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] systems. It is believed that DNA damage is the main cause of [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]]. It concerns both replicative (critically short [[Telomere|telomeres]] are considered as DNA double strand breaks) and stress (oxidative, genotoxic) induced senescence. DNA damage is associated with normal functioning of cells and efficient repair systems are sufficient to protect cells from its accumulation. However, age-related decrease in the ability to repair DNA, causes increased damage accumulation and, in consequence, cell senescence. Sirtuins are indispensable for [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]], controlling inflammation and antioxidative defense which makes them good anti-senescence/anti-ageing targets.

[[Caloric Restriction|Calorie restriction (CR)]] is so far the only effective way to extend lifespan without genetic or pharmacological intervention (more information about CR in the chapter concerning Intervention). The effects of [[Caloric Restriction|calorie restriction]] (besides lifespan extension) are manifested by physiological and behavioral changes such as reduced size, decreased level of growth factors, glucose, triglycerides and increase in the locomotor and foraging activity (McCarter et al. [^145]; Weed et al. [^223]). The level of almost all sirtuins, except [[SIRT4|SIRT4]], increases as an effect of [[Caloric Restriction|calorie restriction]] (Watroba and Szukiewicz [^222]). Therefore, it is believed that sirtuins mediate beneficial effects elicited by such diet. However, sirtuin anti-ageing activity is not limited to mediating the CR effects. Plethora of in vivo and in vitro studies show importance of these enzymes for reaching a lifespan characteristic for a particular species.

#### SIRT1

[[SIRT1|SIRT1]] is the best studied in the family. It plays an important role during fetal development. In the case of mouse zygotes lacking both copies of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] gene only half of the expected individuals are born of which only 20% reach maturity. Such mice are sterile, smaller than normal individuals, develop more slowly and experience abnormalities in morphogenesis of the eye and heart. The latter likely contributes to the neonatal lethality of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] depleted mice (McBurney et al. [^144]; Cheng et al. [^41]). Additionally, among heterozygous embryos cases of anencephaly were reported.

The level of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] decreases in the liver with age, probably due to lower [[NAD+|NAD+]] availability (Braidy et al. [^28]) while a simultaneous increase in accumulation of DNA damage occurs. Age-dependent decrease in the level of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] was observed also in the arteries, suggesting its involvement in the ageing of the cardiovascular system (Bai et al. [^7]). Decrease in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]], caused by accelerated senescence of cord blood endothelial cells, was also a cause of early vascular dysfunction observed in low birth weight preterm infants (Vassallo et al. [^215]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deficiency promoted expression of genes characteristic for ageing (Hwang et al. [^92]).

Mice with an extra copy of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] gene are characterized by a lower level of DNA damage and of p16, which are the hallmarks of ageing (Herranz et al. [^82]). It was shown, that tissue-specific overexpression of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] in cardiac muscle cells diminished the area affected by myocardial infarction and facilitated recovery (Hsu et al. [^87]). It was also shown that some single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) in the [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] gene could affect [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activity and correlate with BMI and a tendency to diet-induced obesity (Clark et al. [^45]). However, no correlation between changes in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activity (caused by SNP) and lifespan extension was found (Flachsbart et al. [^62]).

[[SIRT1|SIRT1]] was shown to delay replicative senescence of normal human umbilical cord fibroblasts and regulate both replicative and premature senescence in stem cells and differentiated cells exposed to oxidative stress (Bellizzi et al. [^14]; Brown et al. [^31]). Activation of the salvage pathway in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC) results in an increase in the replicative lifespan of these cells due to [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activation (Canto et al. [^35]). Moreover, it was demonstrated that inhibition of [[NAMPT|NAMPT]] led to premature replicative senescence, while its overexpression delayed it (Yang and Sauve [^228]). The level of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] decreases in tissues, in which cells proliferate during the organismal lifespan or during long term in vitro culture, as we have recently also shown for VSMC (Bielak-Zmijewska et al. [^20]), but not in immortalized cells (Sasaki et al. [^185]). In H2O2- or genotoxic stress-induced cellular senescence [[PARP1|PARP1]] becomes activated, which results in depletion of [[NAD+|NAD+]] resources and leads to a decrease in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activity (Furukawa et al. [^67]). There are data suggesting that [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can be involved in decision-making over [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]] or [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. In the 3′UTR region of the [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] transcript there is a HuR binding site. HuR is an RNA-binding protein, which can stabilize a transcript when bound. The level of HuR decreases dramatically during senescence (which can also be the cause of the decrease in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] level observed with ageing). In response to oxidative DNA damage HuR is phosphorylated by Chk2, which leads to its dissociation from [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] mRNA. As a result, there is a decrease in the level of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and cells become more prone to [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] (Abdelmohsen et al. [^1]). It is possible that the described phenomenon is one of the mechanisms responsible for sustaining the balance between [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]], senescence and [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. High level of DNA damage can activate Chk2, which leads to a decrease in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] level and moves the balance towards apoptosis.

Pleiotropic activity of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] makes it an important marker of [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]] as well as some diseases such as cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes or cancer (Nakagawa and Guarente [^163]).

#### SIRT2

Expression of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] decreases in fat tissue of obese people (Krishnan et al. [^119]). On the other hand, the level of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] increases in white fat tissue and kidneys of mice subjected to [[Caloric Restriction|calorie restriction]] (Wang et al. [^217]). Recent studies suggest that [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] can serve as a cellular senescence marker. It was shown that the level of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] increased in senescent cells (regardless of whether the inducing factor was stress, oncogene or exhaustion of replicative potential) but not in quiescent cells or in cells that entered [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] (Anwar et al. [^4]). At the same time, the authors excluded [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] as an indispensable factor in senescence induction. This suggests that the increase in the level of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] is rather the effect of the changes occurring in cells during senescence, than the cause of senescence.

> [!note] SIRT2 as a senescence marker
> Elevated [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] is observed across stress-, oncogene- and replicative-induced senescence but is a consequence rather than a driver of the senescent state—useful as a marker, not a target to block senescence induction.

#### SIRT3

[[SIRT3|SIRT3]] is the only sirtuin for which evidence exists that it can influence longevity in humans. It was shown that a certain polymorphism in [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] gene can be found more often in long-lived people (Bellizzi et al. [^15], [^14]). A variable number of tandem repeats in intron five enhancer region can affect activity of this enhancer. People carrying the allele with the least active enhancer were less likely to survive to an old age. Such variant was practically absent in men over 90 years old living in Italy (Bellizzi et al. [^14]). However, studies of other larger populations did not confirm those findings, suggesting that [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] influence on longevity is negligible or even nonexistent (Lescai et al. [^126]; Rose et al. [^179]).

Mice lacking [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] are characterized by decreased oxygen consumption and simultaneous increase in [[Reactive Oxygen Species|reactive oxygen species (ROS)]] production as well as higher [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] in muscle (Jing et al. [^102]). Such observations were confirmed in cell culture—cells lacking [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] had increased ROS level, which could induce DNA damage and activate [[HIF-1α|HIF1α]] (Finley et al. [^61]; Bell et al. [^13]). [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] activates enzymes, that play key roles during CR, such as 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-CoA synthase responsible for ketone formation (Shimazu et al. [^193]) and long chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase responsible for long-chain fatty acid oxidation (Hirschey et al. [^83]).

#### SIRT1, SIRT2, SIRT3

Recent data have shown that the ageing protection mechanism involving sirtuins is quite universal and concerns also germ cells. The ageing of oocytes reduces the quality of metaphase II oocytes, which undergo time-dependent deterioration following ovulation. In mouse oocytes aged in vivo or in vitro the expression of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]], [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] and [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] was dramatically reduced. On the other hand, it has been shown that prolonged expression of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]], [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] and [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] reduced mouse [[Oocyte|oocyte ageing]] both in vitro and in vivo (Zhang et al. [^238]), which suggests a potential protective role of these enzymes against postovulatory ageing. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] are the sensors and guardians of the redox state in oocytes, granulosa cells and early embryos and therefore play a crucial role in female fertility especially when [[Oocyte|oocyte ageing]] is concerned (reviewed in Tatone et al. [^203]).

The age-dependent changes in sirtuin level could be used as a diagnostic tool. Serum sirtuins are considered as a novel noninvasive protein marker of [[Frailty|frailty]] (Kumar et al. [^120]). [[Frailty|Frailty]] is a complex clinical state described as a characteristic set of features among older patients. Diagnosis of [[Frailty|frailty]] is often difficult because of subtle and subjective clinical features, especially at the early stage of the syndrome. To the features of [[Frailty|frailty]] belong: sarcopenia, cognitive decline, abnormal functioning of immune and neuroendocrine systems, poor energy regulation (Clegg et al. [^46]). Currently, there is no defined treatment for [[Frailty|frailty]]. It will be useful to find a set of biochemical abnormalities associated with [[Frailty|frailty]] for better and earlier diagnosis. Sirtuins circulating in serum could be potential markers of [[Frailty|frailty]]. As suggested by analysis of people diagnosed as frail in comparison to non frail individuals, lower levels of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] were associated with [[Frailty|frailty]].

#### SIRT6

The first evidence that sirtuins can be involved in regulation of mammalian ageing came from mice lacking [[SIRT6|SIRT6]]. It appears that among sirtuins, [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] depletion exhibits the most severe phenotype as it seems to be indispensable for reaching a normal lifespan. Three weeks after birth such mice exhibit symptoms of degeneration and premature ageing such as sudden decrease in subcutaneous fat, lordokyphosis, colitis, severe lymphopenia, osteopenia, which all together result in death in about the fourth week of life. [[SIRT6|SIRT6]]−/− mice are also smaller than wild type individuals. Furthermore, severe metabolic abnormalities were observed i.e. low level of [[IGF-1|IGF-1]] and glucose (Mostoslavsky et al. [^158]). Later, it was shown that the main reason of premature death was hypoglycemia caused by increased glucose uptake (due to higher expression of GLUT1 and GLUT 4 transporters) (Xiao et al. [^226]; Zhong et al. [^237]). On the other hand, Kanfi et al. demonstrated that overexpression of [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] could also reduce the activity of the [[IGF-1|IGF-1]] pathway. They observed a decrease in the level of [[IGF-1|IGF-1]], the level of IGF-binding protein was increased, and the phosphorylation status of the main components of the [[IGF-1|IGF-1]] signaling pathway was altered. Such changes facilitated glucose tolerance and reduced fat accumulation, which resulted in lifespan extension of male mice (Kanfi et al. [^108]).

Mouse embryonal fibroblasts (MEF) and embryonal stem (ES) cells devoid of [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] are characterized by decreased proliferation rate and increased genomic instability as well as sensitivity to stress manifested by chromosome fragmentation, detached centromeres, chromosome loss and translocations. [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] level decreases in human fibroblasts during senescence (Sharma et al. [^192]) but also in vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells isolated from human aorta as we have recently demonstrated (Grabowska et al. [^70]).

#### SIRT7

[[SIRT7|SIRT7]]−/− mice age prematurely and are characterized by a progeroid phenotype and lethal heart hypertrophy (Vakhrusheva et al. [^210]). During replicative senescence [[SIRT7|SIRT7]] translocates from nucleoli to chromatin and cytoplasm (Grob et al. [^73]), which can result in reduced rDNA transcription. ### The mechanisms of senescence modulation by sirtuins

The data presented above support the notion that sirtuins play an important role during ageing. It is best evidenced by a widely observed decrease in the level of almost all sirtuins in senescent cells. The mechanism of their action is very complex and not entirely understood yet.

During [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]] changes in chromatin condensation and gene expression occur. Such changes in chromatin structure can influence genome stability, making DNA more susceptible to damage, which is considered the main cause of senescence. Sirtuins play a vital role in sustaining genome integrity. They take part in maintaining normal chromatin condensation state, in [[DNA Repair|DNA damage response and repair]], modulate [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] and energy metabolism. Let us take a closer look at the role of each sirtuin in these processes.

#### Influence on chromatin condensation and gene expression

Among cells isolated from mice lacking both copies of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] gene, almost 40% have impaired chromosome structure including breaks or relaxed/disorganized chromatin (in comparison to 5% in normal individuals) (Wang et al. [^218]). It is suggested that such abnormalities can be the effect of an increase in the acetylation of H3K9, caused by lack of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]]. Acetylation of H3K9 prevents its trimethylation and impairs binding of heterochromatin protein 1 alpha (HP1α) responsible for keeping chromatin in a closed state (Wang et al. [^218]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] (and also other sirtuins), through histone deacetylation, takes part in formation of the constitutive as well as facultative [[Heterochromatin|heterochromatin]]. The removal of acyl groups from histones enhances their affinity to DNA and impedes the access of transcription factors to DNA resulting in silencing of genes neighboring the deacetylated histones (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] preferentially deacetylates H4K16, H3K9, H3K56 and H1K26 (Poulose and Raju [^171]) and also H1K9 and H3K14 during [[Heterochromatin|heterochromatin]] formation (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). It was shown that [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can be found in [[Telomere|telomere]] and pericentromere regions. Oxidative stress inhibits this interaction, which results in altered gene expression (Oberdoerffer et al. [^164]; Palacios et al. [^168]). Moreover, [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deficient mice lack pericentromeric [[Heterochromatin|heterochromatin]] foci (Bosch-Presegué et al. [^26]), which suggest its involvement in formation of constitutive [[Heterochromatin|heterochromatin]].

[[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can influence chromatin condensation not only by deacetylating histones, but also by regulating histone expression and modulating the level and activity of some histone modifying enzymes (Vaquero et al. [^214]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can inhibit [[SUV39H1|Suv39h1]] methyltransferase degradation by inhibiting polyubiquitination of this methyltransferase by MDM2. Moreover, deacetylation of K266 in the catalytic domain of [[SUV39H1|Suv39h1]] activates it (Vaquero et al. [^214]). Therefore, [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] promotes H3K9 trimethylation not only by deacetylation but also through cooperation with [[SUV39H1|Suv39h1]] (Bosch-Presegué and Vaquero [^25]). Under oxidative stress, [[Sirtuins|SIRT1]] along with [[SUV39H1|Suv39h1]] and nucleomethylin initiate formation of facultative [[Heterochromatin|heterochromatin]] in the rDNA region. This, in turn, inhibits ribosome formation and decreases protein expression in general, which protects cells from energy deprivation-dependent [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] (Murayama et al. [^161]) and, facilitates repair. Moreover, [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can deacetylate TBP [TATA-box-binding protein]-associated factor I 68 (TAF I 68) impairing its DNA-binding activity, and in this way, inhibiting RNAPolI-dependent transcription of rDNA (Muth et al. [^162]). In addition to [[SUV39H1|Suv39h1]], [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can modulate the activity of [[P300|p300]] histone acetyltransferase. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] inhibits [[P300|p300]] activity by deacetylating K1020 and K1024 (Bouras et al. [^27]). In this way it contributes to the decreased level of histone acetylation.

[[SIRT2|SIRT2]] participates in formation of metaphase chromosomes via H4K16 deacetylation (Vaquero et al. [^213]). The level of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] fluctuates during cell cycle reaching its peak at the M phase and G2/M transition (Vaquero et al. [^213]). Overexpression of [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] can delay mitotic exit (Dryden et al. [^54]).

[[SIRT3|SIRT3]], as the main mitochondrial deacetylase, plays an important role in homeostasis of these organelles. Under stress the nuclear fraction of [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] can deacetylate H4K16 and H3K9 regulating expression of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolism (Scher et al. [^189]). Moreover, no hyperacetylation is observed in [[SIRT3|SIRT3]]−/− cells, which suggests that [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] is involved in regulation of only specific genes or regions (Scher et al. [^189]).

[[SIRT6|SIRT6]] is a deacetylase as well as ADP-riboylase acting mainly on histones. This sirtuin deacetylates H3K9 in the promotor regions of, among others, genes involved in metabolism (Zhong et al. [^237]). In MEF and ES cells derived from [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] knockout mice, H3K9 hyperacetylation in [[Telomere|telomeres]] was observed. Such hyperacetylation caused a decrease in the level of trimethylated H3K9 in [[Telomere|telomeres]] and chromatin relaxation in these regions. This suggests that [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] can protect cells from [[Telomere|telomere]] dysfunction (Cardus et al. [^36]). In particular, [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] deacetylates H3K9 in [[Telomere|telomere]] regions in response to [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] (Gertler and Cohen [^68]), which results in tightening and stabilization of the [[Telomere|telomere]] structure. [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] [[Telomere|telomere]] binding is dynamic, and the strongest interaction is observed during the S phase of the cell cycle (Michishita et al. [^150]). Moreover, [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] stabilized ATP-dependent helicase [[WRN|WRN]] and prevented [[Telomere|telomere]] dysfunction during [[DNA Repair|DNA replication]] (Gertler and Cohen [^68]). [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] substrates also include H2BK12 and H3K56, increased acetylation level of the latter is associated with genomic instability (Jiang et al. [^100]; Gertler and Cohen [^68]). The role of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] in chromatin condensation is presented in Fig. [1](#Fig1).

[[SIRT7|SIRT7]] interacts with promoter as well as transcribed regions of rDNA genes. This sirtuin deacetylates histones, in particular H2A and H2B (Ford et al. [^64]), however, its main substrate is H3K18 (Barber et al. [^9]). Deacetylation of this histone is associated with repression of tumor suppressor genes. Therefore, [[SIRT7|SIRT7]] can support cancer phenotype by inhibiting expression of tumor suppressors. However, it must be noted that [[SIRT7|SIRT7]] is required only to sustain cancer phenotype and does not promote oncogenic transformation of normal cells (Barber et al. [^9]; Kim et al. [^112]).

#### Influence on DNA damage and DNA repair

Unrepairable DNA damage is believed to be one of the basic causes of [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]] (Sedelnikova et al. [^191]). Already in yeast it was observed that Sir2 takes part in [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]]. Changes in the localization of Sir2 occur not only during senescence but also as a result of [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]]. Sir2 dissociates from HM loci and moves to the sites of DNA breaks (Oberdoerffer et al. [^164]). This has two effects: firstly, it induces expression of HM genes (involved in [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]]) and secondly, inhibits proliferation giving time for [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] to occur. Moreover, at sites of DNA breaks, Sir2 deacetylates histones and [[DNA Repair|DNA damage response]] proteins that recruit proteins responsible for [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] (Oberdoerffer et al. [^164]). The involvement of sirtuins in [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] recognition and repair has been also observed in more complex organisms.

Under normal conditions [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] is bound to hundreds of gene promoters in the mouse genome. The binding pattern is disturbed as a result of genotoxic stress as [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] moves to [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] sites where it plays an important role in the recruitment and activation of repair proteins (Chung et al. [^43]). In cells derived from [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] knockout mice, aside from chromosomal aberrations, impaired [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] was observed further proving that this sirtuin is involved in double helix repair (Wang et al. [^218]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] interacts directly with [[NBS1|NBS1]] and maintains it in a hypoacetylated state, which allows for phosphorylation of S343 that is necessary for efficient [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair response]] and activation of the S-phase checkpoint (Yuan et al. [^232]). Acetylation of [[WRN|WRN]] promotes its translocation to the nucleus while subsequent deacetylation by [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] increases its activity and efficiency of [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] by HR (Li et al. [^129]). In response to [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] [[Ku70|Ku70]] is acetylated on multiple lysine residues. This facilitates dissociation of [[Ku70|Ku70]] from BAX, which results in translocation of the latter to the [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and induction of [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]]. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deacetylates [[Ku70|Ku70]] sustaining its interaction with BAX. This, in turn, inhibits [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] and facilitates [[Ku70|Ku70]]-dependent [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] (Bosch-Presegué and Vaquero [^25]). A similar role was shown for [[SIRT3|SIRT3]]. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] is also involved in the repair of single strand [[DNA Repair|DNA breaks]] via nucleotide excision repair (NER). UV radiation (NER is the main pathway responsible for repair of UV-induced breaks) stimulates interaction of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] with xeroderma pigmentosum group A ([[XPA|XPA]])—one of the key factors in NER. [[XPA|XPA]] recognizes [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] and recruits proteins essential for the repair process. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deacetylates [[XPA|XPA]] on K63 and K67 facilitating its interaction with RPA32 (which stabilizes single-stranded [[DNA Repair|DNA]]) and [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] (Fan and Luo [^58]). Additionally, [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] overexpression in mice inhibits [[Telomere|telomere]] erosion while its silencing accelerates it (see above). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deacetylates shelterin component [[WRN|WRN]] and Tankyrase, influencing [[Telomere|telomere]] length and stability (reviewed elsewhere).

[[SIRT6|SIRT6]] also plays a considerable role in [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] and maintenance of genomic stability by integrating signals of [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] with activation of repair enzymes (Mao et al. [^139]). This sirtuin is involved in HR, non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) as well as base excision repair (BER) (Mostoslavsky et al. [^158]). [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] poly-ADP-ribosylates proteins localized in the vicinity of [[DNA Repair|DNA breaks]] promoting recruitment of repair enzymes (Gertler and Cohen [^68]). Moreover, in response to [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] dynamically binds to chromatin and induces global decrease in H3K9 acetylation. In this way it stabilizes the binding to [[DNA Repair|DNA]] of the catalytic subunit of [[DNA Repair|DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PKcs)]]—a key component of NHEJ that facilitates the access of repair enzymes to double strand breaks. In response to [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] mono-ADP-ribosylates K521 of [[PARP1|PARP-1]] increasing its activity and facilitating [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] via NHEJ and HR (Beneke [^16]). [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] increases the activity of C-terminal binding protein interacting protein ([[CtIP|CtIP]])—an enzyme responsible for excision of damaged [[DNA Repair|DNA]] fragments during HR. Under normal conditions [[CtIP|CtIP]] is acetylated, however, after [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] deacetylates it on K432, K526 and K604 promoting resection of damaged fragments (Kaidi et al. [^106]). It was shown that [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] is also indispensable for BER to occur, however neither direct interaction with any of the components involved in this pathway nor co-localization on the damage site were proven (Mostoslavsky et al. [^158]; Tennen and Chua [^204]).

The negative feedback loop between [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] and [[NAD+|NAD+]] level may also contribute to cell senescence. [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] can induce a decrease in the level of [[NAD+|NAD+]] due to increased [[PARP1|PARP1]] activity, which requires [[NAD+|NAD+]] as a co-factor. Repeated or chronic [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] can result in substantial depletion of [[NAD+|NAD+]] and decrease in sirtuin activity. This in turn, can disrupt [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] (causing increase in the number of DNA breaks) and impair [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] function. The latter may result in increased ROS production and further [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] (Imai and Guarente [^93]). Therefore, [[NAD+|NAD+]] level and sirtuin activity can provide an interface between [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]] and [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] function and combine [[DNA Repair|DNA damage theory]] with Harman's [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial theory of ageing]].

Not only direct involvement in [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] is important, as in the case of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] or [[SIRT6|SIRT6]], which can modify a variety of proteins engaged in repair of [[DNA Repair|DNA damage]]. Glucose and glutamine metabolism is also relevant in this process. Glutamine is the main nitrogen donor, not only for protein, but also nucleotide synthesis. [[SIRT4|SIRT4]] plays an important role in [[DNA Repair|DNA damage response]] by regulating mitochondrial glutamine metabolism. During [[DNA Repair|DNA damage response]] [[SIRT4|SIRT4]] inhibits the transport of intermediates to the Krebs cycle so that the nitrogen atom from glutamine can be used for the synthesis of purine nucleotides crucial in [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] process (Jeong et al. [^98]). [[SIRT4|SIRT4]] is also a negative regulator of [[Glutamate dehydrogenase|glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH)]], the first enzyme in glutamine metabolism (Haigis et al. [^75]). Lack of [[SIRT4|SIRT4]] disturbed [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] and promoted accumulation of the damage, while [[SIRT4|SIRT4]] overexpression supported the removal of γH2AX foci (Jeong et al. [^98]).

#### Influence on oxidative stress and energy metabolism

Among sirtuins the most important role in anti-oxidative defense is played by [[SIRT3|SIRT3]]. Deacetylation of mitochondrial complex I and III by [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] results in an increase in the efficiency of electron transport, which prevents ROS production (Haigis et al. [^76]). Loss of this sirtuin results in hyper-acetylation of the components of mitochondrial complex I and a decrease in its activity and in ATP level (Ahn et al. [^2]). Sirtuins can counteract [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] also by modulating antioxidant enzymes. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can influence the level of manganese superoxide dismutase ([[MnSOD|MnSOD]]) via cooperation with [[FOXO|FOXO]] transcription factors. Deacetylation of [[FOXO3a|FOXO3a]] by [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] leads to an increase in the level of [[MnSOD|MnSOD]] and [[Catalase|catalase]] (Chung et al. [^43]), while recruitment of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] to the promoter region of [[MnSOD|MnSOD]] gene along with [[FoxO1|FOXO1]] is indispensable to co-activate expression of this antioxidant enzyme (Daitoku et al. [^52]). Sirtuins modulate not only the level of antioxidant enzymes but also their activity. It was shown that deacetylation of isocitrate dehydrogenase ([[IDH2|IDH2]]) and of [[MnSOD|MnSOD]] on K122 by [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] increases activity of these enzymes (Bell et al. [^13]). Moreover, activity of [[SOD1|SOD1]] increased after desuccinylation by [[SIRT5|SIRT5]] (Lin et al. [^134]).

### Interactions with other proteins involved in senescence

#### Interaction with p53

Regulation of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and [[p53|p53]] activity is mutual and complicated. In response to stress [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deacetylates [[p53|p53]] on K320, K373 and K382 in the C-terminal regulatory domain. Deacetylation inhibits [[p53|p53]]-dependent transcription and [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]], which facilitates [[DNA Repair|DNA damage repair]] (Cheng et al. [^41]). It was shown that [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] colocalizes with [[p53|p53]] in PML nuclear bodies where it antagonizes PML-induced acetylation of [[p53|p53]] and inhibits premature senescence (Langley et al. [^122]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can also regulate localization of [[p53|p53]]. In mouse embryonic stem cells [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deacetylates [[p53|p53]] on K379 in response to [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], which prevents its translocation to the nucleus. This results in an increase in [[p53|p53]] level in the cytoplasm and in mitochondrial-dependent [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] (Han et al. [^78]). However, in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] knockout animals, the effects associated with modulation of [[p53|p53]] activity are not observed. This inconsistency can be explained by a redundant action of sirtuins—[[SIRT2|SIRT2]] and [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] can also interact with [[p53|p53]] (see below). [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] can inhibit the activity of [[p53|p53]]. It is suggested that [[SIRT3|SIRT3]] can act as a regulator in [[p53|p53]]-dependent senescence by inhibiting [[p53|p53]] ability to promote cell cycle arrest and senescence (Li et al. [^130]).

The sirtuins-[[p53|p53]] interaction is reciprocal and also [[p53|p53]] can influence the activity of these enzymes. In the promoter region of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] there are two [[p53|p53]]-binding sites (Bosch-Presegué and Vaquero [^25]; Anwar et al. [^4]). Moreover, 3′UTR fragment of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] mRNA has a [[miR-34a|miR-34a]]-responsive element. [[miR-34a|miR-34a]] is a small noncoding RNA that can inhibit expression of some proteins. Active [[p53|p53]] can induce expression of [[miR-34a|miR-34a]]. Therefore, increased activity of [[p53|p53]] increases the level of [[miR-34a|miR-34a]], which inhibits [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] translation. This interplay can in part explain the decrease in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]], which we and others observed during [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]] (see Grabowska et al. [^69] and Grabowska et al. [^70]). There is also indirect interplay between [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] and [[p53|p53]] activity. Both proteins depend on [[NAD+|NAD+]] level since sirtuins require it as a co-factor while [[NAD+|NAD+]] can bind to [[p53|p53]] tetramers and affect their conformation thus preventing DNA binding (McLure et al. [^146]). [[p53|p53]] can also positively regulate the level of [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] (Kanfi et al. [^107]).

> [!important] The p53–miR-34a–SIRT1 negative feedback loop
> Active [[p53|p53]] induces [[miR-34a|miR-34a]], which represses [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] translation; since [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] deacetylates/inhibits [[p53|p53]], this forms a loop that helps explain the fall in [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] observed during [[Cellular Senescence|cellular senescence]]. [[NF-κB|NF-κB]] can engage the same [[miR-34a|miR-34a]] arm to suppress [[SIRT1|SIRT1]].

#### Interaction with FOXO family

[[FOXO|FOXO]] transcription factors are believed to promote longevity although the precise mechanism in not yet fully understood. However, it has been shown that they act as sensors of the insulin/[[IGF-1|IGF-1]] signaling pathway, which is crucial for ageing and longevity (Martins et al. [^140]), and regulate expression of key antioxidant enzymes such as [[MnSOD|MnSOD]] and [[Catalase|catalase]] (see above). Activity of the [[FOXO|FOXO]] family can be regulated by phosphorylation and acetylation. Acetylation of these factors facilitates phosphorylation and inactivation therefore decreases their ability to bind to DNA (Matsuzaki et al. [^143]). Sirtuins can deacetylate some members of the [[FOXO|FOXO]] family such as [[FoxO1|FOXO1]], [[FOXO3a|FOXO3a]] and FOXO4 whereby inducing their activity (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). It has been shown that deacetylation of [[FOXO3a|FOXO3a]] increases expression of proteins involved not only in protection against [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] but also in [[DNA Repair|DNA repair]] and cell cycle checkpoints (Michan and Sinclair [^149]). [[SIRT2|SIRT2]] was demonstrated to be the main deacetylase of cytoplasmic [[FoxO1|FOXO1]] (Zhao et al. [^234]). On the other hand, [[FoxO1|FOXO1]] can regulate expression of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] by binding to its gene promoter region (Xiong et al. [^227]), which creates an autoregulatory feedback loop regulating [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] expression.

#### Interaction with NFκB

[[NF-κB|NFκB]] transcription factor was shown to regulate the process of ageing and it seems that its main role is to transactivate genes the products of which contribute to the senescence associated secretory phenotype ([[SASP|SASP]]). It was shown that [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] (and also [[SIRT2|SIRT2]]) can inhibit [[NF-κB|NFκB]] signaling by deacetylating p65 ([[RELA|RELA]]) on K310, which modulates its ability to bind DNA and induces transcription of proteins involved in inflammation. In consequence, [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activity leads to a decrease in inflammation (Chung et al. [^43]). [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] can also inactivate [[NF-κB|NFκB]] by direct interaction with its [[RELA|RELA]] subunit. Such effect is followed by inhibition and destabilization of [[RELA|RELA]] binding at target gene promoters (Gertler and Cohen [^68]), which can contribute to inhibition of [[Apoptosis|apoptosis]] and senescence. Moreover, [[SIRT6|SIRT6]] destabilizes binding of this transcription factor by deacetylating H3K9 in gene promoters of [[NF-κB|NFκB]] target genes (Gertler and Cohen [^68]). On the other hand, [[NF-κB|NFκB]] can decrease the activity of [[SIRT1|SIRT1]], in a similar way to [[p53|p53]], by modulating [[miR-34a|miR-34a]] expression (Kauppinen et al. [^110]).

#### Interaction with AMPK

Many studies revealed that increased [[AMPK|AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase)]] activity can extend the lifespan of some model organisms. It was also shown that [[AMPK|AMPK]] can regulate several signaling pathways involved in senescence and ageing such as those engaging [[p53|p53]], [[mTOR|mTOR]] and [[NF-κB|NFκB]] (Salminen and Kaarniranta [^182]). Moreover, [[AMPK|AMPK]] can regulate cellular energy expenditure/status through modulation of [[NAD+|NAD+]] level, which suggests that this kinase may be involved in regulation of sirtuin activity. Both [[AMPK|AMPK]] and [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] are activated as a result of [[Caloric Restriction|CR]], have similar molecular targets and biological activities (Ruderman et al. [^180]). Activation of [[AMPK|AMPK]] elevates the level of [[NAD+|NAD+]] (among others through increase in the level and activity of [[NAMPT|NAMPT]]) thereby increasing [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activity (Canto et al. [^35]). On the other hand, [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] activation increases the activity of [[AMPK|AMPK]] by [[LKB1|LKB1]] deacetylation on K48. Deacetylated [[LKB1|LKB1]] migrates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, binds to STRAD and mouse embryo scaffold protein (MO25). The latter interaction induces [[LKB1|LKB1]] kinase activity and [[AMPK|AMPK]] phosphorylation (Wang et al. [^219]). This creates a positive feedback loop. [[SIRT4|SIRT4]], on the other hand, inhibits [[AMPK|AMPK]] activity (Ho et al. [^84]).

#### Interaction with P66shc

[[SIRT1|SIRT1]] negatively regulates the expression of [[P66shc|P66shc]] (Chen et al. [^40]), one of the three isoforms of the ShcA family. This protein is involved in [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] because it stimulates mitochondrial ROS generation, and downregulates antioxidant enzyme synthesis (Miyazawa and Tsuji [^154]). It also controls the lifespan/longevity (reviewed in Kong et al. [^117]). [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] decreases both the [[P66shc|P66shc]] level and [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] intensity (Zhou et al. [^238]) because it binds to the gene promoter of [[P66shc|P66shc]] and deacetylates [[Histone H3|histone H3]], which reduces the transcription rate. [[P66shc|P66shc]] knockout mice had longer lifespan and enhanced resistance to [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] and age-related pathologies (Berry et al. [^18]; Vikram et al. [^216]; Kumar et al. [^121]; Ma et al. [^138]). Moreover, [[P66shc|P66shc]] inhibits the activity of [[FOXO3a|FOXO3a]] transcription factor (Miyazawa and Tsuji [^154]). Ageing-initiated [[P66shc|P66shc]]-mediated endothelial dysfunction was shown both in clinical trials and animal experiments, however, not in [[P66shc|P66shc]] knockout mice (Francia et al. [^65]). This suggested that [[P66shc|P66shc]] knockout mice were protected from endothelial dysfunctions. Moreover, such mice had 30% longer lifespan than control ones. [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] seems to be involved in this protection (Berry et al. [^18]). It has been also observed that [[Caloric Restriction|CR]], which evokes an increase in sirtuin activity, could reduce [[P66shc|P66shc]] level (Zhou et al. [^238]).

> [!tip] SIRT1 → P66shc axis
> By repressing [[P66shc|P66shc]] transcription (via [[Histone H3|H3]] deacetylation at its promoter), [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] lowers mitochondrial ROS and is a plausible contributor to the 30% lifespan extension seen in [[P66shc|P66shc]] knockout mice—an attractive downstream node for CR-mimetic strategies.

### Doubts

Despite plethora of research documenting beneficial influence of sirtuins on ageing and longevity there are also some conflicting data. Some authors completely exclude sirtuin involvement in CR-induced lifespan extension (as it was shown that CR can extend lifespan of Sir2-deficient yeast) (Tsuchiya et al. [^208]). Others state that the fact that Sir2 overexpression combined with CR resulted in greater lifespan extension than each intervention alone suggests that sirtuins do not mediate the positive effect of CR (Kaeberlein et al. [^105]). There are studies implying that increased longevity of some model organisms (such as *D. melanogaster* and *C. elegans*) after sirtuin overexpression is due to a lack of genetic background standardization and incorrectly matched controls (Burnett et al. [^33]). Some data also show that [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] can promote replicative senescence. Mouse embryonic fibroblasts lacking [[SIRT1|SIRT1]] are characterized by increased replicative potential under conditions of chronic sublethal stress (Chua et al. [^42]).
