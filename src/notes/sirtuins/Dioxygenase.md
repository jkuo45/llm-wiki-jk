# Dioxygenase

## Overview

Dioxygenases are enzymes that catalyze the incorporation of both atoms of molecular oxygen (O₂) into a substrate. Among the most therapeutically relevant is indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1), a heme-dependent dioxygenase (EC 1.13.11.52) that catalyzes the first, rate-limiting step in the catabolism of [[Tryptophan]] along the [[Kynurenine Pathway]].

## Structure & Domains

IDO1 belongs to the family of heme-dependent dioxygenases, distinct from but functionally related to tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase ([[TDO]], mainly hepatic) and IDO2, a less active paralog. IDO1 is a monomeric cytosolic protein of about 45 kDa encoded by the IDO1 gene on human chromosome 8p11.21. Crystal structures (e.g., PDB 2D0T, 5EK2) reveal a large domain with a deep heme-binding pocket and a smaller domain, with an active site that shows plasticity allowing both inhibitor and substrate binding.

## Mechanism

The reaction inserts both oxygen atoms derived from O₂ into tryptophan, opening the indole ring to yield N-formylkynurenine, which is further metabolized to [[Quinolinic Acid]] and ultimately to [[NAD+]]. By depleting tryptophan and generating downstream metabolites, IDO1 regulates T-cell immunity and contributes to immune tolerance in the tumor microenvironment.

> [!info] Structural insight
> The plasticity of the IDO1 active site revealed by X-ray structures underpins the design of competitive IDO1 inhibitors, a strategy pursued in immuno-oncology to reverse tumor-induced tryptophan depletion.

## Clinical Relevance

IDO1 is overexpressed in many tumors and is associated with immunosuppression. IDO1 inhibitors (e.g., epacadostat) were developed to reactivate antitumor immunity, though early clinical trials had mixed results, underscoring the complexity of the kynurenine pathway in cancer biology.
