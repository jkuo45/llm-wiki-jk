# Deacetylases

## Overview

Deacetylases are enzymes that remove acetyl groups from lysine residues on histones and other proteins, opposing the action of [[Histone Acetyltransferase|acetyltransferases]] and thereby shaping the cellular [[Acetylation]] landscape. Within this broad class, the [[Sirtuins]] are the NAD+-dependent deacetylases: they couple the hydrolysis of [[NAD+]] to the removal of acetyl groups, making their activity directly responsive to the metabolic and energy state of the cell.

## Mechanism

Classical deacetylases include the zinc-dependent [[HDAC1|HDAC]] family, whereas sirtuins (SIRT1–SIRT7 in mammals) use NAD+ as a cofactor. In the sirtuin reaction, NAD+ is cleaved to generate nicotinamide and the ADP-ribose donor that accepts the acetyl group, producing O-acetyl-ADP-ribose and deacetylated substrate. Because NAD+ is consumed, sirtuin deacetylase activity rises when NAD+ levels are high (e.g., during fasting or [[Caloric Restriction]]) and falls when NAD+ is depleted.

## Physiological Function

Deacetylation regulates [[Chromatin]] structure and [[Histone H3]]/[[Histone H4]] modifications, transcription factor activity, DNA-repair enzymes, and metabolic enzymes. Sirtuin-mediated deacetylation has been strongly implicated in the control of [[Aging]] and [[Longevity]], and sirtuin activation has emerged as a candidate anti-aging intervention. The balance between acetylation and deacetylation determines gene expression programs in metabolism, inflammation, and stress resistance (e.g., via [[FOXO]] transcription factors and [[NF-κB]]).

> [!important] NAD+ dependence
> Unlike zinc-dependent HDACs, sirtuins directly consume NAD+. This links deacetylase activity to energetic status, NAD+ precursor availability ([[NMN]], [[Nicotinamide Riboside]]), and interventions that modulate NAD+ levels.

## Clinical Relevance

Pharmacologically, sirtuin deacetylation can be modulated by [[Sirtuin Activators]] (e.g., resveratrol-like STACs) and [[Sirtuin Inhibitors]] (e.g., [[Tenovins|tenovins]], EX-527), with implications for metabolic disease, neurodegeneration, and cancer.
