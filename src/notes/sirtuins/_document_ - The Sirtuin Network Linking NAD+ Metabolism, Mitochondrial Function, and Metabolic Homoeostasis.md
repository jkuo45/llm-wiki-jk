## ABSTRACT

Metabolic disorders such as [[Obesity]], type 2 diabetes mellitus, and fatty liver disease are associated with a disruption in the coordinated regulation of nutrient sensing, energy metabolism, and cellular homoeostasis. Increasing evidence indicates that [[Sirtuins]], a family of [[NAD+]] ‐dependent enzymes, play a central role in integrating metabolic and stress‐responsive pathways. By coupling the cellular redox state to transcriptional and post‐translational regulation, [[Sirtuins]]s coordinate key processes such as glucose and [[Lipid Metabolism]], [[Mitochondria]]l function, and inflammatory signalling. [[Sirtuins]] are functionally specialised according to their subcellular localisation. Nuclear sirtuins ([[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], and [[SIRT7]]) regulate transcriptional programs controlling metabolism and [[Inflammation]], whereas mitochondrial sirtuins ([[SIRT3]], [[SIRT4]], and [[SIRT5]]) directly modulate metabolic enzyme activity and redox homoeostasis. [[SIRT2]] links cytosolic metabolic signalling to cytoskeletal dynamics and cell cycle regulation. Importantly, the activity of all sirtuins is tightly dependent on NAD <sup>+</sup> availability, positioning NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism as a central regulator of the sirtuin network. In this review, we provide a systems‐level perspective on sirtuin biology, highlighting how NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism, subcellular compartmentalisation, and specific functions converge to form an integrated regulatory network governing metabolic homoeostasis. We further discuss how disruption of this network contributes to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders through impaired metabolic flexibility, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation. Overall, this network‐based framework provides a unified view of sirtuin function in metabolic regulation and helps to explain the complexity and tissue‐specific heterogeneity of metabolic diseases.

**Keywords:** metabolic disorders, metabolic homoeostasis, mitochondrial function, NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism, sirtuins

## 1\. Introduction

Metabolic diseases comprise a heterogeneous spectrum of conditions defined by altered control of energy homoeostasis, nutrient handling, and cellular regulatory processes \[[^1]\]. Their global prevalence has risen dramatically over recent decades, driven by complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, lifestyle changes, and ageing \[[^2]\]. Major conditions such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and [[Metabolic Syndrome]] not only increase the risk of cardiovascular disease but also contribute to chronic inflammation, organ dysfunction, and reduced life expectancy \[[^3], [^4], [^5]\]. Although clinical management has improved substantially, most current therapies primarily address downstream effects rather than the molecular mechanisms driving metabolic dysregulation \[[^6], [^7]\]. At the cellular level, metabolic homoeostasis is maintained by tightly coordinated signalling networks that integrate nutrient availability, hormonal inputs, and stress responses to preserve physiological function \[[^8]\]. Disruption of these networks leads to maladaptive metabolic remodelling, altered gene expression, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which collectively drive disease progression \[[^9]\]. In this context, increasing attention has been directed towards molecular systems capable of linking cellular energy status to transcriptional control, protein modification, and metabolic adaptation. Among these, sirtuins have emerged as key regulators at the crossroads of metabolism, epigenetics, and mitochondrial function. As [[Nicotinamide]] adenine dinucleotide (NAD <sup>+</sup>)‐dependent enzymes, sirtuins are uniquely positioned to detect fluctuations in cellular redox and energy states and translate them into coordinated transcriptional and post‐translational responses. Over the past 2 decades, a large body of evidence has implicated sirtuins in the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, mitochondrial activity, inflammation, and stress resistance. However, despite extensive investigation of individual sirtuins, their functions are often studied in isolation, and a comprehensive understanding of how these enzymes operate as an integrated regulatory network remains incomplete. Here, we propose a cohesive framework to better define the contribution of sirtuin biology to the regulation of metabolic processes. Specifically, we examine how NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism, subcellular compartmentalisation, and form‐specific activities converge to form a coordinated sirtuin network that governs metabolic homoeostasis. We further discuss how dysregulation of this network contributes to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders, including obesity, insulin resistance, fatty liver disease, and cardiometabolic complications. By adopting a systems‐level perspective, this review seeks to clarify the interconnected roles of sirtuins and to highlight potential opportunities for therapeutic intervention targeting NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism and sirtuin signalling.

## 2\. Sirtuins

Sirtuins are a conserved family of NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐dependent enzymes that function as central regulators of cellular metabolism, stress responses, and genome stability \[[^10]\]. Originally identified as Silent Information Regulator 2 ([[Sir2 (yeast)|Sir2]]) homologues, mammalian sirtuins (SIRT1–SIRT7) are classified as class III histone deacetylases, although their enzymatic repertoire extends beyond deacetylation to include diverse acyl‐removing activities such as desuccinylation, demalonylation, and ADP‐ribosylation \[[^11]\]. Through these reactions, sirtuins couple cellular energy status to post‐translational modification of histone and non‐histone proteins, thereby linking metabolism to transcriptional and enzymatic regulation.

A defining feature of sirtuin biology is their compartmentalised distribution, which underlies their functional specialisation. Nuclear sirtuins, including SIRT1, SIRT6, and SIRT7, primarily regulate chromatin structure and transcriptional programs controlling metabolism, inflammation, and stress responses. In contrast, mitochondrial sirtuins (SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5) directly modulate metabolic enzyme activity and mitochondrial homoeostasis, while SIRT2, predominantly cytosolic, connects metabolic signalling with cytoskeletal dynamics and cell cycle regulation. This spatial organisation enables sirtuins to coordinate metabolic processes across cellular compartments rather than acting as isolated regulators \[[^12], [^13], [^14], [^15], [^16], [^17]\].

Within the nuclear sirtuin family, SIRT1 is the best‐characterised member and acts as a major integrator of metabolic regulation. By deacetylating transcription factors and coactivators such as PGC‐1α, [[FOXO]] proteins, and NF‐κB, SIRT1 promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, enhances [[Fatty acid oxidation]], and suppresses inflammatory signalling. These actions position SIRT1 as a central mediator of metabolic adaptation to nutrient availability and stress \[[^18], [^19], [^20]\]. SIRT6 complements these functions at the chromatin level by repressing glycolytic gene expression through inhibition of HIF‐1α and by maintaining genomic stability via histone deacetylation \[[^21], [^22], [^23]\]. In contrast, SIRT7 exerts more context‐dependent effects, including regulation of ribosomal biogenesis, mitochondrial gene expression, and lipid metabolism, and in several metabolic settings has been associated with anabolic and lipid‐accumulating programs \[[^24], [^25], [^26], [^27]\].

Mitochondrial sirtuins directly regulate metabolic flux through post‐translational modification of key enzymes. SIRT3, the principal mitochondrial deacetylase, enhances oxidative metabolism by activating enzymes involved in fatty acid β‐oxidation, the [[TCA cycle|TCA cycle]], and the [[Electron Transport Chain]], while also promoting antioxidant defences through activation of [[SOD2]] and related pathways \[[^28], [^29], [^30], [^31]\]. SIRT4 generally counterbalances catabolic metabolism by inhibiting fatty acid oxidation and modulating amino acid‐driven [[Insulin Secretion]], thereby contributing to energy conservation under nutrient‐rich conditions. SIRT5 regulates mitochondrial metabolism through removal of negatively charged acyl modifications, particularly succinylation, malonylation, and glutarylation, thereby influencing multiple pathways including fatty acid oxidation, ketogenesis, and ammonia detoxification \[[^32], [^33]\]. SIRT2 occupies a distinct position within the sirtuin family, linking metabolic regulation to cytoskeletal organisation and cell division. Through deacetylation of substrates such as α‐tubulin and [[FoxO1]], SIRT2 modulates microtubule dynamics, cell cycle progression, and adipocyte differentiation, highlighting its context‐dependent role at the interface of metabolism and cellular architecture \[[^34], [^35], [^36], [^37]\].

Taken together, these findings indicate that sirtuins function as an integrated and compartmentalised regulatory network, as depicted in Figure [1](#dmrr70201-fig-0001). Rather than functioning as independent enzymes, individual sirtuin isoforms contribute to an integrated system that links NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism, transcriptional regulation, and mitochondrial function to maintain cellular and organismal metabolic homoeostasis.

## 3\. NAD+ Metabolism as a Central Regulator of Sirtuin Activity

NAD <sup>+</sup> is an essential metabolic cofactor whose intracellular availability is tightly regulated through multiple biosynthetic routes, reflecting its central importance for cellular homoeostasis and for the activity of NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐dependent enzymes such as sirtuins \[[^38]\]. NAD <sup>+</sup> can be generated from the four principal precursors tryptophan, [[Niacin]], nicotinamide, and [[Nicotinamide Riboside]], each entering distinct but convergent metabolic pathways that ultimately yield the same dinucleotide (Figure [2](#dmrr70201-fig-0002)) \[[^39]\]. NAD <sup>+</sup> can be synthesised through several pathways, among which the [[Preiss-Handler Pathway]] relies on nicotinic acid as the primary precursor. Within this pathway, nicotinic acid is first converted into nicotinic acid mononucleotide by nicotinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase, after which [[Nicotinamide Mononucleotide]] adenylyltransferases catalyse its conversion to nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide. The final amidation step, mediated by NAD synthase, produces NAD <sup>+</sup>. An alternative route for de novo biosynthesis originates from tryptophan via the kynurenine pathway. Through a series of enzymatic reactions, tryptophan is metabolised to quinolinic acid, which is then converted into nicotinic acid mononucleotide by quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase and funnelled into the downstream steps of the [[Preiss-Handler Pathway|Preiss–Handler]] pathway \[[^40]\]. In addition to these pathways, NAD <sup>+</sup> is efficiently regenerated through salvage mechanisms that recycle nicotinamide, a byproduct of NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐consuming reactions, including those catalysed by sirtuins. In the [[NAD+ Salvage Pathway]], nicotinamide is recycled into nicotinamide mononucleotide through the action of nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase ([[NAMPT]]), which represents a rate‐limiting and energetically costly step; this intermediate is then converted into NAD <sup>+</sup> by nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases \[[^41]\]. Nicotinamide riboside represents an additional salvage precursor that is phosphorylated by nicotinamide riboside kinases to generate nicotinamide mononucleotide, thereby bypassing the NAMPT‐dependent step and contributing efficiently to intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> pools \[[^42]\].

![FIGURE 2](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/cae3/13364290/6ad3a64bf5e1/DMRR-42-e70201-g001.jpg)

NAD + metabolism as a central regulator of sirtuin activity and cellular energy homoeostasis. NAD is produced through de novo, Preiss–Handler, and [[NAD+ Salvage Pathway|salvage pathways]] that collectively maintain intracellular NAD pools. Beyond its role as a redox cofactor in central metabolic pathways, NAD serves as an essential substrate for sirtuins, directly linking its availability to their enzymatic activity. Changes in NAD levels across cellular compartments dynamically regulate the sirtuin network, thereby influencing mitochondrial function, metabolic pathways, and stress responses.

Sirtuin enzymes require NAD <sup>+</sup> as an essential co‐substrate for their catalytic activity. During the deacetylation reaction, NAD <sup>+</sup> is consumed and cleaved, linking sirtuin activity directly to intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> availability. Consequently, enzymes that regulate cellular NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism can indirectly modulate sirtuins function \[[^43]\]. An important regulator of NAD <sup>+</sup> homoeostasis is nicotinamide N‐methyltransferase ([[NNMT]]), which catalyses the methylation of nicotinamide to generate 1‐methylnicotinamide, effectively diverting it from the salvage pathway \[[^44], [^45], [^46]\]. Consistent with this central metabolic role, dysregulated NNMT expression has been implicated in several pathological conditions, including metabolic disorders such as obesity and metabolic syndrome, as well as in numerous cancers, where it contributes to metabolic reprogramming \[[^47], [^48], [^49], [^50]\]. By influencing the pool of NAD <sup>+</sup> precursors and overall NAD <sup>+</sup> homoeostasis, NNMT can indirectly affect the activity of NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐dependent enzymes such as sirtuins \[[^51]\].

Beyond its role as a substrate for sirtuins, NAD <sup>+</sup> is indispensable for cellular energy metabolism. It serves as an essential redox cofactor in [[Glycolysis]], where its reduction to NADH is required for the conversion of glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate to 1,3‐bisphosphoglycerate, and in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, where multiple dehydrogenase reactions generate mitochondrial NADH. The NADH produced in these pathways is subsequently oxidised by the mitochondrial electron transport chain, linking NAD <sup>+</sup> /NADH homoeostasis to [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]] \[[^38]\]. Consequently, fluctuations in NAD <sup>+</sup> availability directly influence cytosolic, nuclear, and mitochondrial redox states. Since sirtuin activity depends directly on intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> levels, these enzymes act as metabolic sensors that connect cellular energy status with transcriptional and metabolic regulation. Thus, NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism does not merely sustain sirtuin activity but actively shapes the functional state of the sirtuin network across cellular compartments.

## 4\. Sirtuins as Key Regulators of Metabolic Homoeostasis

### 4.1. Regulation of Glucose Metabolism by Sirtuins

Maintenance of glucose homoeostasis requires the coordinated regulation of multiple metabolic processes, including glycolysis, [[Gluconeogenesis]], insulin secretion, and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Sirtuins have been identified as central regulators of these pathways, acting as metabolic sensors that link cellular energy status to transcriptional and enzymatic regulation. Because their catalytic activity depends on NAD <sup>+</sup>, fluctuations in intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> levels during fasting, [[Caloric Restriction]], or metabolic stress directly influence sirtuin activity and thereby modulate glucose metabolism \[[^52]\].

Among the members of the sirtuin family, SIRT1 has been widely investigated and is recognised as a key regulator of glucose metabolic processes. SIRT1 modulates metabolic homoeostasis through the deacetylation of several transcription factors and metabolic regulators involved in glucose metabolism. In particular, SIRT1 regulates the activity of forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factors and the transcriptional coactivator peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ coactivator‐1α (PGC‐1α), thereby promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis and metabolic adaptation during nutrient deprivation \[[^53]\]. Through these mechanisms, SIRT1 regulates hepatic glucose production and supports systemic metabolic control across multiple tissues, including skeletal muscle, [[Adipose Tissue]], and pancreatic β‐cells \[[^54], [^55]\]. Furthermore, SIRT1 interacts with major metabolic signalling pathways including AMP‐activated protein kinase ([[AMPK]]), highlighting its central role in linking nutrient sensing to metabolic regulation \[[^56]\].

Beyond SIRT1, other nuclear sirtuins are also involved in the transcriptional regulation of glucose metabolism, with SIRT6 playing a key role in controlling glycolytic gene expression. SIRT6 exerts an inhibitory effect on glycolysis by attenuating the transcriptional activity of hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α), a central regulator of glycolytic gene networks. Through histone deacetylation at HIF‐1α target gene promoters, SIRT6 reduces the expression of glycolytic enzymes and limits glycolytic flux \[[^57]\]. Consistent with this function, SIRT6 deficiency leads to increased glycolysis and metabolic imbalance, indicating that SIRT6 functions as an important negative regulator of glycolytic metabolism and glucose homoeostasis \[[^58]\].

Recent evidence also indicates that SIRT7 participates in metabolic regulation. Although it has historically been less studied than other sirtuins, SIRT7 has been shown to regulate genes involved in glucose metabolism and energy homoeostasis in metabolically active tissues such as liver and adipose tissue \[[^59]\]. Through interactions with transcriptional regulators and chromatin‐associated proteins, SIRT7 contributes to the fine regulation of metabolic gene networks and may cooperate with other nuclear sirtuins, particularly SIRT1 and SIRT6, to maintain metabolic homoeostasis \[[^60]\].

Beyond nuclear sirtuins, mitochondrial sirtuins also contribute to the regulation of glucose metabolism by modulating mitochondrial enzyme activity and oxidative processes. SIRT3, the primary mitochondrial deacetylase, regulates the acetylation status of several enzymes involved in energy metabolism and mitochondrial respiration. Through the deacetylation of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, SIRT3 promotes oxidative metabolism and enhances mitochondrial function, supporting efficient utilisation of glucose‐derived substrates \[[^28], [^61]\].

Other mitochondrial sirtuins contribute to glucose‐related metabolic processes through additional mechanisms. SIRT4, for example, regulates mitochondrial metabolic enzymes and plays a role in insulin secretion and energy metabolism \[[^62], [^63]\]. Similarly, SIRT5 regulates metabolic enzyme activity through its desuccinylase and demalonylase activities, influencing metabolic pathways including glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle \[[^16]\].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that sirtuins regulate glucose metabolism at multiple levels, including transcriptional regulation of metabolic genes, modulation of signalling pathways, and post‐translational modification of metabolic enzymes. Through the coordinated actions of nuclear and mitochondrial sirtuins, these enzymes integrate nutrient sensing with cellular metabolic adaptation, thereby contributing to the maintenance of glucose homoeostasis under both physiological and metabolic stress conditions.

### 4.2. Lipid Metabolism

The regulation of lipid metabolism relies on a highly coordinated set of processes that balance fatty acid synthesis, lipid storage, and oxidation in accordance with cellular energy demands. Sirtuins contribute significantly to these processes by regulating transcription factors, metabolic enzymes, and mitochondrial activity, linking cellular energy balance to lipid metabolism. Because their enzymatic activity depends on intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> availability, sirtuins function as metabolic sensors that adapt lipid metabolism to nutrient availability and metabolic stress \[[^20], [^64]\].

Within the sirtuin family, SIRT1 plays a major role in lipid metabolism by regulating lipid homoeostasis, largely through deacetylation of transcriptional regulators involved in fatty acid oxidation and lipid synthesis. In hepatic tissue, SIRT1 enhances the activity of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor alpha ([[PPARα]]) and its coactivator PGC‐1α, thereby promoting the expression of genes involved in mitochondrial fatty acid β‐oxidation \[[^65], [^66]\]. At the same time, SIRT1 suppresses lipogenesis by inhibiting the activity of sterol regulatory element‐binding protein‐1c (SREBP‐1c), a key transcription factor responsible for the expression of enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis \[[^67]\]. Through these coordinated mechanisms, SIRT1 enhances lipid mobilisation and fatty acid oxidation during fasting, while restraining lipid accumulation under metabolic stress conditions \[[^68]\].

In adipose tissue, SIRT1 also regulates adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage. By repressing the activity of the nuclear receptor PPARγ, a master regulator of adipogenesis, SIRT1 inhibits adipocyte maturation and reduces lipid accumulation in [[Adipose Tissue|white adipose tissue]] \[[^69]\]. This mechanism contributes to the regulation of adipose tissue expansion and metabolic flexibility during changes in energy availability.

Mitochondrial sirtuins contribute to lipid metabolism by regulating mitochondrial enzymes that control fatty acid oxidation. Among them, SIRT3 acts as the main mitochondrial deacetylase, promoting β‐oxidation and oxidative metabolism through the deacetylation of key metabolic proteins. Through these actions, SIRT3 enhances fatty acid oxidation and supports mitochondrial energy production, particularly in metabolically active tissues such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and heart \[[^70], [^71], [^72]\].

In contrast, SIRT4 exerts distinct regulatory functions in lipid metabolism. SIRT4 has been demonstrated to modulate mitochondrial metabolic enzymes and can inhibit fatty acid oxidation through the regulation of malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase activity, thereby influencing lipid storage and energy balance \[[^73]\]. In addition, SIRT4 participates in the control of insulin secretion and metabolic homoeostasis, further linking mitochondrial metabolism with systemic lipid and glucose regulation \[[^62]\].

SIRT5 is another mitochondrial sirtuin implicated in lipid metabolism, regulating metabolic enzyme activity through lysine desuccinylation, demalonylation, and deglutarylation. These post‐translational modifications affect key pathways such as fatty acid oxidation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial respiration, thereby supporting lipid metabolic homoeostasis \[[^74], [^75]\].

Nuclear sirtuins also participate in lipid metabolic regulation. SIRT6 has been shown to influence lipid metabolism by regulating gene expression involved in fatty acid metabolism and lipid homoeostasis \[[^58]\]. Similarly, SIRT7 has recently been implicated in the control of lipid metabolism by modulating transcription factors and metabolic pathways that regulate adipogenesis and lipogenesis in adipose tissue and liver \[[^76]\].

Altogether, current data support a model in which nuclear and mitochondrial sirtuins act in concert to regulate lipid metabolism. Through transcriptional control of metabolic genes, post‐translational modification of metabolic enzymes, and modulation of mitochondrial function, sirtuins integrate cellular energy sensing with lipid metabolic adaptation. This multilayered regulatory network enables cells to adjust lipid metabolism in response to physiological conditions including caloric restriction, fasting, and metabolic stress.

### 4.3. Adipose Tissue Biology

Adipose tissue biology encompasses not only triglyceride storage in white adipose tissue (WAT) but also adipocyte differentiation, lipolysis, inflammatory remodelling, and adaptive [[Thermogenesis]] in brown and beige adipose depots. Within this framework, sirtuins have emerged as multilayer regulators that couple nutrient availability and redox state to adipose tissue plasticity. SIRT1 is the best‐characterised anti‐adipogenic sirtuin in WAT, where it represses PPARγ‐dependent transcription by promoting the recruitment of the corepressors NCoR and SMRT to PPARγ target promoters, thereby limiting terminal adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage. In mature adipocytes, SIRT1 also facilitates fasting‐induced lipid mobilisation, and reduced SIRT1 activity is associated with impaired fatty acid release and greater fat accumulation \[[^77], [^78], [^79]\]. Beyond WAT, SIRT1 positively regulates [[Brown Adipose Tissue]] (BAT) activity and beige adipocyte induction; mechanistically, deacetylation of PPARγ at K268/K293 promotes cofactor exchange within the PPARγ transcriptional complex and enhances WAT browning, whereas pharmacological or transgenic SIRT1 activation increases thermogenesis, mitochondrial biogenesis, and PGC‐1α‐dependent oxidative programs in BAT \[[^69], [^80], [^81]\]. SIRT2 generally acts in parallel with SIRT1 as a brake on adipogenesis: it suppresses adipocyte differentiation through deacetylation of FOXO1, which reinforces FOXO1‐mediated repression of PPARγ, and it can also promote lipolysis in differentiated adipocytes; notably, reduced SIRT1/SIRT2 expression in human visceral adipose stem cells from obese subjects correlates with enhanced adipogenic capacity and visceral fat expansion \[[^82], [^83], [^84]\]. SIRT3, the major mitochondrial deacetylase, is especially relevant to BAT, where it supports mitochondrial oxidative capacity and adaptive thermogenesis; early work established that SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial function and thermogenic competence in brown adipocytes, and more recent studies suggest that SIRT3 controls BAT thermogenesis largely through mitochondrial pathways upstream of [[UCP1]] rather than by directly modulating UCP1 itself \[[^85], [^86]\]. SIRT4, in contrast, tends to favour an energy‐conserving adipose phenotype: by inhibiting malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase, it restrains fatty acid oxidation, and through transcriptional control involving C/EBPβ, E2F1, and HOXA5, as well as metabolic regulation of branched‐chain amino acid handling, it contributes to adipocyte differentiation and adipose remodelling; SIRT4 has also been linked to adipose inflammation and systemic metabolic dysfunction in obesity \[[^73], [^87]\]. SIRT5 has emerged as another important mitochondrial regulator of adipose plasticity, particularly in thermogenic fat: reversible succinylation controls UCP1‐linked mitochondrial metabolism in BAT, while SIRT5 itself promotes brown adipocyte differentiation and subcutaneous WAT browning, at least in part by sustaining intracellular α‐ketoglutarate and preventing repressive H3K9 methylation at the PPARγ and PRDM16 loci \[[^88]\]. However, not all studies are fully concordant with the pro‐thermogenic role of SIRT5. Pharmacological inhibition of SIRT5 has been reported to induce a brown fat–like phenotype in 3T3‐L1 preadipocytes, a model primarily suited for white adipocyte differentiation rather than physiological browning, suggesting that model‐specific factors may contribute to the apparent discrepancies in the literature \[[^89]\]. In addition, the timing and duration of pharmacological SIRT5 inhibition in adipose cell cultures may differentially modulate adipogenic differentiation pathways. Among nuclear sirtuins, SIRT6 shows context‐dependent but overall metabolically beneficial actions in adipose tissue. It has been reported to support early adipogenesis by repressing KIF5C and thereby enabling mitotic clonal expansion, although other studies indicate that SIRT6 can inhibit preadipocyte differentiation through AMPK activation; importantly, in mature adipocytes SIRT6 promotes lipolysis by deacetylating FOXO1 and sustaining Atgl transcription, enhances BAT thermogenesis and beige adipocyte recruitment through ATF2‐driven Ppargc1a expression and FOXO1‐dependent lipid catabolism, and suppresses adipose inflammation via repression of NF‐κB target genes \[[^90], [^91], [^92], [^93], [^94], [^95], [^96], [^97]\]. Finally, SIRT7 appears to oppose several of the anti‐obesity functions of SIRT1/SIRT6. In WAT, SIRT7 promotes adipogenesis by inhibiting SIRT1 autodeacetylation and thereby relieving SIRT1‐mediated repression of PPARγ, and it also stimulates lipogenesis through deacetylation of PPARγ2 at K382, increasing the expression of lipogenic genes such as Fasn, Acaca, Scd1, and Srebp1c \[[^76], [^98]\]. In thermogenic fat, SIRT7 suppresses BAT activity and beige induction by deacetylating IMP2/IGF2BP2, which strengthens translational repression of Ucp1; accordingly, SIRT7 deficiency increases UCP1 levels, energy expenditure, BAT thermogenesis, and browning of inguinal WAT \[[^99]\]. Moreover, SIRT7 also appears to amplify adipose inflammatory responses, in contrast to the anti‐inflammatory roles generally attributed to SIRT1 and SIRT6 \[[^100], [^101], [^102]\]. Taken together, these findings indicate that adipose tissue biology is governed by a complex division of labour among sirtuins, in which SIRT1, SIRT3, SIRT5, and SIRT6 mainly support lipolysis, mitochondrial fitness, browning, and metabolic flexibility, whereas SIRT4 and especially SIRT7 are more frequently associated with lipid storage, reduced fatty acid oxidation, and obesogenic remodelling, with SIRT2 acting predominantly as an inhibitor of adipogenesis.

## 4.4. Mitochondrial Metabolism
Mitochondria represent central nodes in cellular metabolism, integrating nutrient signals with ATP generation, redox balance, biosynthetic activity, and stress‐adaptive responses. Accordingly, mitochondrial metabolism depends on the coordinated regulation of substrate oxidation, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), ketogenesis, and [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] detoxification. Sirtuins are deeply involved in this network because their catalytic activity is directly linked to NAD <sup>+</sup> availability, allowing them to function as metabolic regulators that adapt mitochondrial function to energetic stress, fasting, and nutrient excess \[[^53], [^103], [^104], [^105]\]. Although the mitochondrial sirtuins SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 are the most direct regulators of mitochondrial metabolism, nuclear sirtuins such as SIRT1, SIRT6, and SIRT7 also influence mitochondrial homoeostasis by controlling mitochondrial biogenesis, transcriptional programs, and fuel selection.

Among all family members, SIRT3 is widely regarded as the principal mitochondrial deacetylase and a major regulator of mitochondrial metabolic efficiency. SIRT3 deacetylates a broad set of mitochondrial enzymes involved in fatty acid β‐oxidation, the TCA cycle, ketogenesis, and respiratory chain activity, thereby promoting oxidative metabolism and preserving metabolic flexibility \[[^106], [^107], [^108], [^109]\]. In particular, fasting‐induced SIRT3 activation enhances fatty acid oxidation through deacetylation of long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD), while also supporting ketone body production via activation of 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2) \[[^28], [^106]\]. SIRT3 also contributes to the coupling of glycolysis‐derived carbon to mitochondrial oxidation by maintaining [[Pyruvate Dehydrogenase]] ([[Pyruvate Dehydrogenase|PDH]]) activity; loss of SIRT3 leads to PDH hyperacetylation, reduced glucose oxidation, pyruvate/lactate accumulation, and impaired metabolic flexibility in skeletal muscle \[[^61]\]. Beyond substrate oxidation, SIRT3 is crucial for mitochondrial redox homoeostasis. It activates antioxidant defences through the deacetylation of SOD2 and [[FOXO3a]]‐dependent pathways, thereby limiting ROS accumulation, preserving mitochondrial DNA integrity, and protecting OXPHOS function under metabolic stress \[[^110], [^111], [^112]\]. Overall, SIRT3 coordinates mitochondrial substrate use, respiratory competence, and oxidative stress resistance.

In contrast to SIRT3, SIRT4 generally exerts an inhibitory effect on catabolic mitochondrial metabolism, promoting the preservation of energy substrates. Originally described as a mitochondrial ADP‐ribosyltransferase, SIRT4 suppresses glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) activity, thereby reducing amino acid–induced insulin secretion in pancreatic β‐cells \[[^62], [^113]\]. Subsequent studies expanded its role, showing that SIRT4 suppresses fatty acid oxidation and modulates mitochondrial gene expression in liver and muscle \[[^114]\]. In hepatocytes, SIRT4 represses PPARα signalling and inhibits malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase, shifting metabolism away from mitochondrial fatty acid utilisation and toward anabolic lipid conservation \[[^73], [^115]\]. Thus, whereas SIRT3 typically promotes mitochondrial oxidative output during fasting, SIRT4 frequently counterbalances this response by damping mitochondrial substrate oxidation. This functional opposition between SIRT3 and SIRT4 is particularly relevant in tissues that continuously adapt between fed and fasting states.

SIRT5 adds an additional layer of control over mitochondrial metabolism through distinct enzymatic functions. Rather than acting mainly as a deacetylase, SIRT5 preferentially removes negatively charged acyl modifications, especially succinyl, malonyl, and glutaryl groups, from mitochondrial metabolic enzymes \[[^116]\]. This is highly relevant because succinylation and related acylations are abundant in the mitochondrial matrix and can strongly alter enzyme activity. SIRT5 regulates multiple pathways, including fatty acid oxidation, glycolysis, the TCA cycle, ketogenesis, the electron transport chain, and ammonia detoxification \[[^104], [^117], [^118], [^119], [^120]\]. In fatty acid oxidation, SIRT5 activates enzymes such as very‐long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (VLCAD), long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD), medium‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD), and the enoyl‐CoA hydratase α‐subunit (ECHA) through desuccinylation. Deficiency of SIRT5 compromises fatty acid metabolism, leading to acylcarnitine accumulation and decreased ATP production, particularly under metabolic challenges such as fasting or exercise \[[^118], [^121], [^122], [^123]\]. SIRT5 also activates 3‐Hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2), thereby linking mitochondrial acyl‐remodelling to ketone body synthesis \[[^17]\]. In addition, it modulates components of the TCA cycle and the respiratory chain, indicating that SIRT5 is not simply a fine‐tuner of one pathway, but rather a broad regulator of mitochondrial carbon flux and respiration \[[^117]\]. Together, SIRT3 and SIRT5 cooperate to maintain efficient mitochondrial oxidation, although through distinct post‐translational mechanisms: deacetylation in the case of SIRT3, and desuccinylation/demalonylation in the case of SIRT5.

Although SIRT1 is not a mitochondrial sirtuin, it exerts major upstream control over mitochondrial metabolism. Through deacetylation of PGC‐1α, SIRT1 induces transcriptional programs that enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative metabolism, and fatty acid oxidation in the liver and skeletal muscle \[[^80]\]. Its reciprocal functional loop with AMPK amplifies this effect: AMPK increases NAD <sup>+</sup> biosynthesis and SIRT1 activity, whereas SIRT1 activates LKB1/AMPK signalling, together driving mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative adaptation under low‐energy conditions \[[^56], [^124], [^125], [^126], [^127]\]. Thus, SIRT1 acts as a nuclear coordinator that reshapes mitochondrial capacity at the transcriptional level rather than by directly modifying mitochondrial matrix enzymes.

The contribution of SIRT6 to mitochondrial metabolism is also largely indirect but metabolically important. By repressing HIF‐1α‐dependent glycolytic gene expression, SIRT6 limits excessive glycolytic reliance and promotes a more oxidative metabolic phenotype \[[^57]\]. In this context, SIRT6 helps to preserve the balance between cytosolic glucose metabolism and mitochondrial oxidation, opposing the shift toward glycolytic reprogramming that often accompanies metabolic disease. Although its actions are mainly chromatin‐based, the downstream consequence is a more efficient allocation of substrates toward mitochondrial catabolism rather than aerobic glycolysis.

SIRT7 has emerged as an important nuclear regulator of mitochondrial homoeostasis. It promotes the expression of nuclear‐encoded mitochondrial genes by deacetylating GA‐binding protein β1 (GABPβ1), facilitating GABP complex formation and transcriptional activation. Deficiency of SIRT7 is associated with systemic mitochondrial dysfunction, including increased lactate levels, impaired exercise performance, cardiac abnormalities, and hepatic microvesicular steatosis, indicative of compromised mitochondrial homoeostasis \[[^128]\].

Taken together, these findings indicate that sirtuins regulate mitochondrial metabolism at multiple levels. SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 directly remodel the activity of mitochondrial enzymes controlling substrate entry, β‐oxidation, TCA cycle flux, ketogenesis, respiratory chain function, and ROS detoxification, whereas SIRT1, SIRT6, and SIRT7 govern transcriptional and epigenetic programs that determine mitochondrial biogenesis, substrate preference, and overall oxidative capacity. Therefore, the sirtuin network acts not as a set of isolated regulators, but as an integrated system that adapts mitochondrial metabolism to nutrient availability and metabolic stress.

### 4.5. Nutrient Sensing and Metabolic Stress Response

Cells continuously adjust their metabolic programs in response to changes in nutrient availability, energy requirements, and environmental stress. These adaptive responses rely on molecular nutrient‐sensing pathways that integrate intracellular energy status with transcriptional, metabolic, and mitochondrial regulation. Among the key components of this system, sirtuins function as metabolic sensors that translate changes in nutrient availability into coordinated metabolic responses. Because their enzymatic activity depends on intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup>, sirtuins are uniquely positioned to couple the cellular redox state and energy balance to metabolic regulation.

During conditions of nutrient shortage, such as fasting or caloric restriction, intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> levels increase due to enhanced activity of NAD <sup>+</sup> biosynthetic pathways. This elevation in NAD <sup>+</sup> availability activates several sirtuin family members, particularly SIRT1 and SIRT3, which orchestrate metabolic adaptations aimed at maintaining energy homoeostasis. For example, SIRT1 plays a central role in mediating the metabolic response to caloric restriction by regulating transcription factors and coactivators involved in energy metabolism, including PGC‐1α, FOXO proteins, and nuclear receptors controlling fatty acid oxidation and gluconeogenesis \[[^53]\]. Through these interactions, SIRT1 promotes metabolic flexibility, allowing cells to shift from glucose utilisation toward increased fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial respiration during energy deprivation \[[^10]\].

The activity of sirtuins is tightly interconnected with other nutrient‐sensing signalling pathways, most notably AMPK, and the [[mTOR|mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)]]. AMPK acts as a cellular energy sensor that becomes activated under conditions of low ATP levels, such as metabolic stress or exercise \[[^129]\]. Activation of AMPK increases NAD <sup>+</sup> production through stimulation of NAMPT, thereby indirectly enhancing SIRT1 activity \[[^56]\]. Conversely, SIRT1 can activate AMPK through deacetylation of LKB1, establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies metabolic adaptation during energy stress \[[^126], [^127]\]. In contrast, nutrient abundance activates mTOR signalling, which promotes anabolic processes and suppresses autophagy \[[^130]\]. Sirtuins counterbalance this anabolic state by promoting catabolic pathways that restore metabolic equilibrium.

Mitochondrial sirtuins also play a fundamental role in metabolic stress responses. SIRT3 is strongly induced during fasting and caloric restriction, where it enhances mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and protects cells from oxidative stress. By deacetylating metabolic enzymes involved in fatty acid oxidation, the TCA cycle, and antioxidant defence systems such as SOD2, SIRT3 improves mitochondrial efficiency and limits [[Reactive Oxygen Species]] accumulation under metabolic stress \[[^30]\]. This adaptive response is particularly important in highly oxidative tissues, that is, liver, skeletal muscle, and brown adipose tissue.

Other mitochondrial sirtuins contribute to the fine regulation of metabolic stress responses. SIRT4, for example, participates in nutrient sensing by modulating mitochondrial metabolic enzymes and regulating insulin secretion in pancreatic β‐cells \[[^62], [^73]\]. SIRT5, through its desuccinylase and demalonylase activities, regulates metabolic enzymes involved in central carbon metabolism and ammonia detoxification, allowing mitochondria to adapt to changes in metabolic substrate availability \[[^117]\]. These post‐translational modifications provide a rapid mechanism for adjusting mitochondrial metabolic flux in response to nutrient fluctuations.

Nuclear sirtuins play a role in modulating cellular responses to metabolic stress conditions. SIRT6, for example, regulates glucose metabolism and inflammatory responses through chromatin‐based mechanisms and contributes to metabolic adaptation by repressing glycolytic gene expression under conditions of energy stress \[[^131]\]. Similarly, SIRT7 has been implicated in the regulation of metabolic gene expression and mitochondrial homoeostasis during nutrient stress by controlling transcriptional programs involved in energy metabolism. In addition to metabolic regulation, sirtuins are also key mediators of cellular stress resistance. During metabolic stress conditions such as DNA damage, nutrient deprivation, or oxidative stress, sirtuins coordinate protective responses that include enhanced mitochondrial function, activation of antioxidant defences, and modulation of inflammatory signalling pathways. Through these mechanisms, sirtuins contribute not only to metabolic adaptation but also to cellular survival and organismal resilience during environmental stress \[[^132]\].

Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of sirtuins as integrators of nutrient sensing and metabolic stress responses. By linking NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism with mitochondrial function, transcriptional regulation, and cellular signalling pathways, the sirtuin network enables cells to dynamically adapt their metabolism in response to changing nutritional and environmental conditions.

## 5\. Sirtuins in Metabolic Disorders

Metabolic disorders arise when the adaptive networks coordinating nutrient sensing, substrate utilisation, mitochondrial function, and inflammation become chronically dysregulated. Given their NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐dependence and their ability to regulate transcription factors, chromatin and metabolic enzymes, sirtuins are now recognised as central modifiers of the transition from metabolic stress to overt disease. Across insulin resistance, [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus|type 2 diabetes mellitus ([[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus|T2DM]])]], obesity, metabolic associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and cardiometabolic complications, changes in sirtuin expression or activity influence disease severity by modulating lipid storage, glucose handling, mitochondrial efficiency, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signalling \[[^133], [^134], [^135]\]. Although mammalian sirtuins do not act uniformly, a recurrent pattern has emerged where SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 generally preserve metabolic flexibility and protect against disease progression, whereas SIRT7 frequently acts in the opposite direction in adipose tissue and liver. SIRT4 and SIRT5 display more context‐dependent effects linked to mitochondrial substrate selection and post‐translational remodelling of metabolic enzymes.

### 5.1. Obesity and Adipose Tissue Dysfunction

Beyond the accumulation of excess fat, obesity is associated with marked alterations in the structure and function of adipose tissue, a process referred to as adipose tissue remodelling. This remodelling involves adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia, dysregulated lipid mobilisation, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, impaired angiogenesis, immune cell infiltration, and chronic low‐grade inflammation, all of which contribute to systemic insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction \[[^136], [^137]\]. Within this context, sirtuins have emerged as critical regulators of adipose tissue plasticity, orchestrating multiple aspects of adipocyte biology, including white and brown adipocyte differentiation, lipid turnover, mitochondrial function, inflammatory responses, fibrosis, and thermogenic activity.

Under physiological conditions, adipose tissue exhibits a remarkable degree of plasticity, allowing it to expand through hyperplasia while maintaining [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and metabolic homoeostasis. In contrast, obesity is predominantly associated with adipocyte hypertrophy, which leads to cellular stress, hypoxia, and mechanical strain within the tissue. These alterations trigger a cascade of altered responses, including mitochondrial dysfunction, increased production of ROS, and activation of stress signalling pathways, ultimately impairing adipocyte function and systemic metabolic regulation \[[^138]\]. A key feature of dysfunctional adipose tissue is the disruption of lipid handling and metabolic flexibility. In the obesity conditions, adipocytes display impaired capacity to properly store and mobilise lipids, resulting in relatively elevated basal lipolysis and increased release of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the circulation. This ectopic lipid overflow contributes to lipid accumulation in non‐adipose tissues such as liver and skeletal muscle, thereby promoting insulin resistance and lipotoxicity. At the molecular level, these processes are tightly linked to alterations in NAD <sup>+</sup> availability and mitochondrial function, which directly impact the activity of metabolic sensors such as sirtuins \[[^139], [^140], [^141], [^142]\]. At the same time, obesity induces a profound reprogramming of the adipose tissue immune microenvironment. Hypertrophic adipocytes secrete chemokines and danger‐associated signals that promote the recruitment of immune cells, particularly macrophages. The proportion of macrophages within adipose tissue increases dramatically during obesity, accompanied by a phenotypic shift from anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages towards pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages \[[^143], [^144], [^145]\]. These immune cells form characteristic crown‐like structures around dead or dying adipocytes and secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and MCP‐1, which further amplify local inflammation and interfere with insulin signalling pathways. This chronic low‐grade inflammation, often referred to as meta‐inflammation, represents a key mechanistic link between obesity and systemic metabolic diseases \[[^146]\]. In addition to immune dysregulation, obesity is associated with significant alterations in the ECM, leading to adipose tissue fibrosis. Excessive deposition of collagen and other ECM components restricts tissue expandability, increases mechanical stiffness, and exacerbates adipocyte stress and death. Fibrotic remodelling not only impairs adipose tissue function but also perpetuates inflammation by promoting immune cell retention and activation \[[^147]\]. Thus, ECM remodelling emerges as a critical determinant of whether adipose tissue expansion occurs in a metabolically healthy or pathological manner.

Another fundamental aspect of adipose tissue dysfunction in obesity is the impairment of thermogenic capacity and adipose tissue plasticity. Brown and beige adipocytes play a crucial role in energy expenditure through mitochondrial uncoupling and heat production. However, obesity is often associated with reduced browning of white adipose tissue and diminished brown adipose tissue activity, resulting in decreased energy dissipation and further weight gain. This loss of thermogenic potential is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and altered transcriptional regulation of key factors such as PGC‐1α and UCP1 \[[^148]\].

At the molecular level, these diverse pathological processes converge on alterations in nutrient‐sensing and energy‐regulating pathways, among which sirtuins represent central hubs. As NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐dependent enzymes, sirtuins function as metabolic sensors that couple cellular energy status to transcriptional and enzymatic programs controlling adipogenesis, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, inflammation, and stress responses. Importantly, obesity is characterised by reduced NAD <sup>+</sup> availability in adipose tissue, leading to impaired sirtuin activity and disruption of metabolic homoeostasis \[[^139], [^140]\].

Among the sirtuin family, SIRT1 plays a central protective role in adipose tissue. SIRT1 suppresses adipogenesis by repressing PPARγ activity and promotes lipolysis through activation of FOXO1‐dependent [[ATGL]] expression and AMPK signalling. In addition, SIRT1 exerts strong anti‐inflammatory effects by inhibiting NF‐κB signalling, reducing macrophage infiltration, and promoting polarisation toward an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype \[[^81], [^149], [^150], [^151], [^152]\]. Importantly, SIRT1 also contributes to adipose tissue remodelling by limiting ECM deposition and fibrosis and by promoting white adipose tissue browning through deacetylation of PPARγ and activation of thermogenic programs, including PGC‐1α and [[FGF21]] signalling \[[^153]\]. Interestingly, SIRT1‐mediated deacetylation of PPARγ has been shown to induce BAT genes and repress WAT genes \[[^69]\]. However, its role may be context‐dependent, as stage‐specific and tissue‐specific effects have been described during obesity progression \[[^154]\]. SIRT2 shares some functional overlap with SIRT1, particularly in the inhibition of adipogenesis. By deacetylating FOXO1, SIRT2 enhances FOXO1‐mediated repression of PPARγ, thereby limiting adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation. In addition, SIRT2 promotes lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation, suggesting a role in maintaining adipose tissue metabolic flexibility \[[^83]\]. Mitochondrial sirtuins are particularly important for adipose tissue metabolic health. SIRT3 promotes brown adipocyte differentiation and thermogenic competence through PGC‐1α activation and maintenance of mitochondrial function. Loss of SIRT3 leads to increased lipid accumulation, impaired thermogenesis, and enhanced inflammatory responses, partly through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome \[[^155], [^156]\].

Similarly, SIRT5 contributes to adipose tissue remodelling by regulating mitochondrial metabolism and thermogenic gene expression. SIRT5 deficiency impairs fatty acid oxidation and reduces browning capacity in subcutaneous adipose tissue, highlighting its role in energy expenditure and metabolic adaptation \[[^88], [^157]\].

In contrast, SIRT4 generally promotes an energy‐conserving phenotype in adipose tissue. SIRT4 enhances adipocyte differentiation and lipogenesis while repressing fatty acid oxidation through inhibition of malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase, thereby favouring lipid accumulation under nutrient‐rich conditions \[[^87], [^158]\].

SIRT6 exerts predominantly protective effects in adipose tissue by inhibiting adipocyte differentiation, promoting lipolysis, and suppressing inflammatory signalling pathways such as NF‐κB. In addition, SIRT6 supports thermogenic remodelling and browning of adipose tissue, partly through regulation of PGC‐1α and UCP1 expression. Loss of SIRT6 leads to adipocyte hypertrophy, reduced lipolytic activity, and increased inflammation, thereby exacerbating obesity‐related metabolic dysfunction \[[^93], [^159], [^160]\].

In contrast to SIRT1 and SIRT6, SIRT7 appears to promote adipose tissue expansion and dysfunction. SIRT7 enhances adipogenesis and lipogenesis, partly through inhibition of SIRT1 activity and activation of PPARγ‐dependent transcriptional programs. Moreover, SIRT7 has been associated with impaired thermogenesis and increased adipose tissue inflammation, suggesting a role in promoting obesity and metabolic disease \[[^76]\].

Beyond adipocyte‐intrinsic mechanisms, sirtuins also regulate the immune and structural components of adipose tissue remodelling. Chronic overnutrition induces macrophage infiltration and a shift toward pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages, which secrete cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐1β, driving insulin resistance. SIRT1 and SIRT6 counteract this process by suppressing inflammatory signalling pathways and promoting anti‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation, whereas dysregulation of sirtuin activity contributes to sustained inflammation and metabolic dysfunction \[[^161]\].

In addition, sirtuins influence extracellular matrix remodelling and fibrosis, which are increasingly recognised as key determinants of adipose tissue dysfunction. Excessive ECM deposition restricts adipose tissue expandability, promotes adipocyte stress and death, and exacerbates inflammation. SIRT1 has been shown to regulate collagen expression and matrix remodelling, thereby preserving tissue flexibility and metabolic function \[[^162]\].

Human studies provide an important translational counterpart to experimental evidence on SIRT1 in metabolic regulation. Circulating SIRT1, measured in serum, plasma, or whole blood, has been investigated as a potential marker of metabolic health across conditions characterised by excess or reduced energy availability. In subjects with obesity, circulating SIRT1 has been associated with MASLD and liver fat content, visceral and epicardial adiposity, BMI, waist circumference, fat mass, insulin levels, LDL cholesterol, and inflammatory indices \[[^163], [^164], [^165], [^166]\]. Consistently, SIRT1 levels have been reported to increase following intragastric balloon–induced weight loss and intermittent caloric restriction in selected cohorts, supporting its sensitivity to energy balance and metabolic adaptation \[[^167], [^168]\]. Across the underweight—normal weight—obesity spectrum, circulating SIRT1 appears to be highest in anorexia nervosa and lowest in obesity, and shows consistent relationships with leptin and adiponectin, reinforcing its association with adipose tissue distribution and systemic energy availability \[[^165], [^169]\]. Additional clinical evidence links circulating SIRT1 to bone metabolism through its association with sclerostin and bone status in women with different degrees of adiposity \[[^170]\]. Exercise studies further indicate that SIRT1 can be modulated by both acute and chronic exercise interventions, although the direction and magnitude of the response depend on tissue source, assay type, and exercise protocol \[[^171]\]. Moreover, evidence from lipodystrophic syndromes and from matched analyses of circulating and subcutaneous adipose tissue SIRT1 protein levels in human obesity suggests that circulating SIRT1 may reflect adipose tissue distribution, adipose tissue quality, and tissue SIRT1 expression rather than adiposity alone \[[^172], [^173]\]. Overall, these data suggest that circulating SIRT1 may represent an accessible associative biomarker of metabolic flexibility and systemic energy status, although it should not be interpreted as a direct surrogate of intracellular SIRT1 enzymatic activity. Recent mechanistic evidence further supports a broader pathophysiological relevance of SIRT1‐dependent signalling axes In colorectal cancer, an NR3C2 (the gene encoding for the mineralocorticoid receptor)–SIRT1 axis has been shown to promote autophagy and suppress epithelial–mesenchymal transition, thereby reducing metastatic behaviour both in vivo and in vitro \[[^174]\]. In adipocytes, Angiotensin II (AngII)‐induced RAAS activation disrupts the Haem Oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1)–SIRT1 axis, promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, and adipocyte dysfunction; conversely, HO‐1 induction restores SIRT1 expression and attenuates AngII‐driven cellular injury, suggesting that this pathway may be therapeutically relevant for RAAS‐associated adipocyte dysfunction \[[^175]\].

Collectively, these findings indicate that sirtuins orchestrate adipose tissue remodelling through coordinated regulation of adipogenesis, lipid mobilisation, mitochondrial function, inflammation, fibrosis, and thermogenesis. In obesity, disruption of this network characterised by reduced activity of protective sirtuins such as SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 and relative activation of SIRT4 and SIRT7 pathways contributes to adipose tissue dysfunction and the development of systemic metabolic disease.

### 5.2. Insulin Resistance and T2DM

Insulin resistance and T2DM develop through progressive impairment of insulin signalling in liver, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and pancreatic β‐cells, while sirtuins participate in each of these nodes. SIRT1 improves insulin sensitivity by enhancing fatty acid oxidation, supporting mitochondrial biogenesis, suppressing inflammatory signalling, and coordinating hepatic glucose production with fasting responses. SIRT1 functions as a central metabolic sensor that exerts predominantly protective effects against type 2 diabetes through coordinated, deacetylation‐dependent regulation of key signalling and transcriptional networks. Mechanistically, in pancreatic β‐cells SIRT1 enhances glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion by repressing [[UCP2]], thereby increasing mitochondrial ATP production and promoting Ca <sup>2+</sup> ‐dependent insulin exocytosis, while also preserving β‐cell survival via FOXO1‐mediated activation of insulin gene regulators and suppression of NF‐κB–driven inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways \[[^176], [^177]\]. In peripheral tissues, SIRT1 improves insulin sensitivity by directly modulating insulin signalling: it represses the negative regulator PTP1B and deacetylates IRS‐2, enabling efficient tyrosine phosphorylation and downstream PI3K–Akt activation \[[^178], [^179]\]. Concurrently, SIRT1 attenuates chronic inflammation, which is a key driver of insulin resistance through deacetylation of the NF‐κB p65 subunit, thereby reducing proinflammatory cytokine transcription in adipose tissue and macrophages \[[^180], [^181]\]. At the level of energy metabolism, SIRT1 activates PGC‐1α and PPARα to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and fatty acid oxidation, reducing ectopic lipid accumulation and improving metabolic flexibility in muscle and liver \[[^80], [^182]\]. In hepatocytes, SIRT1 integrates fasting signals by dynamically regulating gluconeogenesis through deacetylation of CRTC2, FOXO1, and PGC‐1α, while also modulating STAT3 activity, highlighting a context‐dependent role in hepatic glucose output \[[^183], [^184]\]. Additionally, SIRT1 enhances adiponectin production via FOXO1–C/EBPα complexes and suppresses lipogenesis through inhibition of SREBP‐1c, further contributing to systemic insulin sensitisation \[[^185]\].

SIRT6 also exerts antidiabetic effects by repressing excessive glycolysis, modulating hepatic and adipose inflammation, and helping preserve whole‐body glucose homoeostasis \[[^186], [^187]\]. Mechanistically, SIRT6 suppresses glycolysis by inhibiting [[HIF-1α|Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha]]–dependent transcription of glycolytic genes (e.g., GLUT1, LDH, PFK1), shifting cellular metabolism towards mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation; loss of SIRT6 leads to HIF‐1α activation, enhanced glycolysis, and increased glucose uptake that contributes to systemic metabolic imbalance \[[^57], [^188]\]. In parallel, SIRT6 restrains hepatic gluconeogenesis through dual epigenetic mechanisms: it activates the acetyltransferase GCN5 to increase acetylation and inhibition of PGC‐1α, and directly interacts with FOXO1 to reduce its promoter binding at G6pc and Pepck, thereby limiting glucose production \[[^189], [^190]\]. Regarding insulin signalling, SIRT6 negatively regulates Akt phosphorylation by suppressing upstream insulin receptor/IRS signalling, thereby preventing excessive glucose uptake and maintaining metabolic balance \[[^191]\]. In pancreatic β‐cells, SIRT6 preserves glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion and cell survival by maintaining mitochondrial function and repressing TXNIP expression via histone deacetylation (H3K9/H3K56), linking the chromatin state to insulin secretory capacity \[[^192], [^193]\]. Beyond glucose metabolism, SIRT6 improves systemic insulin sensitivity by inhibiting lipogenesis (via repression of [[SREBP-1c|SREBP1]]/2), promoting fatty acid β‐oxidation, and enhancing adipose lipolysis through FOXO1‐dependent induction of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), thereby preventing ectopic lipid accumulation and lipotoxic insulin resistance \[[^93]\]. Additionally, SIRT6 decreases chronic inflammation, which is a key driver of insulin resistance, through deacetylation of histone H3K9 at NF‐κB target gene promoters, suppressing proinflammatory transcriptional programs \[[^96]\]. These mechanisms position SIRT6 as an epigenetic gatekeeper that integrates nutrient status with transcriptional control of glucose production, utilisation, β‐cell function, and inflammatory tone, and whose loss promotes insulin resistance and diabetes. In contrast to the generally beneficial effects of SIRT1 and SIRT6, SIRT7 has been associated with metabolic phenotypes that include glucose intolerance under high‐fat feeding, reinforcing the notion that nuclear sirtuins can have opposing actions in diabetes‐related settings. Mechanistically, SIRT7 promotes gluconeogenesis in the liver by destabilising the circadian regulator CRY1 and enhancing transcription of gluconeogenic genes such as G6pc, thereby contributing to increased hepatic glucose output. At the same time, SIRT7 suppresses glycolysis via inhibition of HIF1 signalling and deacetylation of glycolytic enzymes such as PGK1, further biasing metabolism towards glucose production rather than utilisation. In adipose tissue and liver, SIRT7 promotes lipogenesis and lipid accumulation while inhibiting thermogenesis, processes that are typically associated with insulin resistance. Consistent with these metabolic effects, SIRT7 deficiency in animal models confers protection against high‐fat diet–induced obesity, insulin resistance, and glucose intolerance, partly through enhanced energy expenditure and increased expression of insulin‐sensitising factors such as FGF21 \[[^60]\].

Mitochondrial sirtuins additionally shape β‐cell and substrate‐specific responses relevant to diabetes. In mitochondria, SIRT3 supports insulin responsiveness by maintaining oxidative metabolism, limiting ROS accumulation, and preserving metabolic flexibility; conversely, reduced SIRT3 activity is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, ectopic lipid accumulation, and impaired insulin signalling \[[^61], [^194]\]. SIRT2 appears more complex: in adipose precursors it inhibits adipogenesis, yet in pancreatic β‐cells it has been implicated in adaptive β‐cell proliferation and metabolic control, indicating that its contribution is highly tissue‐dependent \[[^84], [^133], [^195]\]. SIRT4 inhibits glutamate dehydrogenase and modulates insulin secretion, thereby linking mitochondrial amino acid sensing to endocrine control. Depending on the context, this may be metabolically adaptive or may contribute to impaired flexibility \[[^62]\]. SIRT5, through desuccinylation and demalonylation of metabolic enzymes, influences glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and mitochondrial respiration, suggesting that altered SIRT5 activity may affect insulin‐sensitive tissues by changing central carbon metabolism and redox balance \[[^74], [^104]\].

Overall, insulin resistance and T2DM can be viewed, at least in part, as disorders of defective sirtuin‐mediated coordination between nutrient sensing, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory restraint \[[^133]\].

### 5.3. Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Fatty Liver Disease and Steatohepatitis

In MASLD, impaired fatty acid oxidation, enhanced de novo lipogenesis, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signalling drive the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis and fibrosis \[[^196]\].

Mechanistically, nuclear SIRT1 acts as a master metabolic regulator by deacetylating PGC‐1α to drive mitochondrial biogenesis and fatty acid β‐oxidation, while simultaneously suppressing lipogenesis through inhibition of SREBP‐1c and activation of AMPK signalling, thereby reducing hepatic triglyceride accumulation and restoring metabolic flexibility \[[^65]\]. In parallel, mitochondrial sirtuins, particularly SIRT3, directly reprogramme mitochondrial metabolism through lysine deacetylation of key enzymes, enhancing fatty acid oxidation and antioxidant defences; for instance, SIRT3‐mediated deacetylation of [[MnSOD]] increases its enzymatic activity, lowering mitochondrial ROS and preventing oxidative damage, whereas SIRT3 deficiency leads to impaired oxidative phosphorylation, increased ROS accumulation, and exacerbated steatosis and inflammation \[[^28], [^110], [^197]\].

SIRT5 complements these effects by regulating metabolic flux through lysine desuccinylation and demalonylation of enzymes involved in β‐oxidation and ketogenesis, thereby sustaining mitochondrial energy production and limiting lipid accumulation \[[^17], [^198]\]. In contrast, SIRT4 exerts context‐dependent and often opposing effects by inhibiting fatty acid oxidation via repression of PPARα and destabilisation of mitochondrial trifunctional protein α (MTPα), promoting lipid storage and potentially accelerating MASLD progression under nutrient‐rich conditions \[[^115]\]. Beyond mitochondrial enzymes, chromatin‐associated sirtuins such as SIRT6 and SIRT7 regulate hepatic metabolism at the transcriptional and epigenetic levels. SIRT6 suppresses glycolysis and triglyceride synthesis through histone H3 deacetylation and alleviates [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]] stress via regulation of XBP1, while also promoting fatty acid oxidation and reducing inflammatory cytokine production \[[^135]\]. SIRT7, in turn, modulates lipid metabolism and stress responses by controlling transcriptional programs linked to ribosomal biogenesis, [[c-Myc|Myc]] signalling, and nuclear receptor pathways that influence triglyceride synthesis and fatty acid uptake \[[^199]\].

Collectively, reduced expression or activity of most sirtuins in MASLD leads to hyperacetylation of mitochondrial and nuclear proteins, impaired oxidative metabolism, excessive ROS generation, and dysregulated lipid handling, establishing a feed‐forward cycle of steatosis, insulin resistance, inflammation, and hepatocellular injury that drives progression from simple fatty liver to MASLD and fibrosis.

### 5.4. Metabolic Syndrome and Cardiometabolic Complications

Sirtuins act as central integrators of cellular energy status and stress responses in metabolic syndromes, extending their influence beyond classical metabolic tissues to orchestrate cardiometabolic homoeostasis through coordinated epigenetic and post‐translational mechanisms. Among these, SIRT1 functions as a NAD <sup>+</sup> ‐dependent deacetylase linking the redox state to vascular signalling, particularly via modulation of [[eNOS]] activity, with consequent effects on endothelial function and vascular remodelling \[[^200], [^201]\]. Mechanistically, SIRT1 activity is tightly coupled to intracellular NAD <sup>+</sup> availability, positioning it as a metabolic sensor that translates fluctuations in nutrient flux into adaptive transcriptional programs; this is particularly relevant in metabolic syndrome, where altered NAD <sup>+</sup> /NADH ratios and impaired salvage pathways constrain SIRT1 catalytic efficiency and downstream signalling. In the vasculature, SIRT1 exerts pleiotropic protective effects by deacetylating eNOS to enhance nitric oxide bioavailability, while simultaneously repressing NF‐κB–dependent transcription of adhesion molecules and cytokines, thereby attenuating endothelial activation and vascular inflammation, which are key initiating events in atherogenesis \[[^202]\]. In addition, SIRT1‐mediated deacetylation of FOXO1 and FOXO3 shifts their transcriptional output towards stress resistance and cell‐cycle arrest rather than apoptosis, promoting endothelial cell survival under oxidative and glucolipotoxic stress conditions characteristic of metabolic syndrome \[[^203], [^204]\]. Importantly, SIRT1 also regulates endothelial progenitor cell function and senescence, processes that are critically impaired in cardiometabolic disease. Reduced SIRT1 expression under hyperglycaemic conditions accelerates progenitor cell ageing, whereas its activation restores angiogenic competence and vascular repair capacity \[[^203], [^205]\]. Beyond the endothelium, SIRT1 influences vascular smooth muscle cell phenotype and plaque stability, partly through modulation of inflammatory signalling and lipid handling, including effects on macrophage foam cell formation via regulation of cholesterol efflux pathways \[[^205], [^206]\]. Notably, these cardiovascular actions are highly dose‐dependent, as moderate SIRT1 activation confers cardioprotection, whereas excessive activity may disrupt mitochondrial energetics and contribute to maladaptive cardiac remodelling, underscoring the importance of context‐specific regulation \[[^207], [^208]\].

Other sirtuins contribute to distinct compartmentalised layers of regulation that complement SIRT1 in cardiometabolic disease. Mitochondrial SIRT3, for instance, governs oxidative metabolism and ROS detoxification through deacetylation of key enzymes such as isocitrate dehydrogenase 2, SOD2, and components of the electron transport chain \[[^209], [^210]\]. In metabolic syndrome, reduced SIRT3 activity leads to hyperacetylation of mitochondrial proteins, impaired fatty acid oxidation, and elevated mitochondrial ROS, thereby exacerbating [[Endothelial Dysfunction]] and cardiac injury \[[^211]\].

In addition, SIRT3 modulates the mitochondrial permeability transition pore and exerts protective effects during ischaemia–reperfusion injury, connecting mitochondrial proteostasis with cardiomyocyte viability \[[^212], [^213]\]. SIRT2 regulates microtubule dynamics and inflammatory signalling; its deacetylation of α‐tubulin and modulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation suggest a role in vascular inflammation and insulin resistance–associated endothelial dysfunction, although its net effect appears context‐dependent, with both protective and deleterious phenotypes reported \[[^214], [^215], [^216]\].

In cardiometabolic settings, SIRT6 deficiency promotes endothelial inflammation and accelerates [[Atherosclerosis]] through derepression of NF‐κB target genes, while also impairing lipid homoeostasis via dysregulation of PCSK9 and LDL metabolism \[[^217]\]. SIRT4 and SIRT5 contribute to mitochondrial metabolic inflexibility in metabolic syndrome via distinct enzymatic activities. SIRT4 limits anaplerotic flux and insulin secretion through ADP‐ribosylation‐dependent inhibition of glutamate dehydrogenase and regulation of amino acid metabolism, whereas SIRT5 controls fatty acid oxidation and TCA cycle activity through desuccinylation and demalonylation; disruption of these pathways promotes acylation‐dependent mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired substrate oxidation, and redox imbalance \[[^62]\]. SIRT5‐mediated desuccinylation of enzymes in the TCA and urea cycle is particularly relevant in conditions of nutrient excess, where aberrant acylation states contribute to metabolic inflexibility and ammonia handling defects \[[^15]\]. Collectively, sirtuins form an integrated network in which subcellular localisation dictates substrate specificity and functional output, enabling fine‐tuned regulation of metabolic flux, redox balance, and inflammatory signalling. In metabolic syndrome, disruption of this network, via altered NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism, chronic nutrient excess, and oxidative stress, results in coordinated dysregulation of vascular, cardiac, and immune pathways, thereby driving the progression toward overt cardiometabolic complications.

## 6\. Conclusions

Sirtuins have emerged as central regulators of metabolic homoeostasis, acting at the intersection of cellular energy sensing, transcriptional control, and mitochondrial function. Their strict dependence on NAD <sup>+</sup> availability positions them as dynamic sensors of metabolic state, enabling the integration of nutrient signals with epigenetic and post‐translational regulatory mechanisms across multiple cellular compartments. A key concept that arises from current evidence is that sirtuins do not function as isolated enzymes, but rather as components of a coordinated and compartmentalised regulatory network. Nuclear sirtuins, including SIRT1, SIRT6, and SIRT7, primarily govern transcriptional programs that regulate metabolism, inflammation, and mitochondrial biogenesis, whereas mitochondrial sirtuins, such as SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5, directly modulate enzymatic activity and metabolic fluxes within the organelle. Within this framework, a functional polarisation becomes apparent: SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 generally promote metabolic flexibility, oxidative metabolism, and stress resistance, while SIRT4 and SIRT7 are more frequently associated with energy conservation and, under certain conditions, metabolic dysfunction. SIRT2 plays an intermediate and context‐dependent role linking cytoskeletal organisation, cell cycle progression, and metabolic regulation.

Importantly, metabolic diseases can be interpreted as states of disrupted sirtuin network coordination rather than the consequence of alterations in single isoforms. Changes in NAD <sup>+</sup> homoeostasis, chronic nutrient excess, and persistent inflammatory signalling collectively shift the balance of sirtuin activity, leading to impaired metabolic flexibility, mitochondrial dysfunction, and maladaptive tissue remodelling. This network‐based perspective helps to explain the heterogeneity observed across tissues, disease stages, and experimental models. Despite substantial progress, several challenges remain. The context‐dependent and sometimes opposing roles of different sirtuin isoforms complicate therapeutic targeting, and most mechanistic insights derive from preclinical systems with limited direct translation to human physiology. Furthermore, the spatial and temporal regulation of NAD <sup>+</sup> metabolism and its compartmentalised impact on sirtuin activity are not yet fully understood. Future research should therefore focus on defining the dynamics of the sirtuin network in human metabolic disease, identifying isoform‐specific functions in distinct tissues, and developing strategies capable of selectively modulating sirtuin activity or restoring NAD <sup>+</sup> homoeostasis. In this context, approaches targeting NAD <sup>+</sup> biosynthesis and turnover, as well as isoform‐selective modulators, represent promising avenues for multi‐level therapeutic intervention.

In conclusion, sirtuins should be viewed not as individual metabolic regulators but as an integrated system that links energy status to cellular adaptation. A deeper understanding of this network will be essential for translating sirtuin biology into effective strategies for the prevention and rehabilitational treatment of metabolic diseases.
