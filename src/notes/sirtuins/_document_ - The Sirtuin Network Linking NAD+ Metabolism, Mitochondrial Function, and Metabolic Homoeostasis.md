## ABSTRACT

Metabolic disorders such as [[Obesity]], [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]], and [[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|fatty liver disease]] are associated with a disruption in the coordinated regulation of nutrient sensing, [[Energy Balance|energy metabolism]], and cellular homoeostasis. Increasing evidence indicates that [[Sirtuins]], a family of [[NAD+]]‐dependent enzymes, play a central role in integrating metabolic and stress‐responsive pathways. By coupling the cellular [[Redox]] state to transcriptional and post‐translational regulation, [[Sirtuins]] coordinate key processes such as [[Glycolysis|glucose]] and [[Lipid Metabolism]], [[Mitochondria]]l function, and inflammatory signalling. [[Sirtuins]] are functionally specialised according to their subcellular localisation. Nuclear sirtuins ([[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], and [[SIRT7]]) regulate transcriptional programs controlling metabolism and [[Inflammation]], whereas mitochondrial sirtuins ([[SIRT3]], [[SIRT4]], and [[SIRT5]]) directly modulate metabolic enzyme activity and [[Redox Homeostasis|redox homoeostasis]]. [[SIRT2]] links cytosolic metabolic signalling to cytoskeletal dynamics and [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] regulation. Importantly, the activity of all sirtuins is tightly dependent on [[NAD+]] availability, positioning [[NAD+ Biosynthesis|NAD+ metabolism]] as a central regulator of the sirtuin network. In this review, we provide a systems‐level perspective on sirtuin biology, highlighting how [[NAD+ Biosynthesis|NAD+ metabolism]], subcellular compartmentalisation, and specific functions converge to form an integrated regulatory network governing metabolic homoeostasis. We further discuss how disruption of this network contributes to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders through impaired [[Metabolic Flexibility]], [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], and chronic [[Inflammation]]. Overall, this network‐based framework provides a unified view of sirtuin function in metabolic regulation and helps to explain the complexity and tissue‐specific heterogeneity of metabolic diseases.

**Keywords:** metabolic disorders, metabolic homoeostasis, mitochondrial function, [[NAD+]] metabolism, sirtuins

## 1\. Introduction

Metabolic diseases comprise a heterogeneous spectrum of conditions defined by altered control of energy homoeostasis, nutrient handling, and cellular regulatory processes \[[^1]\]. Their global prevalence has risen dramatically over recent decades, driven by complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, lifestyle changes, and ageing \[[^2]\]. Major conditions such as [[Obesity]], [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]], [[Hyperlipidemia|dyslipidemia]], and [[Metabolic Syndrome]] not only increase the risk of [[Cardiovascular Disease]] but also contribute to chronic [[Inflammation]], organ dysfunction, and reduced life expectancy \[[^3], [^4], [^5]\]. Although clinical management has improved substantially, most current therapies primarily address downstream effects rather than the molecular mechanisms driving metabolic dysregulation \[[^6], [^7]\]. At the cellular level, metabolic homoeostasis is maintained by tightly coordinated signalling networks that integrate nutrient availability, hormonal inputs, and stress responses to preserve physiological function \[[^8]\]. Disruption of these networks leads to maladaptive metabolic remodelling, altered gene expression, and [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], which collectively drive disease progression \[[^9]\]. In this context, increasing attention has been directed towards molecular systems capable of linking cellular energy status to transcriptional control, protein modification, and metabolic adaptation. Among these, [[Sirtuins]] have emerged as key regulators at the crossroads of metabolism, [[Epigenetic Alterations|epigenetics]], and mitochondrial function. As [[Nicotinamide]] adenine dinucleotide ([[NAD+]])‐dependent enzymes, sirtuins are uniquely positioned to detect fluctuations in cellular [[Redox]] and energy states and translate them into coordinated transcriptional and post‐translational responses. Over the past 2 decades, a large body of evidence has implicated sirtuins in the regulation of [[Glycolysis|glucose]] and [[Lipid Metabolism]], [[Mitochondria]]l activity, [[Inflammation]], and [[Cellular Senescence|ageing]]‐related processes \[[^10], [^11], [^12], [^13], [^14], [^15], [^16], [^17]\].

Despite this progress, most studies have examined individual sirtuin isoforms in isolation, leaving the broader architecture of sirtuin‐mediated metabolic regulation incompletely understood. A more integrative perspective is therefore needed to capture how different sirtuins cooperate across subcellular compartments to maintain metabolic homoeostasis. In this review, we provide a systems‐level analysis of sirtuin biology, focusing on how [[NAD+ Biosynthesis|NAD+ metabolism]], subcellular compartmentalisation, and isoform‐specific functions converge to form an integrated regulatory network. We discuss how nuclear and mitochondrial sirtuins coordinate key metabolic processes, including [[Glycolysis|glucose]] and [[Lipid Metabolism]], [[Mitochondria]]l function, [[Thermogenesis]], and inflammatory signalling. We further examine how disruption of this network contributes to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders such as [[Obesity]], [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]], [[Metabolic Syndrome]], and [[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|fatty liver disease]] through impaired [[Metabolic Flexibility]], [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], and chronic [[Inflammation]]. By synthesising current evidence across molecular, cellular, and physiological levels, this network‐based framework aims to unify the diverse functions of sirtuins in metabolic regulation and to provide a basis for understanding the tissue‐specific complexity of metabolic diseases.

## 2\. Sirtuins

Sirtuins are a conserved family of [[NAD+]]‐dependent enzymes that function as central regulators of cellular metabolism, stress responses, and [[Genomic Instability|genome stability]] \[[^10]\]. Originally identified as Silent Information Regulator 2 ([[Sir2 (yeast)|Sir2]]) homologues, mammalian sirtuins (SIRT1–SIRT7) are classified as class III [[HDAC inhibitors|histone deacetylases]], although their enzymatic repertoire extends beyond [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] to include diverse acyl‐removing activities such as [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]], [[Acetylation|demalonylation]], and [[ADP-ribosylation]] \[[^11]\]. Through these reactions, sirtuins couple cellular energy status to post‐translational modification of histone and non‐histone proteins, thereby linking metabolism to transcriptional and enzymatic regulation.

A defining feature of sirtuin biology is their compartmentalised distribution, which underlies their functional specialisation. Nuclear sirtuins, including [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], and [[SIRT7]], primarily regulate [[Chromatin Remodeling|chromatin structure]] and transcriptional programs controlling metabolism, [[Inflammation]], and stress responses. In contrast, mitochondrial sirtuins ([[SIRT3]], [[SIRT4]], and [[SIRT5]]) directly modulate metabolic enzyme activity and mitochondrial homoeostasis, while [[SIRT2]], predominantly cytosolic, connects metabolic signalling with cytoskeletal dynamics and [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] regulation. This spatial organisation enables sirtuins to coordinate metabolic processes across cellular compartments rather than acting as isolated regulators \[[^12], [^13], [^14], [^15], [^16], [^17]\].

Within the nuclear sirtuin family, [[SIRT1]] is the best‐characterised member and acts as a major integrator of metabolic regulation. By [[Deacetylation|deacetylating]] transcription factors and coactivators such as [[PGC-1α]], [[FOXO]] proteins, and [[NF-κB]], SIRT1 promotes [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], enhances [[Fatty acid oxidation]], and suppresses inflammatory signalling. These actions position SIRT1 as a central mediator of metabolic adaptation to nutrient availability and stress \[[^18], [^19], [^20]\]. [[SIRT6]] complements these functions at the chromatin level by repressing glycolytic gene expression through inhibition of [[HIF-1α]] and by maintaining genomic stability via histone [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] \[[^21], [^22], [^23]\]. In contrast, [[SIRT7]] exerts more context‐dependent effects, including regulation of [[Ribosome Biogenesis|ribosomal biogenesis]], mitochondrial gene expression, and [[Lipid Metabolism]], and in several metabolic settings has been associated with anabolic and lipid‐accumulating programs \[[^24], [^25], [^26], [^27]\].

Mitochondrial sirtuins directly regulate metabolic flux through post‐translational modification of key enzymes. [[SIRT3]], the principal mitochondrial [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]], enhances [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]] by activating enzymes involved in fatty acid β‐oxidation, the [[TCA cycle]], and the [[Electron Transport Chain]], while also promoting antioxidant defences through activation of [[MnSOD]] and related pathways \[[^28], [^29], [^30], [^31]\]. [[SIRT4]] generally counterbalances catabolic metabolism by inhibiting fatty acid oxidation and modulating amino acid‐driven [[Insulin Secretion]], thereby contributing to energy conservation under nutrient‐rich conditions. [[SIRT5]] regulates mitochondrial metabolism through removal of negatively charged acyl modifications, particularly [[Acetylation|succinylation]], [[Acetylation|malonylation]], and [[Acetylation|glutarylation]], thereby influencing multiple pathways including fatty acid oxidation, [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], and ammonia detoxification \[[^32], [^33]\]. [[SIRT2]] occupies a distinct position within the sirtuin family, linking metabolic regulation to cytoskeletal organisation and cell division. Through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of substrates such as [[Tubulin|α‐tubulin]] and [[FoxO1]], SIRT2 modulates microtubule dynamics, [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] progression, and [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]], highlighting its context‐dependent role at the interface of metabolism and cellular architecture \[[^34], [^35], [^36], [^37]\].

Taken together, these findings indicate that sirtuins function as an integrated and compartmentalised regulatory network, as depicted in Figure [1](#dmrr70201-fig-0001). Rather than functioning as independent enzymes, individual sirtuin isoforms contribute to an integrated system that links [[NAD+]] metabolism, transcriptional regulation, and mitochondrial function to maintain cellular and organismal metabolic homoeostasis.

## 3\. NAD+ Metabolism as a Central Regulator of Sirtuin Activity

[[NAD+]] is an essential metabolic cofactor whose intracellular availability is tightly regulated through multiple biosynthetic routes, reflecting its central importance for cellular homoeostasis and for the activity of [[NAD+]]‐dependent enzymes such as sirtuins \[[^38]\]. [[NAD+]] can be generated from the four principal precursors [[Tryptophan|tryptophan]], [[Niacin]], [[Nicotinamide]], and [[Nicotinamide Riboside]], each entering distinct but convergent metabolic pathways that ultimately yield the same dinucleotide (Figure [2](#dmrr70201-fig-0002)) \[[^39]\]. [[NAD+]] can be synthesised through several pathways, among which the [[Preiss-Handler Pathway]] relies on nicotinic acid as the primary precursor. Within this pathway, nicotinic acid is first converted into nicotinic acid mononucleotide by nicotinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase ([[NAPRT]]), after which [[NMNAT|nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases]] catalyse its conversion to nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide. The final amidation step, mediated by [[NAD+ Biosynthesis|NAD synthase]], produces [[NAD+]]. An alternative route for de novo biosynthesis originates from [[Tryptophan]] via the [[Kynurenine Pathway]]. Through a series of enzymatic reactions, tryptophan is metabolised to [[Quinolinic Acid|quinolinic acid]], which is then converted into nicotinic acid mononucleotide by quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase ([[QPRT]]) and funnelled into the downstream steps of the [[Preiss-Handler Pathway]] \[[^40]\]. In addition to these pathways, [[NAD+]] is efficiently regenerated through salvage mechanisms that recycle [[Nicotinamide]], a byproduct of [[NAD+]]‐consuming reactions, including those catalysed by sirtuins. In the [[NAD+ Salvage Pathway]], nicotinamide is recycled into [[Nicotinamide Mononucleotide]] through the action of nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase ([[NAMPT]]), which represents a rate‐limiting and energetically costly step; this intermediate is then converted into [[NAD+]] by [[NMNAT|nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases]] (Figure [2](#dmrr70201-fig-0002)) \[[^41], [^42]\].

![FIGURE 2](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/cae3/13364290/6ad3a64bf5e1/DMRR-42-e70201-g001.jpg)

[[NAD+]] metabolism as a central regulator of sirtuin activity and cellular energy homoeostasis. [[NAD+]] is produced through de novo, [[Preiss-Handler Pathway|Preiss–Handler]], and [[NAD+ Salvage Pathway|salvage pathways]] that collectively maintain intracellular [[NAD+]] pools. Beyond its role as a redox cofactor in central metabolic pathways, [[NAD+]] serves as an essential substrate for sirtuins, directly linking its availability to their enzymatic activity. Changes in [[NAD+]] levels across cellular compartments dynamically regulate the sirtuin network, thereby influencing mitochondrial function, metabolic pathways, and stress responses.

Sirtuin enzymes require [[NAD+]] as an essential co‐substrate for their catalytic activity. During the [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] reaction, [[NAD+]] is consumed and cleaved, linking sirtuin activity directly to intracellular [[NAD+]] availability. Consequently, enzymes that regulate cellular [[NAD+]] metabolism can indirectly modulate sirtuins function \[[^43]\]. An important regulator of [[NAD+]] homoeostasis is [[NNMT|nicotinamide N‐methyltransferase]], which catalyses the methylation of [[Nicotinamide]] to generate [[1-Methylnicotinamide]], effectively diverting it from the [[NAD+ Salvage Pathway|salvage pathway]] \[[^44], [^45], [^46]\]. Consistent with this central metabolic role, dysregulated [[NNMT]] expression has been implicated in several pathological conditions, including metabolic disorders such as [[Obesity]] and [[Metabolic Syndrome]], as well as in numerous cancers, where it contributes to metabolic reprogramming \[[^47], [^48], [^49], [^50]\]. By influencing the pool of [[NAD+]] precursors and overall [[NAD+]] homoeostasis, [[NNMT]] can indirectly affect the activity of [[NAD+]]‐dependent enzymes such as sirtuins \[[^51]\].

> [!info] NAD+ consuming enzymes
> Beyond sirtuins, other major [[NAD+]]‐consuming enzymes include [[PARP1]], [[PARP2]], [[CD38]], and [[SARM1]]. Activity of these enzymes—particularly [[CD38]], which increases with [[Aging|age]]—can deplete cellular [[NAD+]] pools and compromise sirtuin function.

Beyond its role as a substrate for sirtuins, [[NAD+]] is indispensable for cellular energy metabolism. It serves as an essential redox cofactor in [[Glycolysis]], where its reduction to [[NADH]] is required for the conversion of glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate to [[1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate|1,3‐bisphosphoglycerate]], and in the [[TCA cycle|tricarboxylic acid cycle]], where multiple dehydrogenase reactions generate mitochondrial [[NADH]]. The [[NADH]] produced in these pathways is subsequently oxidised by the mitochondrial [[Electron Transport Chain]], linking [[NAD+]]/[[NADH]] homoeostasis to [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]] \[[^38]\]. Consequently, fluctuations in [[NAD+]] availability directly influence cytosolic, nuclear, and mitochondrial [[Redox|redox]] states. Since sirtuin activity depends directly on intracellular [[NAD+]] levels, these enzymes act as metabolic sensors that connect cellular energy status with transcriptional and metabolic regulation. Thus, [[NAD+]] metabolism does not merely sustain sirtuin activity but actively shapes the functional state of the sirtuin network across cellular compartments.

## 4\. Sirtuins as Key Regulators of Metabolic Homoeostasis

### 4.1. Regulation of Glucose Metabolism by Sirtuins

Maintenance of glucose homoeostasis requires the coordinated regulation of multiple metabolic processes, including [[Glycolysis]], [[Gluconeogenesis]], [[Insulin Secretion]], and mitochondrial [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]]. Sirtuins have been identified as central regulators of these pathways, acting as metabolic sensors that link cellular energy status to transcriptional and enzymatic regulation. Because their catalytic activity depends on [[NAD+]], fluctuations in intracellular [[NAD+]] levels during [[Fasting]], [[Caloric Restriction]], or metabolic stress directly influence sirtuin activity and thereby modulate glucose metabolism \[[^52]\].

Among the members of the sirtuin family, [[SIRT1]] has been widely investigated and is recognised as a key regulator of glucose metabolic processes. SIRT1 modulates metabolic homoeostasis through the [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of several transcription factors and metabolic regulators involved in glucose metabolism. In particular, SIRT1 regulates the activity of [[FOXO|forkhead box O (FOXO)]] transcription factors and the transcriptional coactivator [[PGC-1α|peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ coactivator‐1α (PGC‐1α)]], thereby promoting hepatic [[Gluconeogenesis]] and metabolic adaptation during nutrient deprivation \[[^53]\]. Through these mechanisms, SIRT1 regulates hepatic glucose production and supports systemic metabolic control across multiple tissues, including [[Skeletal Muscle]], [[Adipose Tissue]], and pancreatic β‐cells \[[^54], [^55]\]. Furthermore, SIRT1 interacts with major metabolic signalling pathways including [[AMPK|AMP‐activated protein kinase]], highlighting its central role in linking nutrient sensing to metabolic regulation \[[^56]\].

Beyond SIRT1, other nuclear sirtuins are also involved in the transcriptional regulation of glucose metabolism, with [[SIRT6]] playing a key role in controlling glycolytic gene expression. SIRT6 exerts an inhibitory effect on [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]] by attenuating the transcriptional activity of [[HIF-1α|hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α]], a central regulator of glycolytic gene networks. Through histone [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] at [[HIF-1α]] target gene promoters, SIRT6 reduces the expression of glycolytic enzymes and limits glycolytic flux \[[^57]\]. Consistent with this function, SIRT6 deficiency leads to increased [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]] and metabolic imbalance, indicating that SIRT6 functions as an important negative regulator of glycolytic metabolism and glucose homoeostasis \[[^58]\].

Recent evidence also indicates that [[SIRT7]] participates in metabolic regulation. Although it has historically been less studied than other sirtuins, SIRT7 has been shown to regulate genes involved in glucose metabolism and energy homoeostasis in metabolically active tissues such as [[Liver|liver]] and [[Adipose Tissue]] \[[^59]\]. Through interactions with transcriptional regulators and chromatin‐associated proteins, SIRT7 contributes to the fine regulation of metabolic gene networks and may cooperate with other nuclear sirtuins, particularly SIRT1 and SIRT6, to maintain metabolic homoeostasis \[[^60]\].

Beyond nuclear sirtuins, mitochondrial sirtuins also contribute to the regulation of glucose metabolism by modulating mitochondrial enzyme activity and oxidative processes. [[SIRT3]], the primary mitochondrial [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]], regulates the [[Acetylation|acetylation]] status of several enzymes involved in energy metabolism and mitochondrial respiration. Through the [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, SIRT3 promotes [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]] and enhances mitochondrial function, supporting efficient utilisation of glucose‐derived substrates \[[^28], [^61]\].

Other mitochondrial sirtuins contribute to glucose‐related metabolic processes through additional mechanisms. [[SIRT4]], for example, regulates mitochondrial metabolic enzymes and plays a role in [[Insulin Secretion]] and energy metabolism \[[^62], [^63]\]. Similarly, [[SIRT5]] regulates metabolic enzyme activity through its [[Acetylation|desuccinylase]] and [[Acetylation|demalonylase]] activities, influencing metabolic pathways including [[Glycolysis]] and the [[TCA cycle|tricarboxylic acid cycle]] \[[^16]\].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that sirtuins regulate glucose metabolism at multiple levels, including transcriptional regulation of metabolic genes, modulation of signalling pathways, and post‐translational modification of metabolic enzymes. Through the coordinated actions of nuclear and mitochondrial sirtuins, these enzymes integrate nutrient sensing with cellular metabolic adaptation, thereby contributing to the maintenance of glucose homoeostasis under both physiological and metabolic stress conditions.

### 4.2. Lipid Metabolism

The regulation of [[Lipid Metabolism]] relies on a highly coordinated set of processes that balance fatty acid synthesis, lipid storage, and oxidation in accordance with cellular energy demands. Sirtuins contribute significantly to these processes by regulating transcription factors, metabolic enzymes, and mitochondrial activity, linking cellular energy balance to [[Lipid Metabolism]]. Because their enzymatic activity depends on intracellular [[NAD+]] availability, sirtuins function as metabolic sensors that adapt [[Lipid Metabolism]] to nutrient availability and metabolic stress \[[^20], [^64]\].

Within the sirtuin family, [[SIRT1]] plays a major role in [[Lipid Metabolism]] by regulating lipid homoeostasis, largely through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of transcriptional regulators involved in [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and lipid synthesis. In hepatic tissue, SIRT1 enhances the activity of [[PPARα|peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor alpha]] and its coactivator [[PGC-1α]], thereby promoting the expression of genes involved in mitochondrial fatty acid β‐oxidation \[[^65], [^66]\]. At the same time, SIRT1 suppresses [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] by inhibiting the activity of [[SREBP-1c|sterol regulatory element‐binding protein‐1c]], a key transcription factor responsible for the expression of enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis \[[^67]\]. Through these coordinated mechanisms, SIRT1 enhances lipid mobilisation and [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] during [[Fasting]], while restraining lipid accumulation under metabolic stress conditions \[[^68]\].

In [[Adipose Tissue]], SIRT1 also regulates [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]] and lipid storage. By repressing the activity of the nuclear receptor [[PPARγ]], a master regulator of [[Adipogenesis]], SIRT1 inhibits adipocyte maturation and reduces lipid accumulation in [[Adipose Tissue|white adipose tissue]] \[[^69]\]. This mechanism contributes to the regulation of adipose tissue expansion and [[Metabolic Flexibility]] during changes in energy availability.

Mitochondrial sirtuins contribute to [[Lipid Metabolism]] by regulating mitochondrial enzymes that control [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]]. Among them, [[SIRT3]] acts as the main mitochondrial [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]], promoting β‐oxidation and [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]] through the [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of key metabolic proteins. Through these actions, SIRT3 enhances [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and supports mitochondrial energy production, particularly in metabolically active tissues such as the [[Liver]], [[Skeletal Muscle]], and heart \[[^70], [^71], [^72]\].

In contrast, [[SIRT4]] exerts distinct regulatory functions in [[Lipid Metabolism]]. SIRT4 has been demonstrated to modulate mitochondrial metabolic enzymes and can inhibit [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] through the regulation of malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase activity, thereby influencing lipid storage and energy balance \[[^73]\]. In addition, SIRT4 participates in the control of [[Insulin Secretion]] and metabolic homoeostasis, further linking mitochondrial metabolism with systemic lipid and glucose regulation \[[^62]\].

[[SIRT5]] is another mitochondrial sirtuin implicated in [[Lipid Metabolism]], regulating metabolic enzyme activity through lysine [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]], [[Acetylation|demalonylation]], and [[Acetylation|deglutarylation]]. These post‐translational modifications affect key pathways such as [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], the [[TCA cycle|tricarboxylic acid cycle]], and mitochondrial respiration, thereby supporting lipid metabolic homoeostasis \[[^74], [^75]\].

Nuclear sirtuins also participate in lipid metabolic regulation. [[SIRT6]] has been shown to influence [[Lipid Metabolism]] by regulating gene expression involved in fatty acid metabolism and lipid homoeostasis \[[^58]\]. Similarly, [[SIRT7]] has recently been implicated in the control of [[Lipid Metabolism]] by modulating transcription factors and metabolic pathways that regulate [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]] and [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] in [[Adipose Tissue]] and [[Liver]] \[[^76]\].

Altogether, current data support a model in which nuclear and mitochondrial sirtuins act in concert to regulate [[Lipid Metabolism]]. Through transcriptional control of metabolic genes, post‐translational modification of metabolic enzymes, and modulation of mitochondrial function, sirtuins integrate cellular energy sensing with lipid metabolic adaptation. This multilayered regulatory network enables cells to adjust [[Lipid Metabolism]] in response to physiological conditions including [[Caloric Restriction]], [[Fasting]], and metabolic stress.

### 4.3. Adipose Tissue Biology

[[Adipose Tissue]] biology encompasses not only triglyceride storage in white adipose tissue (WAT) but also [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]], [[Lipolysis]], inflammatory remodelling, and adaptive [[Thermogenesis]] in brown and beige adipose depots. Within this framework, sirtuins have emerged as multilayer regulators that couple nutrient availability and [[Redox|redox]] state to adipose tissue plasticity. [[SIRT1]] is the best‐characterised anti‐adipogenic sirtuin in WAT, where it represses [[PPARγ]]‐dependent transcription by promoting the recruitment of the corepressors NCoR and SMRT to [[PPARγ]] target promoters, thereby limiting terminal [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]] and lipid storage. In mature adipocytes, SIRT1 also facilitates fasting‐induced lipid mobilisation, and reduced SIRT1 activity is associated with impaired [[Fatty acid|fatty acid]] release and greater fat accumulation \[[^77], [^78], [^79]\]. Beyond WAT, SIRT1 positively regulates [[Brown Adipose Tissue]] (BAT) activity and beige adipocyte induction; mechanistically, [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[PPARγ]] at K268/K293 promotes cofactor exchange within the [[PPARγ]] transcriptional complex and enhances WAT browning, whereas pharmacological or transgenic SIRT1 activation increases [[Thermogenesis]], [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], and [[PGC-1α]]‐dependent oxidative programs in BAT \[[^69], [^80], [^81]\]. [[SIRT2]] generally acts in parallel with SIRT1 as a brake on [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]]: it suppresses adipocyte differentiation through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[FoxO1]], which reinforces [[FoxO1]]‐mediated repression of [[PPARγ]], and it can also promote [[Lipolysis]] in differentiated adipocytes; notably, reduced SIRT1/SIRT2 expression in human visceral adipose stem cells from obese subjects correlates with enhanced adipogenic capacity and visceral fat expansion \[[^82], [^83], [^84]\]. [[SIRT3]], the major mitochondrial [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]], is especially relevant to BAT, where it supports mitochondrial oxidative capacity and adaptive [[Thermogenesis]]; early work established that SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial function and thermogenic competence in [[Brown Adipose Tissue|brown adipocytes]] \[[^85], [^86]\]. [[SIRT4]] generally promotes an energy‐conserving, pro‐adipogenic program; it enhances [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]] and [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] while repressing [[Fatty acid oxidation]] through inhibition of malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase \[[^87]\]. [[SIRT5]] contributes to both BAT function and white adipose tissue browning: SIRT5 deficiency impairs [[Fatty acid oxidation]] and reduces browning capacity in subcutaneous adipose tissue, while its inhibition paradoxically induces a brown fat‐like phenotype in certain preadipocyte models \[[^88], [^89]\]. [[SIRT6]] exerts broad anti‐adipogenic and pro‐thermogenic effects; it restrains [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]], promotes [[Lipolysis]], and supports thermogenic remodelling through regulation of [[PGC-1α]] and [[UCP1]] expression, with cold‐inducible SIRT6 expression linking environmental temperature to BAT and beige fat activation \[[^90], [^91], [^93], [^94], [^95]\]. In contrast, [[SIRT7]] generally promotes adipose tissue expansion and suppresses energy expenditure: it enhances [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]] and [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] partly through inhibition of SIRT1 and deacetylation of [[PPARγ]]2, and its deficiency increases [[Thermogenesis]] and energy dissipation in BAT \[[^76], [^98], [^99]\].

#> [!tip] SIRT3 is the principal mitochondrial deacetylase
> [[SIRT3]] [[Deacetylation|deacetylates]] a broad set of matrix enzymes ([[Pyruvate Dehydrogenase|PDH]], [[IDH2]], [[MnSOD]], and [[Fatty acid oxidation]] enzymes), coordinating substrate oxidation, ROS defense, and [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], whereas [[SIRT4]] and [[SIRT5]] counterbalance via [[ADP-ribosylation]] and [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]].

## 4.4. Mitochondrial Metabolism

[[Mitochondria]] represent central nodes in cellular metabolism, integrating nutrient signals with [[Adenosine Triphosphate|ATP]] generation, [[Redox Homeostasis|redox balance]], biosynthetic activity, and stress‐adaptive responses. Accordingly, mitochondrial metabolism depends on the coordinated regulation of substrate oxidation, [[TCA cycle|tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle]] activity, [[Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS)]], [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], and [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] detoxification. Sirtuins are deeply involved in this network because their catalytic activity is directly linked to [[NAD+]] availability, allowing them to function as metabolic regulators that adapt mitochondrial function to energetic stress, [[Fasting]], and nutrient excess \[[^53], [^103], [^104], [^105]\]. Although the mitochondrial sirtuins [[SIRT3]], [[SIRT4]], and [[SIRT5]] are the most direct regulators of mitochondrial metabolism, nuclear sirtuins such as [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], and [[SIRT7]] also influence mitochondrial homoeostasis by controlling [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], transcriptional programs, and fuel selection.

Among all family members, [[SIRT3]] is widely regarded as the principal mitochondrial [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]] and a major regulator of mitochondrial metabolic efficiency. SIRT3 [[Deacetylation|deacetylates]] a broad set of mitochondrial enzymes involved in fatty acid β‐oxidation, the [[TCA cycle]], [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], and respiratory chain activity, thereby promoting [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]] and preserving [[Metabolic Flexibility]] \[[^106], [^107], [^108], [^109]\]. In particular, fasting‐induced SIRT3 activation enhances [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD), while also supporting ketone body production via activation of [[HMGCS2|3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA synthase 2]] \[[^28], [^106]\]. SIRT3 also contributes to the coupling of [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]]‐derived carbon to mitochondrial oxidation by maintaining [[Pyruvate Dehydrogenase]] ([[Pyruvate Dehydrogenase|PDH]]) activity; loss of SIRT3 leads to PDH hyperacetylation, reduced glucose oxidation, pyruvate/lactate accumulation, and impaired [[Metabolic Flexibility]] in [[Skeletal Muscle]] \[[^61]\]. Beyond substrate oxidation, SIRT3 is crucial for mitochondrial [[Redox Homeostasis|redox homoeostasis]]. It activates antioxidant defences through the [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[MnSOD]] and [[FOXO3a]]‐dependent pathways, thereby limiting [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] accumulation, preserving mitochondrial DNA integrity, and protecting [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|OXPHOS]] function under metabolic stress \[[^110], [^111], [^112]\]. Overall, SIRT3 coordinates mitochondrial substrate use, respiratory competence, and oxidative stress resistance.

In contrast to SIRT3, [[SIRT4]] generally exerts an inhibitory effect on catabolic mitochondrial metabolism, promoting the preservation of energy substrates. Originally described as a mitochondrial [[ADP-ribosylation|ADP‐ribosyltransferase]], SIRT4 suppresses [[Glutamate Dehydrogenase (GDH)|glutamate dehydrogenase]] activity, thereby reducing amino acid–induced [[Insulin Secretion]] in pancreatic β‐cells \[[^62], [^113]\]. Subsequent studies expanded its role, showing that SIRT4 suppresses [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and modulates mitochondrial gene expression in [[Liver]] and muscle \[[^114]\]. In hepatocytes, SIRT4 represses [[PPARα]] signalling and inhibits malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase, shifting metabolism away from mitochondrial fatty acid utilisation and toward anabolic lipid conservation \[[^73], [^115]\]. Thus, whereas SIRT3 typically promotes mitochondrial oxidative output during [[Fasting]], SIRT4 frequently counterbalances this response by damping mitochondrial substrate oxidation. This functional opposition between SIRT3 and SIRT4 is particularly relevant in tissues that continuously adapt between fed and fasting states.

[[SIRT5]] adds an additional layer of control over mitochondrial metabolism through distinct enzymatic functions. Rather than acting mainly as a [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]], SIRT5 preferentially removes negatively charged acyl modifications, especially succinyl, malonyl, and glutaryl groups, from mitochondrial metabolic enzymes \[[^116]\]. This is highly relevant because [[Acetylation|succinylation]] and related acylations are abundant in the mitochondrial matrix and can strongly alter enzyme activity. SIRT5 regulates multiple pathways, including [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], [[Glycolysis]], the [[TCA cycle]], [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], the [[Electron Transport Chain]], and ammonia detoxification \[[^104], [^117], [^118], [^119], [^120]\]. In [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], SIRT5 activates enzymes such as very‐long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (VLCAD), long‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD), medium‐chain acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD), and the enoyl‐CoA hydratase α‐subunit (ECHA) through [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]]. Deficiency of SIRT5 compromises fatty acid metabolism, leading to acylcarnitine accumulation and decreased [[Adenosine Triphosphate|ATP]] production, particularly under metabolic challenges such as [[Fasting]] or exercise \[[^118], [^121], [^122], [^123]\]. SIRT5 also activates [[HMGCS2|3‐Hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA synthase 2]], thereby linking mitochondrial acyl‐remodelling to ketone body synthesis \[[^17]\]. In addition, it modulates components of the [[TCA cycle]] and the respiratory chain, indicating that SIRT5 is not simply a fine‐tuner of one pathway, but rather a broad regulator of mitochondrial carbon flux and respiration \[[^117]\]. Together, SIRT3 and SIRT5 cooperate to maintain efficient mitochondrial oxidation, although through distinct post‐translational mechanisms: [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] in the case of SIRT3, and [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]]/[[Acetylation|demalonylation]] in the case of SIRT5.

Although [[SIRT1]] is not a mitochondrial sirtuin, it exerts major upstream control over mitochondrial metabolism. Through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[PGC-1α]], SIRT1 induces transcriptional programs that enhance [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]], and [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] in the [[Liver]] and [[Skeletal Muscle]] \[[^80]\]. Its reciprocal functional loop with [[AMPK]] amplifies this effect: [[AMPK]] increases [[NAD+]] biosynthesis and SIRT1 activity, whereas SIRT1 activates [[LKB1]]/[[AMPK]] signalling, together driving [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]] and oxidative adaptation under low‐energy conditions \[[^56], [^124], [^125], [^126], [^127]\]. Thus, SIRT1 acts as a nuclear coordinator that reshapes mitochondrial capacity at the transcriptional level rather than by directly modifying mitochondrial matrix enzymes.

The contribution of [[SIRT6]] to mitochondrial metabolism is also largely indirect but metabolically important. By repressing [[HIF-1α]]‐dependent glycolytic gene expression, SIRT6 limits excessive glycolytic reliance and promotes a more oxidative metabolic phenotype \[[^57]\]. In this context, SIRT6 helps to preserve the balance between cytosolic [[Glycolysis|glucose metabolism]] and mitochondrial oxidation, opposing the shift toward glycolytic reprogramming that often accompanies metabolic disease. Although its actions are mainly chromatin‐based, the downstream consequence is a more efficient allocation of substrates toward mitochondrial catabolism rather than aerobic glycolysis.

[[SIRT7]] has emerged as an important nuclear regulator of mitochondrial homoeostasis. It promotes the expression of nuclear‐encoded mitochondrial genes by [[Deacetylation|deacetylating]] [[GABPβ1|GA‐binding protein β1]], facilitating GABP complex formation and transcriptional activation. Deficiency of SIRT7 is associated with systemic [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], including increased [[Lactate|lactate levels]], impaired exercise performance, cardiac abnormalities, and hepatic microvesicular steatosis, indicative of compromised mitochondrial homoeostasis \[[^128]\].

Taken together, these findings indicate that sirtuins regulate mitochondrial metabolism at multiple levels. [[SIRT3]], [[SIRT4]], and [[SIRT5]] directly remodel the activity of mitochondrial enzymes controlling substrate entry, β‐oxidation, [[TCA cycle]] flux, [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], respiratory chain function, and [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] detoxification, whereas [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], and [[SIRT7]] govern transcriptional and epigenetic programs that determine [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], substrate preference, and overall oxidative capacity. Therefore, the sirtuin network acts not as a set of isolated regulators, but as an integrated system that adapts mitochondrial metabolism to nutrient availability and metabolic stress.

### 4.5. Nutrient Sensing and Metabolic Stress Response

Cells continuously adjust their metabolic programs in response to changes in nutrient availability, energy requirements, and environmental stress. These adaptive responses rely on molecular nutrient‐sensing pathways that integrate intracellular energy status with transcriptional, metabolic, and mitochondrial regulation. Among the key components of this system, sirtuins function as metabolic sensors that translate changes in nutrient availability into coordinated metabolic responses. Because their enzymatic activity depends on intracellular [[NAD+]], sirtuins are uniquely positioned to couple the cellular [[Redox|redox]] state and energy balance to metabolic regulation.

During conditions of nutrient shortage, such as [[Fasting]] or [[Caloric Restriction]], intracellular [[NAD+]] levels increase due to enhanced activity of [[NAD+ Biosynthesis|NAD+ biosynthetic pathways]]. This elevation in [[NAD+]] availability activates several sirtuin family members, particularly [[SIRT1]] and [[SIRT3]], which orchestrate metabolic adaptations aimed at maintaining energy homoeostasis. For example, SIRT1 plays a central role in mediating the metabolic response to [[Caloric Restriction]] by regulating transcription factors and coactivators involved in energy metabolism, including [[PGC-1α]], [[FOXO]] proteins, and nuclear receptors controlling [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and [[Gluconeogenesis]] \[[^53]\]. Through these interactions, SIRT1 promotes [[Metabolic Flexibility]], allowing cells to shift from glucose utilisation toward increased [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and mitochondrial respiration during energy deprivation \[[^10]\].

The activity of sirtuins is tightly interconnected with other nutrient‐sensing signalling pathways, most notably [[AMPK]], and the [[mTOR|mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)]]. [[AMPK]] acts as a cellular energy sensor that becomes activated under conditions of low [[Adenosine Triphosphate|ATP]] levels, such as metabolic stress or exercise \[[^129]\]. Activation of [[AMPK]] increases [[NAD+]] production through stimulation of [[NAMPT]], thereby indirectly enhancing SIRT1 activity \[[^56]\]. Conversely, SIRT1 can activate [[AMPK]] through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[LKB1]], establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies metabolic adaptation during energy stress \[[^126], [^127]\]. In contrast, nutrient abundance activates [[mTOR]] signalling, which promotes anabolic processes and suppresses [[Autophagy|autophagy]] \[[^130]\]. Sirtuins counterbalance this anabolic state by promoting catabolic pathways that restore metabolic equilibrium.

Mitochondrial sirtuins also play a fundamental role in metabolic stress responses. [[SIRT3]] is strongly induced during [[Fasting]] and [[Caloric Restriction]], where it enhances mitochondrial [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]] and protects cells from oxidative stress. By [[Deacetylation|deacetylating]] metabolic enzymes involved in [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], the [[TCA cycle]], and antioxidant defence systems such as [[MnSOD]], SIRT3 improves mitochondrial efficiency and limits [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] accumulation under metabolic stress \[[^30]\]. This adaptive response is particularly important in highly oxidative tissues, that is, [[Liver]], [[Skeletal Muscle]], and [[Brown Adipose Tissue]].

Other mitochondrial sirtuins contribute to the fine regulation of metabolic stress responses. [[SIRT4]], for example, participates in nutrient sensing by modulating mitochondrial metabolic enzymes and regulating [[Insulin Secretion]] in pancreatic β‐cells \[[^62], [^73]\]. [[SIRT5]], through its [[Acetylation|desuccinylase]] and [[Acetylation|demalonylase]] activities, regulates metabolic enzymes involved in central carbon metabolism and ammonia detoxification, allowing mitochondria to adapt to changes in metabolic substrate availability \[[^117]\]. These post‐translational modifications provide a rapid mechanism for adjusting mitochondrial metabolic flux in response to nutrient fluctuations.

Nuclear sirtuins play a role in modulating cellular responses to metabolic stress conditions. [[SIRT6]], for example, regulates glucose metabolism and inflammatory responses through chromatin‐based mechanisms and contributes to metabolic adaptation by repressing glycolytic gene expression under conditions of energy stress \[[^131]\]. Similarly, [[SIRT7]] has been implicated in the regulation of metabolic gene expression and mitochondrial homoeostasis during nutrient stress by controlling transcriptional programs involved in energy metabolism. In addition to metabolic regulation, sirtuins are also key mediators of cellular stress resistance. During metabolic stress conditions such as [[DNA Damage]], nutrient deprivation, or [[Oxidative Stress]], sirtuins coordinate protective responses that include enhanced mitochondrial function, activation of antioxidant defences, and modulation of inflammatory signalling pathways. Through these mechanisms, sirtuins contribute not only to metabolic adaptation but also to cellular survival and organismal resilience during environmental stress \[[^132]\].

Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of sirtuins as integrators of nutrient sensing and metabolic stress responses. By linking [[NAD+]] metabolism with mitochondrial function, transcriptional regulation, and cellular signalling pathways, the sirtuin network enables cells to dynamically adapt their metabolism in response to changing nutritional and environmental conditions.

## 5\. Sirtuins in Metabolic Disorders

Metabolic disorders arise when the adaptive networks coordinating nutrient sensing, substrate utilisation, mitochondrial function, and [[Inflammation]] become chronically dysregulated. Given their [[NAD+]]‐dependence and their ability to regulate transcription factors, chromatin and metabolic enzymes, sirtuins are now recognised as central modifiers of the transition from metabolic stress to overt disease. Across [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]], [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]], [[Obesity]], metabolic associated steatotic liver disease ([[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|MASLD]]), and cardiometabolic complications, changes in sirtuin expression or activity influence disease severity by modulating lipid storage, glucose handling, mitochondrial efficiency, [[Oxidative Stress]], and inflammatory signalling \[[^133], [^134], [^135]\]. Although mammalian sirtuins do not act uniformly, a recurrent pattern has emerged where [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT3]], and [[SIRT6]] generally preserve [[Metabolic Flexibility]] and protect against disease progression, whereas [[SIRT7]] frequently acts in the opposite direction in [[Adipose Tissue]] and [[Liver]]. [[SIRT4]] and [[SIRT5]] display more context‐dependent effects linked to mitochondrial substrate selection and post‐translational remodelling of metabolic enzymes.

### 5.1. Obesity and Adipose Tissue Dysfunction

Beyond the accumulation of excess fat, [[Obesity]] is associated with marked alterations in the structure and function of [[Adipose Tissue]], a process referred to as adipose tissue remodelling. This remodelling involves adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia, dysregulated lipid mobilisation, [[ECM|extracellular matrix]] deposition, impaired [[Angiogenesis]], immune cell infiltration, and chronic low‐grade [[Inflammation]], all of which contribute to systemic [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]] and metabolic dysfunction \[[^136], [^137]\]. Within this context, sirtuins have emerged as critical regulators of adipose tissue plasticity, orchestrating multiple aspects of adipocyte biology, including white and brown adipocyte differentiation, lipid turnover, mitochondrial function, inflammatory responses, [[Cardiac Fibrosis|fibrosis]], and thermogenic activity.

Under physiological conditions, [[Adipose Tissue]] exhibits a remarkable degree of plasticity, allowing it to expand through hyperplasia while maintaining [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and metabolic homoeostasis. In contrast, [[Obesity]] is predominantly associated with adipocyte hypertrophy, which leads to cellular stress, [[Hypoxia]], and mechanical strain within the tissue. These alterations trigger a cascade of altered responses, including [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], increased production of [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]], and activation of stress signalling pathways, ultimately impairing adipocyte function and systemic metabolic regulation \[[^138]\]. A key feature of dysfunctional adipose tissue is the disruption of lipid handling and [[Metabolic Flexibility]]. In the obesity conditions, adipocytes display impaired capacity to properly store and mobilise lipids, resulting in relatively elevated basal [[Lipolysis]] and increased release of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the circulation. This [[Lipotoxicity|ectopic lipid overflow]] contributes to lipid accumulation in non‐adipose tissues such as [[Liver]] and [[Skeletal Muscle]], thereby promoting [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]] and [[Lipotoxicity]] \[[^139], [^140], [^141], [^142]\]. At the molecular level, these processes are tightly linked to alterations in [[NAD+]] availability and mitochondrial function, which directly impact the activity of metabolic sensors such as sirtuins \[[^139], [^140]\]. At the same time, [[Obesity]] induces a profound reprogramming of the adipose tissue immune microenvironment. Hypertrophic adipocytes secrete chemokines and danger‐associated signals that promote the recruitment of immune cells, particularly [[Macrophages]]. The proportion of macrophages within adipose tissue increases dramatically during obesity, accompanied by a phenotypic shift from anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages towards pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages \[[^143], [^144], [^145]\]. These immune cells form characteristic crown‐like structures around dead or dying adipocytes and secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including [[TNFα|TNF‐α]], [[IL-1β]], and [[IL-6]], which directly impair [[Insulin Signaling|insulin signalling]] through activation of [[NF-κB]], [[JNK]], and [[IKKbeta|IKKβ]] pathways and increased serine phosphorylation of [[IRS1|insulin receptor substrate proteins]] \[[^146], [^147]\].

Another fundamental aspect of adipose tissue dysfunction in [[Obesity]] is the impairment of thermogenic capacity and adipose tissue plasticity. [[Brown Adipose Tissue|Brown and beige adipocytes]] play a crucial role in energy expenditure through mitochondrial uncoupling and heat production. However, obesity is often associated with reduced browning of white adipose tissue and diminished [[Brown Adipose Tissue]] activity, resulting in decreased energy dissipation and further weight gain. This loss of thermogenic potential is closely linked to [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]] and altered transcriptional regulation of key factors such as [[PGC-1α]] and [[UCP1]] \[[^148]\].

At the molecular level, these diverse pathological processes converge on alterations in nutrient‐sensing and energy‐regulating pathways, among which sirtuins represent central hubs. As [[NAD+]]‐dependent enzymes, sirtuins function as metabolic sensors that couple cellular energy status to transcriptional and enzymatic programs controlling [[Adipogenesis]], [[Lipid Metabolism]], mitochondrial function, [[Inflammation]], and stress responses. Importantly, [[Obesity]] is characterised by reduced [[NAD+]] availability in adipose tissue, leading to impaired sirtuin activity and disruption of metabolic homoeostasis \[[^139], [^140]\].

Among the sirtuin family, [[SIRT1]] plays a central protective role in adipose tissue. SIRT1 suppresses [[Adipogenesis]] by repressing [[PPARγ]] activity and promotes [[Lipolysis]] through activation of [[FoxO1]]‐dependent [[ATGL]] expression and [[AMPK]] signalling. In addition, SIRT1 exerts strong anti‐inflammatory effects by inhibiting [[NF-κB]] signalling, reducing macrophage infiltration, and promoting polarisation toward an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype \[[^81], [^149], [^150], [^151], [^152]\]. Importantly, SIRT1 also contributes to adipose tissue remodelling by limiting [[ECM|extracellular matrix]] deposition and [[Cardiac Fibrosis|fibrosis]] and by promoting white adipose tissue browning through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[PPARγ]] and activation of thermogenic programs, including [[PGC-1α]] and [[FGF21]] signalling \[[^153]\]. Interestingly, SIRT1‐mediated [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[PPARγ]] has been shown to induce BAT genes and repress WAT genes \[[^69]\]. However, its role may be context‐dependent, as stage‐specific and tissue‐specific effects have been described during obesity progression \[[^154]\]. [[SIRT2]] shares some functional overlap with SIRT1, particularly in the inhibition of [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]]. By [[Deacetylation|deacetylating]] [[FoxO1]], SIRT2 enhances [[FoxO1]]‐mediated repression of [[PPARγ]], thereby limiting [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]] and lipid accumulation. In addition, SIRT2 promotes [[Lipolysis]] and [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], suggesting a role in maintaining adipose tissue [[Metabolic Flexibility]] \[[^83]\]. Mitochondrial sirtuins are particularly important for adipose tissue metabolic health. [[SIRT3]] promotes brown adipocyte differentiation and thermogenic competence through [[PGC-1α]] activation and maintenance of mitochondrial function. Loss of SIRT3 leads to increased lipid accumulation, impaired [[Thermogenesis]], and enhanced inflammatory responses, partly through activation of the [[NLRP3]] inflammasome \[[^155], [^156]\].

Similarly, [[SIRT5]] contributes to adipose tissue remodelling by regulating mitochondrial metabolism and thermogenic gene expression. SIRT5 deficiency impairs [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and reduces browning capacity in subcutaneous adipose tissue, highlighting its role in energy expenditure and metabolic adaptation \[[^88], [^157]\].

In contrast, [[SIRT4]] generally promotes an energy‐conserving phenotype in adipose tissue. SIRT4 enhances [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]] and [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] while repressing [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] through inhibition of malonyl‐CoA decarboxylase, thereby favouring lipid accumulation under nutrient‐rich conditions \[[^87], [^158]\].

[[SIRT6]] exerts predominantly protective effects in adipose tissue by inhibiting [[Adipogenesis|adipocyte differentiation]], promoting [[Lipolysis]], and suppressing inflammatory signalling pathways such as [[NF-κB]]. In addition, SIRT6 supports thermogenic remodelling and browning of adipose tissue, partly through regulation of [[PGC-1α]] and [[UCP1]] expression. Loss of SIRT6 leads to adipocyte hypertrophy, reduced lipolytic activity, and increased [[Inflammation]], thereby exacerbating obesity‐related metabolic dysfunction \[[^93], [^159], [^160]\].

In contrast to SIRT1 and SIRT6, [[SIRT7]] appears to promote adipose tissue expansion and dysfunction. SIRT7 enhances [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]] and [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]], partly through inhibition of SIRT1 activity and activation of [[PPARγ]]‐dependent transcriptional programs. Moreover, SIRT7 has been associated with impaired [[Thermogenesis]] and increased adipose tissue [[Inflammation]], suggesting a role in promoting [[Obesity]] and metabolic disease \[[^76]\].

Beyond adipocyte‐intrinsic mechanisms, sirtuins also regulate the immune and structural components of adipose tissue remodelling. Chronic overnutrition induces macrophage infiltration and a shift toward pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages, which secrete cytokines such as [[TNFα|TNF‐α]] and [[IL-1β]], driving [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]]. [[SIRT1]] and [[SIRT6]] counteract this process by suppressing inflammatory signalling pathways and promoting anti‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation, whereas dysregulation of sirtuin activity contributes to sustained [[Inflammation]] and metabolic dysfunction \[[^161]\].

In addition, sirtuins influence [[ECM|extracellular matrix]] remodelling and [[Cardiac Fibrosis|fibrosis]], which are increasingly recognised as key determinants of adipose tissue dysfunction. Excessive [[ECM]] deposition restricts adipose tissue expandability, promotes adipocyte stress and death, and exacerbates [[Inflammation]]. SIRT1 has been shown to regulate [[Collagen]] expression and matrix remodelling, thereby preserving tissue flexibility and metabolic function \[[^162]\].

Human studies provide an important translational counterpart to experimental evidence on SIRT1 in metabolic regulation. Circulating SIRT1, measured in serum, plasma, or whole blood, has been investigated as a potential marker of metabolic health across conditions characterised by excess or reduced energy availability. In subjects with [[Obesity]], circulating SIRT1 has been associated with [[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|MASLD]] and liver fat content, visceral and epicardial adiposity, [[Body Mass Index|BMI]], waist circumference, fat mass, [[Insulin]] levels, [[LDL|LDL cholesterol]], and inflammatory indices \[[^163], [^164], [^165], [^166]\]. Consistently, SIRT1 levels have been reported to increase following intragastric balloon–induced weight loss and intermittent [[Caloric Restriction]] in selected cohorts, supporting its sensitivity to energy balance and metabolic adaptation \[[^167], [^168]\]. Across the underweight—normal weight—obesity spectrum, circulating SIRT1 appears to be highest in anorexia nervosa and lowest in obesity, and shows consistent relationships with [[Leptin]] and [[Adiponectin]], reinforcing its association with adipose tissue distribution and systemic energy availability \[[^165], [^169]\]. Additional clinical evidence links circulating SIRT1 to bone metabolism through its association with sclerostin and bone status in women with different degrees of adiposity \[[^170]\]. Exercise studies further indicate that SIRT1 can be modulated by both acute and chronic [[Exercise]] interventions, although the direction and magnitude of the response depend on tissue source, assay type, and exercise protocol \[[^171]\]. Moreover, evidence from lipodystrophic syndromes and from matched analyses of circulating and subcutaneous adipose tissue SIRT1 protein levels in human obesity suggests that circulating SIRT1 may reflect adipose tissue distribution, adipose tissue quality, and tissue SIRT1 expression rather than adiposity alone \[[^172], [^173]\]. Overall, these data suggest that circulating SIRT1 may represent an accessible associative biomarker of adipose tissue metabolic quality and systemic energy availability, although its interpretation requires careful consideration of the compartmental and methodological factors that influence its measurement.

Collectively, these findings indicate that sirtuins orchestrate adipose tissue remodelling through coordinated regulation of [[Adipogenesis]], [[Lipolysis]], mitochondrial function, [[Inflammation]], [[Cardiac Fibrosis|fibrosis]], and [[Thermogenesis]]. In [[Obesity]], disruption of this network characterised by reduced activity of protective sirtuins such as [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT3]], and [[SIRT6]] and relative activation of [[SIRT4]] and [[SIRT7]] pathways contributes to adipose tissue dysfunction and the development of systemic metabolic disease.

### 5.2. Insulin Resistance and T2DM

[[Insulin Sensitivity|Insulin resistance]] and [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus|T2DM]] develop through progressive impairment of [[Insulin Signaling|insulin signalling]] in [[Liver]], [[Skeletal Muscle]], [[Adipose Tissue]], and pancreatic β‐cells, while sirtuins participate in each of these nodes. [[SIRT1]] improves [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin sensitivity]] by enhancing [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], supporting [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], suppressing inflammatory signalling, and coordinating hepatic glucose production with [[Fasting]] responses. SIRT1 functions as a central metabolic sensor that exerts predominantly protective effects against [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus|type 2 diabetes]] through coordinated, [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]]‐dependent regulation of key signalling and transcriptional networks. Mechanistically, in pancreatic β‐cells SIRT1 enhances glucose‐stimulated [[Insulin Secretion]] by repressing [[UCP2]], thereby increasing mitochondrial [[Adenosine Triphosphate|ATP]] production and promoting [[Calcium|Ca<sup>2+</sup>]]‐dependent insulin exocytosis, while also preserving β‐cell survival via [[FoxO1]]‐mediated activation of insulin gene regulators and suppression of [[NF-κB]]–driven inflammatory and [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]] pathways \[[^176], [^177]\]. In peripheral tissues, SIRT1 improves [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin sensitivity]] by directly modulating [[Insulin Signaling|insulin signalling]]: it represses the negative regulator [[PTP1B]] and [[Deacetylation|deacetylates]] [[IRS-2]], enabling efficient tyrosine phosphorylation and downstream [[PI3K]]–[[Akt]] activation \[[^178], [^179]\]. Concurrently, SIRT1 attenuates chronic [[Inflammation]], which is a key driver of [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]] through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of the [[NF-κB]] p65 subunit, thereby reducing proinflammatory cytokine transcription in [[Adipose Tissue]] and [[Macrophages]] \[[^180], [^181]\]. At the level of energy metabolism, SIRT1 activates [[PGC-1α]] and [[PPARα]] to promote [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]] and [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], reducing [[Lipotoxicity|ectopic lipid accumulation]] and improving [[Metabolic Flexibility]] in muscle and liver \[[^80], [^182]\]. In hepatocytes, SIRT1 integrates [[Fasting]] signals by dynamically regulating [[Gluconeogenesis]] through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[CRTC2]], [[FoxO1]], and [[PGC-1α]], while also modulating [[STAT3]] activity, highlighting its role as a nutrient‐responsive rheostat of hepatic glucose output \[[^183], [^184], [^185]\].

[[SIRT6]] also exerts antidiabetic effects by repressing excessive [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]], modulating hepatic and adipose [[Inflammation]], and helping preserve whole‐body glucose homoeostasis \[[^186], [^187]\]. Mechanistically, SIRT6 suppresses [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]] by inhibiting [[HIF-1α|Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha]]–dependent transcription of glycolytic genes (e.g., GLUT1, LDH, PFK1), shifting cellular metabolism towards mitochondrial [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]]; loss of SIRT6 leads to [[HIF-1α]] activation, enhanced [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]], and increased glucose uptake that contributes to systemic metabolic imbalance \[[^57], [^188]\]. In parallel, SIRT6 restrains hepatic [[Gluconeogenesis]] through dual epigenetic mechanisms: it activates the acetyltransferase GCN5 to increase [[Acetylation|acetylation]] and inhibition of [[PGC-1α]], and directly interacts with [[FoxO1]] to reduce its promoter binding at G6pc and Pepck, thereby limiting glucose production \[[^189], [^190]\]. Regarding [[Insulin Signaling|insulin signalling]], SIRT6 negatively regulates [[Akt]] phosphorylation by suppressing upstream [[Insulin Signaling|insulin receptor]]/[[IRS1|IRS]] signalling, thereby preventing excessive glucose uptake and maintaining metabolic balance \[[^191]\]. In pancreatic β‐cells, SIRT6 preserves glucose‐stimulated [[Insulin Secretion]] and cell survival by maintaining mitochondrial function and repressing [[TXNIP]] expression via histone [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] (H3K9/H3K56), linking the chromatin state to insulin secretory capacity \[[^192], [^193]\]. Beyond glucose metabolism, SIRT6 improves systemic [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin sensitivity]] by inhibiting [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] (via repression of [[SREBP-1c|SREBP1]]/2), promoting fatty acid β‐oxidation, and enhancing adipose [[Lipolysis]] through [[FoxO1]]‐dependent induction of adipose triglyceride lipase ([[ATGL]]), thereby preventing [[Lipotoxicity|ectopic lipid accumulation]] and lipotoxic [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]] \[[^93]\]. Additionally, SIRT6 decreases chronic [[Inflammation]], which is a key driver of [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]], through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of histone H3K9 at [[NF-κB]] target gene promoters, suppressing proinflammatory transcriptional programs in metabolic tissues \[[^96]\]. Collectively, these findings establish SIRT6 as a pleiotropic regulator of glucose, lipid, and inflammatory pathways whose coordinated modulation underlies its potent antidiabetic effects.

Mitochondrial sirtuins additionally shape β‐cell and substrate‐specific responses relevant to diabetes. In mitochondria, [[SIRT3]] supports insulin responsiveness by maintaining [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]], limiting [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] accumulation, and preserving [[Metabolic Flexibility]]; conversely, reduced SIRT3 activity is associated with [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], [[Lipotoxicity|ectopic lipid accumulation]], and impaired [[Insulin Signaling|insulin signalling]] \[[^61], [^194]\]. [[SIRT2]] appears more complex: in adipose precursors it inhibits [[Adipogenesis|adipogenesis]], yet in pancreatic β‐cells it has been implicated in adaptive β‐cell proliferation and metabolic control, indicating that its contribution is highly tissue‐dependent \[[^84], [^133], [^195]\]. [[SIRT4]] inhibits [[Glutamate Dehydrogenase (GDH)|glutamate dehydrogenase]] and modulates [[Insulin Secretion]], thereby linking mitochondrial amino acid sensing to endocrine control. Depending on the context, this may be metabolically adaptive or may contribute to impaired flexibility \[[^62]\]. [[SIRT5]], through [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]] and [[Acetylation|demalonylation]] of metabolic enzymes, influences [[Glycolysis]], the [[TCA cycle]], and mitochondrial respiration, suggesting that altered SIRT5 activity may affect insulin‐sensitive tissues by changing central carbon metabolism and [[Redox Homeostasis|redox balance]] \[[^74], [^104]\].

Overall, [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]] and [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus|T2DM]] can be viewed, at least in part, as disorders of defective sirtuin‐mediated coordination between nutrient sensing, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory restraint \[[^133]\].

### 5.3. Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Fatty Liver Disease and Steatohepatitis

In [[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|MASLD]], impaired [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], enhanced de novo [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]], [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], [[Oxidative Stress]], and inflammatory signalling drive the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis and [[Cardiac Fibrosis|fibrosis]] \[[^196]\].

Mechanistically, nuclear [[SIRT1]] acts as a master metabolic regulator by [[Deacetylation|deacetylating]] [[PGC-1α]] to drive [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]] and fatty acid β‐oxidation, while simultaneously suppressing [[Lipid Synthesis|lipogenesis]] through inhibition of [[SREBP-1c]] and activation of [[AMPK]] signalling, thereby reducing hepatic triglyceride accumulation and restoring [[Metabolic Flexibility]] \[[^65]\]. In parallel, mitochondrial sirtuins, particularly [[SIRT3]], directly reprogramme mitochondrial metabolism through lysine [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of key enzymes, enhancing [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and antioxidant defences; for instance, SIRT3‐mediated [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[MnSOD]] increases its enzymatic activity, lowering mitochondrial [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] and preventing oxidative damage, whereas SIRT3 deficiency leads to impaired [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]], increased ROS accumulation, and exacerbated steatosis and [[Inflammation]] \[[^28], [^110], [^197]\].

[[SIRT5]] complements these effects by regulating metabolic flux through lysine [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]] and [[Acetylation|demalonylation]] of enzymes involved in β‐oxidation and [[HMGCS2|ketogenesis]], thereby sustaining mitochondrial energy production and limiting lipid accumulation \[[^17], [^198]\]. In contrast, [[SIRT4]] exerts context‐dependent and often opposing effects by inhibiting [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] via repression of [[PPARα]] and destabilisation of [[Mitochondrial Trifunctional Protein α|MTPα]], promoting lipid storage and potentially accelerating [[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|MASLD]] progression under nutrient‐rich conditions \[[^115]\]. Beyond mitochondrial enzymes, chromatin‐associated sirtuins such as [[SIRT6]] and [[SIRT7]] regulate hepatic metabolism at the transcriptional and epigenetic levels. SIRT6 suppresses [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]] and triglyceride synthesis through histone H3 [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] and alleviates [[Endoplasmic Reticulum]] stress via regulation of [[XBP1]], while also promoting [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and reducing inflammatory cytokine production \[[^135]\]. SIRT7, in turn, modulates [[Lipid Metabolism]] and stress responses by controlling transcriptional programs linked to [[Ribosome Biogenesis|ribosomal biogenesis]], [[c-Myc|Myc]] signalling, and nuclear receptor pathways that influence triglyceride synthesis and fatty acid uptake \[[^199]\].

Collectively, reduced expression or activity of most sirtuins in [[Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease|MASLD]] leads to hyperacetylation of mitochondrial and nuclear proteins, impaired [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]], excessive [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] generation, and dysregulated lipid handling, establishing a feed‐forward cycle of steatosis, [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]], [[Inflammation]], and hepatocellular injury that drives progression from simple fatty liver to MASLD and [[Cardiac Fibrosis|fibrosis]].

### 5.4. Metabolic Syndrome and Cardiometabolic Complications

Sirtuins act as central integrators of cellular energy status and stress responses in [[Metabolic Syndrome|metabolic syndromes]], extending their influence beyond classical metabolic tissues to orchestrate cardiometabolic homoeostasis through coordinated epigenetic and post‐translational mechanisms. Among these, [[SIRT1]] functions as a [[NAD+]]‐dependent [[Deacetylation|deacetylase]] linking the [[Redox|redox]] state to vascular signalling, particularly via modulation of [[eNOS]] activity, with consequent effects on [[Endothelial Dysfunction|endothelial function]] and vascular remodelling \[[^200], [^201]\]. Mechanistically, SIRT1 activity is tightly coupled to intracellular [[NAD+]] availability, positioning it as a metabolic sensor that translates fluctuations in nutrient flux into adaptive transcriptional programs; this is particularly relevant in [[Metabolic Syndrome]], where altered [[NAD+]]/[[NADH]] ratios and impaired [[NAD+ Salvage Pathway|salvage pathways]] constrain SIRT1 catalytic efficiency and downstream signalling. In the vasculature, SIRT1 exerts pleiotropic protective effects by [[Deacetylation|deacetylating]] [[eNOS]] to enhance [[Nitric Oxide]] bioavailability, while simultaneously repressing [[NF-κB]]–dependent transcription of adhesion molecules and cytokines, thereby attenuating endothelial activation and vascular [[Inflammation]], which are key initiating events in [[Atherosclerosis|atherogenesis]] \[[^202]\]. In addition, SIRT1‐mediated [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[FoxO1]] and [[FOXO3a]] shifts their transcriptional output towards stress resistance and cell‐cycle arrest rather than [[Apoptosis]], promoting endothelial cell survival under [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative]] and glucolipotoxic stress conditions characteristic of [[Metabolic Syndrome]] \[[^203], [^204]\]. Importantly, SIRT1 also regulates endothelial progenitor cell function and senescence, processes that are critically impaired in cardiometabolic disease. Reduced SIRT1 expression under hyperglycaemic conditions accelerates progenitor cell ageing, whereas its activation restores angiogenic competence and vascular repair capacity \[[^203], [^205]\]. Beyond the endothelium, SIRT1 in vascular smooth muscle cells modulates proliferation, migration, and phenotypic switching, processes central to neointima formation and vascular remodelling, with SIRT1 generally exerting anti‐proliferative and anti‐inflammatory effects \[[^206]\]. In the myocardium, SIRT1 preserves contractile function and limits pathological hypertrophy by enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, reducing [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]], and modulating autophagic flux, although chronic overactivation may shift its effect toward pro‐hypertrophic signalling, underscoring its dose‐dependent and context‐dependent nature \[[^207], [^208]\].

Other sirtuins contribute to distinct compartmentalised layers of regulation that complement SIRT1 in cardiometabolic disease. Mitochondrial [[SIRT3]], for instance, governs [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]] and [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] detoxification through [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of key enzymes such as [[IDH2|isocitrate dehydrogenase 2]], [[MnSOD]], and components of the [[Electron Transport Chain]] \[[^209], [^210]\]. In [[Metabolic Syndrome]], reduced SIRT3 activity leads to hyperacetylation of mitochondrial proteins, impaired [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]], and elevated mitochondrial [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]], thereby exacerbating [[Endothelial Dysfunction]] and cardiac injury \[[^211]\].

In addition, SIRT3 modulates the [[Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore|mitochondrial permeability transition pore]] and exerts protective effects during [[Ischemia-reperfusion Injury|ischaemia–reperfusion injury]], connecting mitochondrial proteostasis with cardiomyocyte viability \[[^212], [^213]\]. [[SIRT2]] regulates microtubule dynamics and inflammatory signalling; its [[Deacetylation|deacetylation]] of [[Tubulin|α‐tubulin]] and modulation of [[NLRP3]] inflammasome activation suggest a role in vascular [[Inflammation]] and [[Insulin Sensitivity|insulin resistance]]–associated [[Endothelial Dysfunction|endothelial dysfunction]], although its net effect appears context‐dependent, with both protective and deleterious phenotypes reported \[[^214], [^215], [^216]\].

In cardiometabolic settings, [[SIRT6]] deficiency promotes endothelial [[Inflammation]] and accelerates [[Atherosclerosis]] through derepression of [[NF-κB]] target genes, while also impairing lipid homoeostasis via dysregulation of [[PCSK9]] and [[LDL|LDL metabolism]] \[[^217]\]. [[SIRT4]] and [[SIRT5]] contribute to mitochondrial [[Metabolic Flexibility|metabolic inflexibility]] in [[Metabolic Syndrome]] via distinct enzymatic activities. SIRT4 limits anaplerotic flux and [[Insulin Secretion]] through [[ADP-ribosylation|ADP‐ribosylation]]‐dependent inhibition of [[Glutamate Dehydrogenase (GDH)|glutamate dehydrogenase]] and regulation of amino acid metabolism, whereas SIRT5 controls [[Fatty acid oxidation|fatty acid oxidation]] and [[TCA cycle]] activity through [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]] and [[Acetylation|demalonylation]]; disruption of these pathways promotes acylation‐dependent mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired substrate oxidation, and [[Redox Homeostasis|redox imbalance]] \[[^62]\]. SIRT5‐mediated [[Acetylation|desuccinylation]] of enzymes in the [[TCA cycle|TCA]] and urea cycle is particularly relevant in conditions of nutrient excess, where aberrant acylation states contribute to [[Metabolic Flexibility|metabolic inflexibility]] and ammonia handling defects \[[^15]\]. Collectively, sirtuins form an integrated network in which subcellular localisation dictates substrate specificity and functional output, enabling fine‐tuned regulation of metabolic flux, [[Redox Homeostasis|redox balance]], and inflammatory signalling. In [[Metabolic Syndrome]], disruption of this network, via altered [[NAD+]] metabolism, chronic nutrient excess, and [[Oxidative Stress|oxidative stress]], results in coordinated dysregulation of vascular, cardiac, and immune pathways, thereby driving the progression toward overt cardiometabolic complications.

## 6\. Conclusions

Sirtuins have emerged as central regulators of metabolic homoeostasis, acting at the intersection of cellular energy sensing, transcriptional control, and mitochondrial function. Their strict dependence on [[NAD+]] availability positions them as dynamic sensors of metabolic state, enabling the integration of nutrient signals with epigenetic and post‐translational regulatory mechanisms across multiple cellular compartments. A key concept that arises from current evidence is that sirtuins do not function as isolated enzymes, but rather as components of a coordinated and compartmentalised regulatory network. Nuclear sirtuins, including [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT6]], and [[SIRT7]], primarily govern transcriptional programs that regulate metabolism, [[Inflammation]], and [[Mitochondrial Biogenesis]], whereas mitochondrial sirtuins, such as [[SIRT3]], [[SIRT4]], and [[SIRT5]], directly modulate enzymatic activity and metabolic fluxes within the organelle. Within this framework, a functional polarisation becomes apparent: [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT3]], and [[SIRT6]] generally promote [[Metabolic Flexibility]], [[Oxidative Phosphorylation|oxidative metabolism]], and stress resistance, while [[SIRT4]] and [[SIRT7]] are more frequently associated with energy conservation and, under certain conditions, metabolic dysfunction. [[SIRT2]] plays an intermediate and context‐dependent role linking cytoskeletal organisation, [[Cell Cycle|cell cycle]] progression, and metabolic regulation.

Importantly, metabolic diseases can be interpreted as states of disrupted sirtuin network coordination rather than the consequence of alterations in single isoforms. Changes in [[NAD+]] homoeostasis, chronic nutrient excess, and persistent inflammatory signalling collectively shift the balance of sirtuin activity, leading to impaired [[Metabolic Flexibility]], [[Mitochondrial Dysfunction]], and maladaptive tissue remodelling. This network‐based perspective helps to explain the heterogeneity observed across tissues, disease stages, and experimental models. Despite substantial progress, several challenges remain. The context‐dependent and sometimes opposing roles of different sirtuin isoforms complicate therapeutic targeting, and most mechanistic insights derive from preclinical systems with limited direct translation to human physiology. Furthermore, the spatial and temporal regulation of [[NAD+]] metabolism and its compartmentalised impact on sirtuin activity are not yet fully understood. Future research should therefore focus on defining the dynamics of the sirtuin network in human metabolic disease, identifying isoform‐specific functions in distinct tissues, and developing strategies capable of selectively modulating sirtuin activity or restoring [[NAD+]] homoeostasis. In this context, approaches targeting [[NAD+ Biosynthesis|NAD+ biosynthesis]] and turnover, as well as isoform‐selective modulators, represent promising avenues for multi‐level therapeutic intervention.

In conclusion, sirtuins should be viewed not as individual metabolic regulators but as an integrated system that links energy status to cellular adaptation. A deeper understanding of this network will be essential for translating sirtuin biology into effective strategies for the prevention and rehabilitational treatment of metabolic diseases.
