# CobB

## Overview

CobB is the founding member of prokaryotic sirtuins, first identified in *Salmonella enterica*. Its name reflects its original context: it was discovered through involvement in cobalamin (vitamin B12) biosynthesis and propionate catabolism. Structurally and functionally, CobB is a [[NAD+]]-dependent [[Deacetylases|deacetylase]] that belongs to the [[Sirtuins]] family, demonstrating that sirtuin-type enzymes are not restricted to eukaryotes but are broadly conserved across the tree of life.

## Mechanism

Like eukaryotic sirtuins, CobB couples the hydrolysis of NAD+ to the removal of acetyl (and related acyl) groups from lysine residues on protein substrates. In bacteria, one or two sirtuin genes are typically present, and CobB serves as the archetypal example of how prokaryotes use sirtuin-mediated acylation control to regulate metabolic pathways. Its activity links the availability of NAD+ — a universal metabolic currency — to the post-translational regulation of enzymes, particularly in carbon and cofactor metabolism.

## Physiological Function

In *Salmonella* and related enteric bacteria, CobB regulates central metabolism, including propionate catabolism and the cobalamin biosynthetic pathway, by adjusting the acetylation state of key metabolic enzymes. This connects nutrient availability to enzyme activity in a way that is directly analogous to the regulation of mammalian metabolism by sirtuins such as [[SIRT1]], [[SIRT3]], and [[SIRT4]]. The conservation of this mechanism from bacteria to humans underscores the deep evolutionary roots of NAD+-dependent acylation control.

> [!info] Evolutionary significance
> CobB shows that NAD+-dependent deacetylation predates eukaryotic multicellularity, supporting the view that sirtuin-mediated metabolic sensing is a primordial regulatory strategy conserved across domains of life.

## Research Implications

CobB remains a useful model for dissecting sirtuin enzymology and for understanding how acylation-based regulation is integrated with bacterial physiology. Comparative study of CobB and human sirtuins can also inform the design of selective [[Sirtuin Inhibitors]] and activators.
