Network Diagrams

Integrated views of the sirtuin regulatory network across cellular compartments and NAD+ metabolism.

From: "The Sirtuin Network: Linking NAD+ Metabolism, Mitochondrial Function, and Metabolic Homoeostasis" (2025)

Figure 1 · Compartment Map

The Compartmentalised Sirtuin Network

Nuclear sirtuins (SIRT1, SIRT6, SIRT7) orchestrate transcriptional programs controlling metabolism and inflammation. Mitochondrial sirtuins (SIRT3, SIRT4, SIRT5) directly regulate metabolic flux and redox homoeostasis. Cytosolic SIRT2 connects metabolic signalling with cytoskeletal and cell-cycle processes. NAD+ metabolism dynamically modulates sirtuin activity across compartments.
Central to this system, NAD+ metabolism enables coupling of cellular energy status to coordinated metabolic responses across glucose/lipid metabolism, inflammatory signalling, and mitochondrial function.
Source: PMC13364290 ↗
Figure 2 · NAD+ Metabolism

NAD+ as Central Regulator of Sirtuin Activity

NAD+ is produced through de novo (tryptophan → kynurenine), Preiss–Handler (nicotinic acid), and salvage (nicotinamide → NMN via NAMPT) pathways. Beyond its role as a redox cofactor, NAD+ serves as essential substrate for sirtuins, directly linking its availability to their enzymatic activity across compartments.
Changes in NAD+ levels across cellular compartments dynamically regulate the sirtuin network, influencing mitochondrial function, metabolic pathways, and stress responses. Age-related NAD+ decline (via CD38 upregulation) compromises sirtuin function.
Source: PMC13364290 ↗
Figure 2 · Disease Network

SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 Attenuate Age-Associated Diseases

Schematic overview of how SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 protect against neurodegeneration, metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, cancer, and cardiovascular disease. Tumor suppressor and oncogenic properties have been reported for each protein. SIRT1 and SIRT6 overexpression extend longevity in mice.
This figure from the landmark Giblin et al. (2014) review summarizes the evidence that mammalian sirtuins act as "guardians of healthspan" across the major hallmarks of ageing.
Source: PMC4077918 ↗

Chromatin Biology & DNA Repair

Sirtuins as epigenetic regulators — deacetylating histones to promote heterochromatin formation, telomere maintenance, and DNA damage repair.

From: "Sirtuins, a promising target in slowing down the ageing process" (2017) & "Rejuvenating Sirtuins" (2013)

Figure 1 · Chromatin

Role of SIRT1 and SIRT6 in Chromatin Condensation

Both sirtuins promote heterochromatin formation through three mechanisms: (1) deacetylation of H3K9 enabling its trimethylation and HP1α binding, (2) SIRT1 decreases p300 histone acetyltransferase activity, and (3) SIRT1 activates SUV39H1 methyltransferase by deacetylating K266. Arrows = positive regulation; T-bars = inhibition. Thick arrows indicate changes during ageing.
H3K9 deacetylation by SIRT1 and SIRT6 is essential for heterochromatin formation. Loss of this function during ageing contributes to genomic instability, aberrant gene expression, and cellular senescence.
Source: PMC5514220 ↗
Figure 3 · Interactome

Sirtuin Protein Interacting Partners & Substrates

Examples of sirtuin interacting partners and substrates relevant to the pathways outlined in the review: metabolism (PGC-1α, FOXO, NF-κB), chromatin biology (H3K9, H3K56, H4K16), DNA repair (Ku70, WRN, CtIP), cell survival (p53, survivin, c-MYC), and mitochondrial function (SOD2, IDH2).
The overlapping substrate usage among SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 — sometimes with opposing functional outcomes — underscores the context-dependent nature of sirtuin biology and the difficulty of pan-sirtuin therapeutic strategies.
Source: PMC4077918 ↗

Lifespan & Healthspan

Key experimental evidence for sirtuin-mediated lifespan extension in mice — brain-specific SIRT1 and whole-body SIRT6 overexpression.

From: "Sirtuins: Guardians of Mammalian Healthspan" (2014)

Figure 1 · Lifespan

SIRT1 & SIRT6 Overexpression Extend Mouse Lifespan

Panel A: SIRT1 overexpressed in dorsomedial/lateral hypothalamic nuclei (DMH/LH) extends lifespan of both sexes by upregulating the orexin type 2 receptor (OX2R) via deacetylation of NK2 homeobox 1 (NKX2-1). Panel B: Whole-body SIRT6 overexpression extends male lifespan by inhibiting IGF-1/AKT signaling cascade, including downstream pAKT, pFOXO1, pFOXO3, and pS6 kinase.
Brain-specific SIRT1 overexpression (BRASTO mice) increases median lifespan ~11% and mimics calorie restriction. SIRT6 overexpression extends male median lifespan by 14.5% and 9.9% in two independent strains — a male-specific effect linked to reduced serum IGF-1 levels.
Source: PMC4077918 ↗

Protein Structures

Crystal structures of human sirtuin isoforms resolved by X-ray crystallography, revealing the conserved Rossmann-fold domain, zinc-binding domain, and cofactor/substrate binding sites.

From: "Sirtuins: Shedding light on structure, function and regulation" (2023) & "Rejuvenating Sirtuins" (2013)

Figure 1 · SIRT1

Structure of SIRT1 (PDB: 4IG9)

The NAD+ binding region consists of a core six-stranded parallel β-sheet (strands β1–3, β7–9) and eight helices. The helical module (αC–F) and Zn2+-binding unit (β4–6, αI) form the smaller domain. The C-terminal regulatory region (CTR) creates a β-hairpin covering a hydrophobic core.
SIRT1 has strong deacetylase activity and modulates anti-ageing signalling with AMPK. It promotes healthy ageing, protects against metabolic-syndrome-linked cancers, and delays neurodegeneration.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 2 · SIRT2

Structure of SIRT2 (PDB: 3ZGO)

The catalytic core is an elongated structure with a larger Rossmann-fold domain (six β-strands, six α-helices) and a smaller domain comprising a helical module (α3–α6) and a zinc-binding region (β4–6, α9). A large groove at the domain intersection forms the active site cleft.
SIRT2 has both mono-ADP-ribosyl transferase and deacetylase activity. It controls metabolic reprogramming during induced pluripotency and mediates tubulin acetylation. Upregulated in NSCLC and metastatic melanoma.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 3 · SIRT3

Structure of SIRT3 (PDB: 3GLS)

SIRT3 has a two-domain structure: a large Rossmann-fold domain for NAD+ binding and a smaller domain consisting of a helical bundle and zinc-binding region created by two extending loops from the large domain. A groove between the zinc-binding and helical bundle may participate in protein–protein interactions.
The principal mitochondrial deacetylase. Full-length SIRT3 is inactive; cleavage of the N-terminal 101 residues by mitochondrial processing peptidase activates it. Suppresses tumor growth via HIF-1α regulation and ROS suppression.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 4 · SIRT5

Structure of SIRT5 (PDB: 2NYR)

Made up of 14 α-helices and 9 β-strands arranged in two globular domains. The larger Rossmann-fold domain contains a core β-sheet of six parallel strands and nine α-helices. The smaller zinc-binding domain has a unique insertion: helix α9 and a 16-residue preceding loop specific to human SIRT5.
SIRT5 has weak deacetylase activity but is a potent desuccinylase/demalonylase/deglutarylase. Its deacetylation activity is unusually resistant to nicotinamide inhibition — relevant for drug design.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 5 · SIRT6

Structure of SIRT6 (PDB: 3K35)

Two globular domains with eight α-helices and nine β-strands. The Rossmann-fold domain features a six-stranded parallel β-sheet. The smaller zinc-binding domain is formed by two extending loops. Notably, SIRT6 lacks the cofactor-binding loop found in other sirtuins — replaced by a single helix (α3) with NAD+-binding residues.
SIRT6 is a mono-ADP-ribosyl transferase essential for telomere maintenance, DNA repair, and inhibition of glycolysis and ROS. Its compact small domain (no helix bundle) may underlie its unique NAD+ binding properties.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 6 · SIRT7

Structure of SIRT7 (PDB: 5IQZ)

The SIRT7 N-terminal domain (NTD) consists of three helices (α1–α3) connected by a short 310 helix. α1 is the longest helix (35 residues, Gly5–Arg37). α2 and α3 are nearly perpendicular to α1. The NTD shows structural similarities to transcription factor regulators and may participate in DNA binding.
SIRT7 has deacetylase activity critical for ageing and hematopoietic cell lifespan. It regulates rDNA transcription, pre-rRNA cleavage, and acts as a heterochromatin stabiliser counteracting stem cell ageing.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 1 · SIRT3 Complex

X-ray Structure of SIRT3 with Acetyl-CoA Synthetase 2

Human SIRT3 in complex with ADPR bound to the Acetyl-coenzyme A synthetase 2 (AceCS2) peptide containing thioacetyl lysine (PDB: 3GLT). Color-coded: red = Rossmann-fold domain, yellow = zinc-binding domain, green = cofactor-binding loop. Surfaces of substrate-binding (purple) and NAD+-binding (light blue) sites are highlighted.
This co-crystal structure reveals the productive binding mode of the acetyl-lysine substrate and NAD+ cofactor in the extended cleft between the two domains — a template for structure-based drug design.
Source: PMC3549556 ↗

Pharmacology — Activators & Inhibitors

Chemical structures of small molecules that modulate sirtuin activity, from natural polyphenols to synthetic STACs and inhibitors.

From: "Sirtuins: Shedding light on structure" & "Rejuvenating Sirtuins" & "The Sirtuin System: The Holy Grail of Resveratrol?"

Figure 7 · Activators

Sirtuin Activating Compounds (STACs)

Structures of five sirtuin activators: (a) buteīn, (b) isoliquiritigenin, (c) piceatannol, (d) resveratrol, and (e) SRT1720. The first four are natural polyphenols; SRT1720 is a synthetic STAC with substantially more potent SIRT1 activation.
Plant polyphenols activate recombinant SIRT1. Resveratrol replicates calorie restriction effects in lower organisms and improves insulin resistance in HFD mice. Synthetic STACs (imidazo[1,2-b]thiazole core) activate SIRT1 more potently.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 8 · Inhibitors

Sirtuin Inhibiting Compounds (STICs)

Structures of seven sirtuin inhibitors: (a) Splitomicin, (b) HR73, (c) Sirtinol, (d) AGK2, (e) Cambinol, (f) Salermide, and (g) Suramin. Most inhibit SIRT1 and/or SIRT2; some also affect SIRT3 and SIRT5.
Up-regulated SIRT1 in cancer cell lines makes inhibitors potential therapeutics. Suramin inhibits SIRT1/SIRT2 at nanomolar concentrations. Cambinol is effective against Burkitt lymphoma. Salermide causes p53-independent apoptosis in cancer cells.
Source: PMC9805487 ↗
Figure 1 · Resveratrol & CAD

Resveratrol's Effects on Coronary Artery Disease Risk

Comprehensive diagram summarizing the proposed mechanisms by which resveratrol influences factors contributing to coronary artery disease (CAD). Resveratrol modifies a large number of atherogenic pathways including oxidative stress, inflammation, lipid metabolism, endothelial function, and platelet aggregation.
The "French Paradox" — reduced CVD in French populations with moderate wine consumption — motivated research into resveratrol as a cardioprotective polyphenol. It is a known SIRT1 activator with anti-proliferative and anti-inflammatory properties.
Source: PMC3613783 ↗

Substrates & Chemical Probes

Chemical structures of sirtuin inhibitors and activators from the cancer drug discovery perspective, covering diverse scaffolds identified through screening, rational design, and natural product analysis.

From: "Rejuvenating Sirtuins: The Rise of a New Family of Cancer Drug Targets" (2013)

Figure 2 · Inhibitors

Sirtuin Inhibitor Chemical Space

Structural diversity of sirtuin inhibitors spanning: nicotinamide (endogenous product inhibitor), sirtinol, salermide, cambinol, splitomicin, kinase inhibitors (Ro31-8220, GW5074), EX-527 (nanomolar-selective SIRT1 inhibitor), suramin (nanomolar dual SIRT1/SIRT2), and NF675 (20-fold SIRT1 selectivity).
Inhibitors bind different sirtuin pockets depending on the isoform and scaffold. EX-527 is the most potent and selective SIRT1 inhibitor known. Suramin occupies the nicotinamide ribose pocket (B-pocket), C-pocket, and part of the substrate-binding site (co-crystal with SIRT5).
Source: PMC3549556 ↗
Figure 3 · Activators

Sirtuin Activating Compounds (STACs)

Natural polyphenolic activators (1–5): resveratrol, piceatannol, buteīn, quercetin, and myricetin. Synthetic STACs (6–8): SRT1460, SRT1720, and SRT2183 — chemically diverse and more potent than the natural compounds. Non-polyphenolic allosteric activators have also been reported.
Controversy: SIRT1 activation by these compounds was often only demonstrated with fluorescently tagged substrates. Resveratrol's in vivo effects may be indirect via the cAMP–Epac1–AMPK pathway rather than direct SIRT1 binding. The specific amino acid in SIRT1 targeted by STACs has been identified — mutation at this site abolishes STAC response.
Source: PMC3549556 ↗
