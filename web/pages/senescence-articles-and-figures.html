Foundational Review — Coppé et al. (2010)

The landmark Annual Review of Pathology article that framed the SASP as the "dark side of tumor suppression" — defining its composition, regulation, and effects on the tissue microenvironment.

From: "The Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype: The Dark Side of Tumor Suppression" (2010) — NIH Public Access manuscript

Figure 1 · Senescence Triggers

Stimuli That Provoke Cellular Senescence

A variety of stresses provoke cellular senescence: telomeric dysfunction from repeated cell division (replicative senescence), mitochondrial deterioration, oxidative stress, severe or irreparable DNA damage and chromatin disruption (genotoxic stress), and oncogene expression. The growth arrest depends on the tumor-suppressor pathways controlled by p16INK4a/Rb and p53.
When irreversible arrest is triggered by severe DNA damage (dysfunctional telomeres or oncogenic stress), senescent cells mount a SASP. Cells that overexpress p16 or p21 arrest with many senescent features but little or no SASP — showing the growth arrest and the secretome can be uncoupled.
Source: PMC4166495 ↗
Figure 2 · IL-6 / IL-8

The Lead SASP Cytokines: IL-6 & IL-8

Presenescent (PRE) and senescent (SEN) human fibroblasts — made senescent by replicative exhaustion (REP), ionizing radiation (IR), or oncogenic RAS expression — immunostained for the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 together with the senescence marker p16. IL-6 is the most prominent SASP cytokine; its secretion is controlled by persistent DNA-damage signaling (ATM/CHK2) independently of p53.
The SASP forms a self-amplifying network: CXCR2-binding chemokines (IL-8, GROα, GROβ) reinforce both the growth arrest and the secretome through an autocrine positive-feedback loop that locks in the senescent phenotype.
Source: PMC4166495 ↗
Figure 3 · p53 & the SASP

p53 as a Cell-Nonautonomous Guardian

The DNA damage signaling pathway activates p53, which triggers cell-fate decisions such as senescence or apoptosis. Depending on cell context, p53 suppresses cancer through transient cell-cycle arrest and DNA repair — and additionally restrains the SASP, a cell-nonautonomous tumor-suppressor function.
Loss of p53 in senescing or damaged fibroblasts greatly enhances the SASP and its stimulatory effects on malignant epithelial cells. The short-lived SASP promotes tissue repair, whereas long-term exposure drives chronic inflammation, cancer and aging.
Source: PMC4166495 ↗

cGAS-STING Signaling & SASP

The cGAS-STING DNA-sensing pathway as a master trigger of the SASP — from its evolutionary origins to its roles in cancer and neuroinflammation.

From: "Cellular senescence and SASP via the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in cancer" (Loo et al., Cancer Sci, 2019) & "Expanding roles of cGAS-STING signaling in neuroinflammation" (Feng et al., J Clin Invest, 2026)

Figure 1 · Loo 2019

Characteristics of Cellular Senescence

Cellular senescence is triggered by stressors such as irradiation, reactive oxygen species, oncogene activation and telomere shortening. Senescent cells show increased p16INK4a and/or p21, SASP factors, DNA damage foci, senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF), and decreased Lamin B1. Arrest is imposed via the p16- and/or p21-dependent blockade of CDK–Rb–E2F signaling.
SA-β-gal staining is the most common marker in practice, but it is not specific to senescence — a combination of markers is required to identify senescent cells confidently.
Source: PMC7004529 ↗
Figure 2 · Loo 2019

The Two Faces of the SASP

SASP factors play beneficial roles in wound healing (PDGF-AA), embryonic development (TGF-β; Wnt) and immune recruitment (CXCL1, CCL2, CCL5) — but under pathological conditions they drive tumor progression and migration (MMP, IL-1β, VEGF) and suppress anti-tumor immunity (PGE2). SASP factors also reinforce senescent arrest through paracrine and autocrine loops.
Declining immune function during aging weakens senescence surveillance, so senescent cells accumulate and their prolonged SASP output — e.g., IL-1β from senescent hepatic stellate cells — can drive obesity-associated hepatocellular carcinoma.
Source: PMC7004529 ↗
Figure 3 · Loo 2019

cGAS-STING Cascade in Senescence

Cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCF), mitochondrial DNA, and LINE-1-derived cDNA accumulate in senescent cells and are sensed by cGAS, which produces 2′3′-cGAMP. cGAMP activates STING and TBK1, phosphorylating IRF3, and activates NF-κB via IκBα — inducing type-I interferons and inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-β, IL-6 and IL-8 that maintain the senescent state.
E2F-controlled cytoplasmic DNases DNase2 and TREX1 are downregulated in senescence, allowing cytosolic DNA to accumulate; LINE-1 retrotransposon transcription is also upregulated, feeding the cDNA pool that triggers cGAS-STING.
Source: PMC7004529 ↗
Figure 4 · Loo 2019

Context-Dependent Roles of cGAS-STING in Liver Cancer

DNA damage induces senescence in hepatic stellate cells and hepatocytes; cytosolic DNA activates cGAS-STING and produces SASP factors. Short-term SASP exposure recruits immune cells to clear pre-malignant and senescent cells, preventing tumorigenesis — but long-term exposure generates chronic inflammation that promotes tumorigenesis in obese mice.
STING knockout blunted SASP production and attenuated obesity-associated HCC, while inhibiting cGAS-STING in other settings impaired immune surveillance of senescent/premalignant hepatocytes — the pathway is a double-edged sword whose net effect depends on biological context.
Source: PMC7004529 ↗
Figure 1 · Feng 2026 (JCI)

Evolutionary Diversification of cGAS-STING

The cGAS-STING pathway is an evolutionarily ancient innate immune system that predates metazoans. cGAS has a conserved Mab21 catalytic domain, a divergent N-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR) that promotes liquid-liquid phase separation with DNA, and a vertebrate-specific zinc-ribbon domain. Vertebrate STING acquired a C-terminal tail enabling TBK1/IRF3 recruitment and robust type-I IFN signaling.
The Nematostella vectensis cGAS homolog — which lacks the zinc-ribbon domain — can synthesize cGAMP but fails to respond to dsDNA, supporting the view that DNA sensing evolved later as a specialized IFN-driven adaptation.
Source: JCI204550 ↗
Figure 2 · Feng 2026 (JCI)

Canonical & Noncanonical Signaling Outputs

cGAMP-bound STING translocates from the ER to the ERGIC, recruits and is phosphorylated by TBK1, then phosphorylates IRF3, which dimerizes and drives type-I IFN and ISG expression. STING additionally engages NF-κB (via IκB kinase), and feeds autophagy through LC3 lipidation on ERGIC membranes and TFEB-driven lysosomal biogenesis.
Nuclear cGAS is tethered to nucleosomes through the H2A/H2B acidic patch to prevent aberrant activation; it also interacts with PARP1 to suppress homologous recombination — while the long-lived naked mole rat's cGAS shows enhanced chromatin retention and DNA repair.
Source: JCI204550 ↗
Figure 3 · Feng 2026 (JCI)

cGAS-STING in the Nervous System

Sustained cGAS-STING/IFN-I signaling activates microglia and astrocytes across brain disorders, driving ISG and cytokine induction. Neuronal STING restricts viral replication and promotes axonal regeneration, while endothelial cGAS-STING activation (triggered by damaged mtDNA) induces IFN-I and pyroptotic pathways that disrupt the blood-brain barrier.
Skull bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells survey the brain via meningeal lymphatics and maintain quiescence by suppressing cGAS-STING — activation or IFN-I drives HSC mobilization or cellular senescence in this niche.
Source: JCI204550 ↗
Figure 4 · Feng 2026 (JCI)

cGAS-STING in Neurodegenerative Diseases

In Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and frontotemporal dementia models, cGAS or STING inhibition selectively suppresses the neurotoxic components of gliosis while preserving beneficial microglial functions — cGAS deletion even enhances Aβ clearance by increasing microglial recruitment to plaques. Tau-induced microglial cGAS-STING/IFN-I signaling also promotes tau seeding and propagation.
Pharmacological cGAS inhibition phenocopies the protective APOE3 Christchurch (R136S) resilience signature in tauopathy — restoring neuronal MEF2C-driven gene networks and preserving synapses, motor function and cognition.
Source: JCI204550 ↗

Senotherapeutics — Fisetin

The senolytic flavone fisetin from identification to translation: senescent-cell clearance, healthspan and lifespan extension in mice, and reversal of age-related endothelial dysfunction.

From: "Fisetin is a senotherapeutic that extends health and lifespan" (Yousefzadeh et al., EBioMedicine, 2018) & "Senolytic Treatment With Fisetin Reverses Age-Related Endothelial Dysfunction Partially Mediated by SASP Factor CXCL12" (Mahoney et al., Aging Cell, 2026)

Figure 1 · Fisetin 2018

Identification of Fisetin as a Senolytic

Passage 5 Ercc1−/− MEFs were treated with a panel of flavonoid compounds at 5 μM and cell viability measured with an IN Cell Analyzer 6000, distinguishing senescent (SA-β-gal+) from total cells. Fisetin emerged as the most potent senolytic of the screened flavonoids — killing senescent cells while sparing proliferating cells.
The screen exploits the heightened dependence of senescent cells on survival pathways: among polyphenols tested, fisetin preferentially eliminated senescent fibroblasts via apoptosis induction.
Source: PMC6197652 ↗
Figure 2 · Fisetin 2018

Intermittent Fisetin Reduces Senescent Burden

Age-matched 12-week-old male p16+/Luc;Ercc1−/Δ progeroid mice were fed a diet containing 500 ppm fisetin or control chow. Luciferase signal was measured biweekly in p16+/Luc reporter mice, with fisetin administered intermittently — two weeks on, then off — (yellow bars) to avoid continuous drug exposure.
Intermittent dosing is the clinically relevant paradigm: even short pulses of fisetin significantly reduced the p16-driven luminescent senescent-cell burden in these accelerated-aging mice.
Source: PMC6197652 ↗
Figure 3 · Fisetin 2018

Chronic Fisetin Suppresses Senescence & SASP

Tissues from 20-week-old Ercc1−/Δ mice chronically exposed to fisetin through the diet were analyzed by qRT-PCR for senescence markers (p16Ink4a, p21) and SASP markers (Il1β, Il6, Il10, Tnfα, Cxcl2, Mcp1, Pai1), normalized to age-matched wild-type tissues.
Chronic dietary fisetin decreased both cell-autonomous senescence markers and the pro-inflammatory SASP transcriptome across multiple tissues of progeroid mice.
Source: PMC6197652 ↗
Figure 4 · Fisetin 2018

Acute Fisetin in Aged Mice & Human Explants

22–24-month-old WT C57BL/6 mice received fisetin (100 mg/kg) or vehicle by oral gavage for 5 days; 72 h after the final dose, SA-β-gal+ cells were quantified in inguinal fat. The figure also diagrams the INK-ATTAC transgene used to detect p16-expressing cells, alongside human tissue explant experiments.
Even a short acute course of fisetin reduced senescent cell burden in naturally aged mice and in explants of human adipose tissue — evidence for potential translational relevance.
Source: PMC6197652 ↗
Figure 5 · Fisetin 2018

Late-Life Fisetin Extends Healthspan & Lifespan

At 85 weeks of age (>20 months), male and female mice were switched to a diet containing 500 ppm fisetin or control chow, and lifespan was measured. Median lifespan of the treated cohort is plotted per animal, with blood clinical chemistry (e.g., ALT liver markers) assessed in parallel.
Intervention began at an age roughly equivalent to late life in humans — fisetin extended remaining lifespan and improved healthspan metrics even when treatment was started in aged animals.
Source: PMC6197652 ↗
Figure 1 · Fisetin-CXCL12 2026

scRNA-seq Atlas of the Aging Aorta

Study-design schematic for four mouse groups — young vehicle (Y VEH), young fisetin (Y FIS), old vehicle (O VEH) and old fisetin (O FIS) — with combined UMAP projection of whole-aorta single-cell transcriptomes, counts of differentially abundant RNA (DAR) between groups, Reactome pathway analysis of age-increased DARs, canonical senescence marker UMAPs, and SenMayo module scoring to identify senescent cells.
Single-cell resolution revealed that the senescent-cell compartment expands across the vessel wall with aging and contracts with oral, intermittent fisetin treatment in old animals.
Source: PMC13103471 ↗
Figure 2 · Fisetin-CXCL12 2026

Senescent Endothelial Cells with Aging

UMAP and subcluster percentages of aortic endothelial cells (ECs) across the four treatment groups; module gene scoring with the EndoSen signature identifies senescent ECs in subcluster 10, with canonical senescence/SASP transcript dot plots, top DARs, and KEGG pathway analysis of the senescent EC program.
Endothelial subcluster 10 is a senescent EC population that expands in old mice and is selectively reduced by fisetin — the cellular basis for improved endothelial function after senolysis.
Source: PMC13103471 ↗
Figure 3 · Fisetin-CXCL12 2026

SASP Factors in the Aorta & Plasma

Violin plots of the top SASP-related DARs in senescent endothelial subcluster 10 across young/old, vehicle/ fisetin groups, together with circulating plasma protein abundance of the top SASP-related DARs — showing that the age-driven SASP rise is ameliorated by senolytic treatment.
The concordance between tissue transcriptomes and plasma proteomes links the local senescent-cell burden to a systemic, measurable SASP signature.
Source: PMC13103471 ↗
Figure 4 · Fisetin-CXCL12 2026

Fisetin Mitigates Senescent EC-Derived CXCL12

Cell-cell communication dot plots of senescent EC-derived CXCL12 interactions across cell types; combined network diagrams of outgoing (top) and incoming (bottom) signals; a differential interaction-strength heatmap; and expression profiles of CXCL12 receptors in senescent EC cluster 10 across treatment groups.
CXCL12 emerged as a dominant SASP factor released by senescent endothelial cells — a signaling axis that fisetin disrupts both by eliminating the senescent EC source and by damping its secretory output.
Source: PMC13103471 ↗
Figure 5 · Fisetin-CXCL12 2026

CXCL12 Drives Endothelial Dysfunction

Endothelium-dependent and -independent dilation of isolated carotid arteries perfused with young or old plasma ± recombinant CXCL12; SA-β-gal signal; CDKN1A/CDKN2A mRNA; nitric oxide bioactivity; and mitochondrial superoxide in human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) — with the CXCL12 antagonist LIT-927 blocking the aging-plasma effects.
Recombinant CXCL12 recapitulated the effects of old plasma on arteries and HAECs — SA-β-gal induction, p16/p21 expression, oxidative stress, and impaired NO — establishing CXCL12 as a causal SASP mediator of vascular aging.
Source: PMC13103471 ↗
Figure 6 · Fisetin-CXCL12 2026

Fisetin Prevents SASP-Driven EndoMT

Dot plots of endothelial and mesenchymal mRNA markers in senescent endothelial subcluster 10, plus HAEC mRNA levels of endothelial biomarkers (CDH5, PECAM1) and mesenchymal biomarkers (TGFB1, ACTA2) following plasma exposure with or without recombinant CXCL12 and LIT-927.
Old-plasma and CXCL12 drive endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition — a phenotypic switch implicated in vascular remodeling and atherosclerosis — which fisetin treatment and CXCL12 antagonism both prevent.
Source: PMC13103471 ↗

Epigenetics & SASP Regulation

How the dynamic epigenetic landscape of senescent cells orchestrates SASP expression — and how epigenetic drugs can act as senomorphics.

From: "The role of the dynamic epigenetic landscape in senescence: orchestrating SASP expression" (Dasgupta et al., npj Aging, 2024)

Figure 1 · Epigenetic Landscape

Epigenetic Control of the SASP

Senescent cells undergo profound epigenetic reprogramming — heterochromatin reorganization, altered DNA methylation patterns, histone modifications, loss of core histones, accumulation of histone variants, and dysregulated non-coding RNAs — that integrates senescence-inducing signals (e.g., cytoplasmic chromatin fragments activating the cGAS-STING-NF-κB axis) with NF-κB, the "master regulator" of SASP transcription.
Because SASP expression is epigenetically locked in, epigenetic-modulating drugs can act as senomorphics — suppressing the inflammatory secretome without eliminating the senescent cells themselves.
Source: npj Aging (2024) ↗

Mitochondria & Neurodegeneration

Mitochondrial dysfunction as a driver of cellular senescence — and as the proposed bridge connecting senescence to neurodegenerative disease.

From: "Mitochondrial dysfunction in cellular senescence: a bridge to neurodegenerative disease" (Hruby & Higuchi-Sanabria, npj Aging, 2025)

Figure 1 · Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Mitochondria in Senescence: Arrest & SASP

Senescent cells exhibit a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), increased uncoupling via UCP2, reduced OXPHOS capacity, elevated ROS production, and damage to mtDNA, mitochondrial proteins and lipids (e.g., 4-HNE adducts) — a feed-forward loop that both aids the proliferative arrest and enhances the SASP, with mitochondrial metabolites and DAMPs feeding the pro-inflammatory secretome.
The review expands "mitochondrial dysfunction" beyond bioenergetics to encompass mitochondrial form, calcium handling, dynamics, quality control, and DAMP release — and summarizes mitochondrial-targeted anti-senescence interventions (Table 1).
Source: npj Aging (2025) ↗
Figure 2 · Shared Features

A Bridge to Neurodegenerative Disease

The same mitochondrial defects seen in senescent cells recur in Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and Huntington's disease: Aβ and phosphorylated tau impair ETC complexes I and IV in neurons, calcium overload triggers mPTP formation, and ER-mitochondria contact sites (MERCS) are remodeled — coupling mitochondrial dysfunction, senescence, and neurodegeneration.
The proposal: mitochondrial dysfunction promotes senescence, which in turn contributes to neurodegeneration — positioning mitochondria as an upstream therapeutic target for both senescent-cell clearance and neuroprotection.
Source: npj Aging (2025) ↗

Cancer & the SASP

The double-edged role of cellular senescence and SASP in tumor progression — from senescence inducers and signaling pathways to the mechanistically distinct senolytic and senomorphic strategies.

From: "Cellular senescence and SASP in tumor progression and therapeutic opportunities" (Dong et al., Molecular Cancer, 2024)

Figure 1 · Dong 2024

Factors Inducing Cellular Senescence

Cellular senescence is triggered by a broad range of stressors: oncogene activation, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction, the DNA damage response (DDR), protein homeostasis disorders, and chemoradiotherapy — converging on the p53/p21 and p16/Rb pathways that impose a permanent cell-cycle arrest.
Therapy-induced senescence is directly relevant to oncology: conventional chemo- and radiotherapy can push residual tumor or stroma cells into senescence, seeding a SASP that reshapes the tumor microenvironment.
Source: PMC11365203 ↗
Figure 2 · Dong 2024

Senescence Signaling Pathways

During DNA replication, telomeres progressively shorten, triggering a DDR with double-strand breaks that activate ATM and ATR, leading to CHK2 activation. CHK2 blocks E2F transcription through the p53/p21 and p16/RB pathways, arresting the cell cycle in G1 — and oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) and therapy-induced senescence (TIS) feed into the same DDR/DSB machinery.
The DDR is the common upstream integrator: regardless of the initiating stress, persistent DNA-damage signaling is what locks the senescence arrest in place.
Source: PMC11365203 ↗
Figure 3 · Dong 2024

The Double-Edged Sword of SASP in Tumors

On one side, the SASP enforces cell-cycle arrest, inhibits tumor cell proliferation, and recruits immune cells to clear senescent cells — bolstering immunosurveillance and antigen presentation. On the other side, it promotes tumor cell proliferation, cancer stem cell emergence and EMT, and suppresses immune responses — contributing to tumor progression and immune evasion.
This duality frames the therapeutic question: when should the SASP be amplified (to harness immunosenescence) and when suppressed (to prevent tumor-promoting inflammation)?
Source: PMC11365203 ↗
Figure 4 · Dong 2024

Mechanisms of Senolytics & Senomorphics

Senolytics induce apoptosis in senescent cells by targeting senescent cell anti-apoptotic pathways (SCAPs): MDM2, Bcl-2 family members, HSP90, SA-β-gal, PI3K/AKT, Na+-K+ pumps, FOXO4, and antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) approaches. Senomorphics instead inhibit NF-κB, MAPK, mTOR and Bcl-2 signaling, preventing senescent cells from releasing SASP factors such as IL-6, IL-8 and MMPs.
Senolytics clear the cells (cytotoxic); senomorphics dampen the phenotype (cytostatic on the secretome) — two complementary, mechanistically distinct arms of senotherapy.
Source: PMC11365203 ↗
