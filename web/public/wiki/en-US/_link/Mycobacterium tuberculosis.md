# Mycobacterium tuberculosis

**Mycobacterium tuberculosis** is a slow-growing, obligately aerobic, acid-fast bacterium of the genus *Mycobacterium* and the causative agent of [[Tuberculosis]]. It is the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide, distinguished by a distinctive cell wall that resists host killing and by a chronic granulomatous pathology.

## Overview

- **Biology:** non-motile, non-spore-forming rods 2–4 µm long that do not Gram stain reliably but retain carbol fuchsin after acid-alcohol decolourisation (Ziehl-Neelsen staining). They grow slowly (18–24 h doubling) on egg-based media such as Löwenstein-Jensen, and are aerobic, with a preference for the oxygen-rich apical lung.
- **Genome:** ~4.4 Mb, encoding ~4,000 genes including the ESAT-6 and CFP-10 antigens, the RD1 region encoding the ESX-3 secretion system, and the *mecA*/*mecB* genes for ethambutol resistance. The Beijing lineage, which carries the *mecA* locus, is spreading globally.
- **Human reservoir:** no animal reservoir; transmission is person-to-person via airborne droplet nuclei, so infection control and contact tracing matter.

## Structure & Cell Wall

- **Mycomembrane:** an outer lipid bilayer rich in mycolic acids (long-chain, α-alkylated β-hydroxy fatty acids) with a high degree of α-alkylation and, in *M. tuberculosis*, a covalently attached trehalose dimycolate (TDM, "Cord factor"). This waxy barrier excludes most antibiotics and limits permeability, contributing to intrinsic drug resistance and to persistence.
- **Lipids and glycolipids:** trehalose dimycolate, phthioceranic acids, and sulphated glycolipids such as SL-1 and SL-2 (which inhibit NADPH oxidase and reactive oxygen species) protect against host oxidative killing.
- **Capsule and complement evasion:** the capsule and surface proteins (e.g. PE/PE-PGRS families) resist [[Complement System]]-mediated killing.
- **Induction of dormancy:** the DosR/System DosR regulon drives a hypoxic, non-replicating "latency" state with altered metabolism (shift to cholesterol and fatty-acid utilization, reliance on host lipids), which is the basis of phenotypic drug tolerance and of the latent reservoir.

## Mechanism of Action & Pathways

1. **Entry:** inhaled droplet nuclei reach the alveoli and are phagocytosed by [[Alveolar Macrophages]].
2. **Intracellular survival:** *M. tuberculosis* prevents phagosome–lysosome fusion (via ESX-3-dependent effects on the phagosomal membrane and recruitment of host factors), and blocks reactive oxygen species and acidification. Its survival depends on host cholesterol, which it imports and traffics via the intracellular cholesterol esterification pathway.
3. **Granuloma:** 2–3 weeks later, cell-mediated immunity — CD4+ Th1 cells releasing [[Interferon-gamma|IFN-γ]] and [[TNF-alpha|TNF]] — drives macrophage recruitment and epithelioid conversion with Langhans giant cells, forming the granuloma. TNF is essential for both granuloma integrity and for the chemokine-driven recruitment of infected monocytes.
4. **Caseation:** the granuloma centre becomes hypoxic and necrotic, producing caseous necrosis; liquefaction and erosion into airways yields cavitary, highly infectious disease and spreads bacilli.
5. **Latency:** if containment succeeds, bacilli persist in a walled, low-metabolic state for decades — a dynamic equilibrium, not sterility.
6. **Host-directed effectors:** infected macrophages produce TNF, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-12, activate the [[Inflammasome]] and pyroptosis, and — relevant to ageing biology — bacillus-infected macrophages show impaired [[Autophagy]] flux and mitochondrial dysfunction with excess [[Reactive Oxygen Species|ROS]] and a senescence-like transcriptional programme.

## Clinical Presentation & Biomarkers

- **Pulmonary:** chronic cough, haemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss; apical cavitation on imaging.
- **Extrapulmonary:** lymphadenitis, pleural effusion, meningitis, vertebral (Pott) disease, genitourinary and abdominal involvement; in immunocompromised hosts, disseminated or miliary disease.
- **Biomarkers:** interferon-gamma release assay and tuberculin skin test for infection; sputum culture (gold standard), nucleic-acid amplification tests, and GeneXpert MTB/RIF for rapid diagnosis with rifampicin-resistance detection; chest radiography for disease; baseline liver enzymes and drug susceptibility testing.
- **Resistance:** multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB and XDR-TB) reflect mutations in rpoB (rifampicin), katG and inhA (isoniazid), gyrA (fluoroquinolones) and mabA/fabG1.

## Therapeutic Landscape

- **Standard regimen:** for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB, a four-drug regimen (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) for two months followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for four months, under directly observed therapy.
- **Latent infection:** isoniazid for 6–9 months, or shorter rifamycin-based regimens; screening for active disease is mandatory first.
- **Host-directed therapy:** the only proven host-directed agent is anti-TNF (e.g. infliximab, adalimumab) in TNF-dependent disease, including paradoxical reactions and rheumatoid arthritis patients with latent TB. Metformin, everolimus, mTOR inhibition, host-acting antimicrobials (e.g. auxotroph-directed 5-aminolevulinic acid in hypoxia-activated designs), and vitamin D adjuncts remain investigational.
- **Resistance:** MDR/RR-TB is treated with bedaquiline, linezolid, levofloxacin/moxifloxacin, clofazimine and injectable agents (amikacin) according to susceptibility; shorter all-oral bedaquiline-based regimens have improved outcomes.
- **Prevention:** BCG vaccination in infancy protects against severe childhood forms; contact screening, infection control and rapid treatment of source cases remain the highest-yield interventions.
