# Tissue Factor

**Tissue factor (TF, factor III, CD142)** is a type I single-pass transmembrane glycoprotein and the primary cellular initiator of the extrinsic (tissue factor) coagulation pathway. Exposed TF binds circulating factor VIIa to form the TF–FVIIa complex, which activates factor X and factor IX, generating thrombin and a fibrin clot.

## Overview

Tissue factor (factor III) is the cell-surface receptor-cofactor that initiates the extrinsic coagulation cascade. It is the only coagulation protein with a transmembrane domain, and it is normally sequestered beneath the endothelium; its exposure, or its aberrant expression on cells and microparticles, is the single common trigger of thrombin generation in haemostasis and in disease.

## Structure & Domains

- **Gene:** *F3* on chromosome 1p21; alternative splicing yields a soluble form. TF is the only coagulation factor with a cellular transmembrane domain and an extracellular "tissue" presence in addition to a circulating soluble pool.
- **Three domains:** an N-terminal extracellular domain homologous to fibroblast growth factor, a single transmembrane helix, and a short C-terminal cytoplasmic tail.
- **Post-translational modification:** N-linked glycosylation and, crucially, **disulfide-dependent homodimerization** of the TF–FVIIa complex, which markedly enhances procoagulant activity.
- **Expression:** abundant in subendothelial smooth muscle and pericytes, fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes; induced on [[Monocytes]] and [[Endothelial Cells]] by TNF, IL-1 and thrombin (via PAR1) and by tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) depletion; ectopically expressed by many tumours.

## Mechanism of Action & Pathways

1. TF binds factor VIIa (its cofactor) with high affinity, forming the TF–FVIIa complex.
2. The complex activates factor X (to Xa) and factor IX (to IXa, with cofactor VIIIa); Xa with Va converts prothrombin to thrombin.
3. Thrombin cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin, activates factor XIII to XIIIa for cross-linking, and activates [[Platelets]] via the PAR receptor.
4. **Amplification loops:** thrombin and TF feed back to increase TF expression (PAR1 signalling), and Xa/TF–FVIIa activity is buffered by tissue factor pathway inhibitor, [[Antithrombin III]] and [[Factor XIIIa]].
5. **Signalling beyond coagulation:** TF ligation activates PAR2 (PAR1/FVIIa) → MAPK, PI3K–AKT and calcium signalling, driving [[Inflammation]], [[Cellular Senescence]] induction and, in tumours, pro-invasive gene expression.

## Physiological Function

TF initiates haemostasis only where tissue injury exposes subendothelial TF — normally covered by intact endothelium. Its co-localization with tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) on endothelium sets the threshold: TF exposed without adequate TFPI produces thrombin and fibrin. TF also functions as a signal receptor on cells that do not participate in coagulation, mediating pro-inflammatory and pro-migratory gene expression (senescence-associated secretory phenotype induction, chemokine production).

## Pathology & Clinical Relevance

- **Thrombosis:** TF exposure on disrupted plaques, on cancer cells, on microparticles and on circulating TF-bearing monocytes drives arterial and venous thrombosis. TF-containing microparticles are elevated in sepsis, trauma and COVID-like hypercoagulable states and contribute to fibrin deposition and organ injury.
- **Cancer:** tumour cells express TF, which promotes both tumour-associated thrombosis and TF-mediated pro-invasive signalling, angiogenesis and metastatic seeding. TF expression is a marker of aggressive disease in breast, pancreatic and lung cancer; the PAR2 axis is a target in [[Tumor Microenvironment]] biology.
- **Inflammation and sepsis:** monocyte TF drives disseminated intravascular coagulation; TF blockade improves outcomes in experimental sepsis and is explored in viral pneumonitis.
- **Diagnostics/therapy:** soluble TF and TF-bearing microparticles are candidate biomarkers. Direct TF inhibitors (e.g. anti-TF antibodies, TFPI mimetics) and factor VIIa inhibitors are in development for thrombosis and cancer-associated coagulopathy; anticoagulation in cancer reduces thrombosis without compromising tumour control.
