# Gaucher Disease

**Gaucher disease (GD)** is the most common lysosomal storage disorder, first described by Philippe Gaucher in 1882 and the first LSD traced to a single enzyme deficiency. It is autosomal recessive and caused by deficiency or absence of the lysosomal enzyme **[[Glucocerebrosidase]]** (acid beta-glucosidase, *GBA1*, EC 3.2.1.45), leading to accumulation of **glucosylceramide** and glucosylsphingosine in tissue monocyte-macrophages — the lipid-laden "Gaucher cells." GD1 (non-neuronopathic) accounts for >90–95% of cases, with a prevalence of roughly 1/20,000–1/40,000; types 2 and 3 are ~1/100,000 each. The commonest causative allele, *N370S*, is associated with type 1 and is frequent in Ashkenazi Jewish and non-Jewish European populations.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

- **The enzymatic block.** Glucocerebrosidase normally hydrolyses glucosylceramide to ceramide and glucose in the lysosome. Its deficiency causes substrate accumulation in the monocyte-macrophage lineage, and it is this accumulation — not a primary defect in any other tissue — that drives most pathology. Gaucher cells have lipid-laden "crumpled tissue paper" cytoplasm.
- **Pathophysiology beyond macrophages.** GD involvement in bone, lung, immune and CNS is not fully explained by macrophage infiltration alone, which is the stated rationale for therapies reaching beyond that cell type.
- **Gaucher cells and their consequences:** hepatosplenomegaly and hypersplenism producing anaemia and thrombocytopenia; bone marrow infiltration; osteopenia and osteonecrosis with Erlanger's "Erlenmeyer flask deformity" of the distal femur; pulmonary hypertension in some; elevated globulins; a substrate flux through the monocyte/macrophage system also implicated in multiple myeloma, other haematological malignancy, and pulmonary hypertension.
- **Genotype–phenotype is imperfect.** Type 1 disease with *N370S*/*N370S* is the commonest mild genotype, but genotype alone does not reliably predict phenotype, and phenotypic overlap between types occurs — which complicates definitive classification and is why clinical judgement and biomarkers remain necessary.

**Clinical types:**

| Type | Onset | Course |
|---|---|---|
| **1 (non-neuronopathic)** | Adulthood or late childhood | Chronic, 95% of cases; splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, cytopenias, bone disease; normal life expectancy with treatment |
| **2 (acute neuronopathic)** | First year of life | CNS involvement early, progressive, death in infancy |
| **3 (chronic neuronopathic)** | Childhood | CNS involvement later; oculomotor apraxia, myoclonus, ataxia, seizures, calcification of basal ganglia and aortic root |

## Biomarkers

- **Chitotriosidase** (plasma), **CCL18/PARC**, **glucosylsphingosine (lyso-Gb1)** and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase — chitotriosidase is the classical biomarker, though its interpretation is complicated by the common *CHIT1* polymorphism that produces an undetectable enzyme.
- Biocchemical response tracks clinical response: in one retrospective series, median chitotriosidase fell 89% with eliglustat, 88% with ERT, and only 37% with miglustat in naive patients after 2 years.
- Response is assessed on haematological (haemoglobin, platelets), visceral (liver and spleen volume), skeletal (bone mineral density, marrow infiltration) and patient-reported axes.

## Therapeutic Landscape

> [!important] Two approved modalities
> **Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT)** supplies the missing enzyme: **imiglucerase**, **velaglucerase alfa** and **taliglucerase alfa**, all intravenous, typically every other week for life. ERT markedly improves cytopenias and hepatosplenomegaly and is the established gold standard, but has substantial cost, infusion burden, does not fully prevent bone complications (focal disease persists in bone and marrow even at 60–120 U/kg), and penetrates the CNS poorly. ERT is ineffective in type 2 and of limited efficacy in type 3.
>
> **Substrate reduction therapy (SRT)** instead throttles the upstream flux by inhibiting glucosylceramide synthase, the rate-limiting step of glycosphingolipid synthesis — feasible only because patients retain some residual enzyme activity. **Eliglustat** (ceramide analogue, IC50 ~0.024 µM) is potent and specific, oral, and does not inhibit intestinal glycosidases — hence far fewer GI side effects than **miglustat** (IC50 5–50 µM; GI complaints in up to 80%, plus tremor, weight loss, peripheral neuropathy). Eliglustat was **non-inferior to imiglucerase** in the phase 3 ENCORE trial of patients stabilised on ERT, and the FDA approved it as first-line for adults with GD1 who are CYP2D6 extensive, intermediate or poor metabolisers (>90% of the population).
> Eliglustat dosing depends on CYP2D6 genotype (84 mg twice daily for extensive/intermediate, once daily for poor metabolisers); ultra-rapid and indeterminate metabolisers are ineligible. It is not recommended with pre-existing cardiac disease, long QT syndrome, class IA/IIIA antiarrhythmics, or strong CYP3A inducers. Eliglustat is actively effluxed from the CNS by P-glycoprotein, so it has little utility in neuronopathic GD — whereas miglustat does cross the blood–brain barrier, with contested effects on type 3 neurology.

> [!warning] Unmet needs
> No approved therapy exists for type 2 GD; type 3 has no disease-modifying drug. ERT is expensive and lifelong. Predicting phenotype from genotype, measuring progression with objective biomarkers, and choosing between ERT and SRT per individual remain open problems.

## Broader Biology

Gaucher disease is one of the strongest genetic risk factors for [[Parkinson's Disease]]: GBA1 variants (heterozygous carriers, GD1 included) are the most common genetic risk factor for PD, and biallelic GD1 associates with a substantially increased PD risk. This connects Gaucher biology to [[Alpha-synuclein]] accumulation, [[Neurodegeneration]] and to the broader lysosomal-trafficking and [[Autophagy]] failure hypotheses of PD pathogenesis. GD1 also shows [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial]] and [[Inflammation|inflammatory]] involvement, and Gaucher cells sit within the wider family of [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases]] and [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases]].

## Connections
- [[Glucocerebrosidase]] — the deficient enzyme itself; all of GD's primary pathophysiology follows from its loss, and the enzyme is also a wild-type PD risk factor, which is why Gaucher sits at the centre of the lysosomal–Parkinson's intersection.
- [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases]] — GD is the prototype and most common member of this class, providing the general model of substrate accumulation in a single cell type producing multisystem disease.
- [[Lysosomal Storage Diseases]] — the vault's larger cross-cutting note on the LSD family, into which GD's substrate-accumulation logic, biomarkers and therapy-development lessons generalise.
- [[Lysosome]] — the organelle in which glucocerebrosidase acts and glucosylceramide accumulates; the shared machinery that connects GD to Niemann-Pick, Fabry and Pompe disease.
- [[Macrophages]] — the cell type that becomes the Gaucher cell; the monocyte-macrophage restriction of storage explains why hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias and marrow disease dominate the non-neuronopathic phenotype.
- Spleen — hepatosplenomegaly with hypersplenism is a hallmark, and splenectomy (once a common intervention) is a marker of severe disease.
- [[Liver]] — hepatomegaly is a cardinal feature and one of the primary response endpoints in treatment trials.
- [[Bone Marrow]] — infiltration causes cytopenias and contributes to the characteristic skeletal complications, including Erlenmeyer flask deformity.
- [[Anemia]] and [[Thrombocytopenia]] — the two cytopenias that define the haematological phenotype and drive treatment decisions.
- [[Ceramide]] — the immediate product of glucosylceramide hydrolysis and the structural template on which eliglustat is designed as a competitive ceramide analogue.
- [[Acid ceramidase]] — a neighbouring lysosomal sphingolipid enzyme; acid ceramidase deficiency (Farber disease) is the differential diagnosis for isolated sphingolipid storage in bone and joint tissue.
- [[Parkinson's Disease]] — GD1 and GBA1 carrier status are the strongest known genetic PD risk factors, making Gaucher a neurodegeneration-adjacent lysosomal disease.
- [[Alpha-synuclein]] — the PD protein whose accumulation is linked to GBA1 genotype, giving the Gaucher–PD relationship a protein-level as well as a clinical handle.
- [[Autophagy]] — lysosomal clearance failure underlies protein aggregation across neurodegeneration, and Gaucher's PD link is usually framed through lysosomal trafficking and autophagy deficits.
