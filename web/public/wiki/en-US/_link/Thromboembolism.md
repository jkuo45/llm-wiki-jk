# Thromboembolism

**Thromboembolism** is the obstruction of a blood vessel by a **thrombus** that has broken away from its site of formation and travelled through the bloodstream, lodging at a site where it occludes flow, causes ischemia, and inflicts organ damage. It is distinguished from **thrombosis**, in which the clot forms and stays where it formed, and from **embolism** generally, which can also be caused by fat, air, amniotic fluid, atheromatous debris or tumour.

> [!info] The two great divisions
> - **Venous thromboembolism (VTE)** — deep vein thrombosis of the legs and pelvis, with pulmonary embolism as the lethal complication. Arises from **stasis, hypercoagulability and endothelial injury** (Virchow's triad), and accounts for the large majority of thromboembolic disease.
> - **Arterial thromboembolism** — stroke and myocardial infarction, almost always from an **atherosclerotic plaque** rupture or erosion, less often from a cardiac source (atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, mural thrombus, infective endocarditis) or from a large artery. Arterial clots form on flowing blood, so they are platelet-rich, tightly adherent, and pale.
>
> The distinction is not cosmetic: arterial emboli lodge in arteries and produce infarction, and are managed with antiplatelet therapy; venous emboli lodge in the pulmonary circulation and are managed with anticoagulation.

## Venous thromboembolism

**Deep vein thrombosis** typically arises in the calf, popliteal, femoral or iliac veins. The clinical triad of swelling, pain and tenderness with a warm, non-pithed limb is neither sensitive nor specific; phlebothography and compression ultrasonography are the diagnostic standards, and D-dimer is used to rule out DVT in low-risk patients.

**Pulmonary embolism** occurs when thrombus from a leg or pelvic DVT embolises through the right heart into the pulmonary arteries. It presents with sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia and hypoxaemia, and can cause sudden death within minutes. Massive (high-risk) PE — defined by haemodynamic instability — carries mortality of 30–50% and is the one thromboembolic emergency requiring thrombolysis.

**Risk factors.** Prolonged immobility (surgery, hospitalisation, long-haul flight, plaster immobilisation), prior VTE, malignancy and chemotherapy, pregnancy and the puerperium, oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy, obesity, age >65, thrombophilia, and nephrotic syndrome. In hospitalised medical patients, VTE causes a large share of preventable iatrogenic harm, which is why risk assessment scores and prophylaxis are quality metrics.

**Inherited thrombophilias**: factor V Leiden (activated protein C resistance, commonest), prothrombin G20210A, and deficiencies of antithrombin III, protein C and protein S, and dysfibrinogenaemia. These are worth testing for only in selected presentations (younger than 50, recurrent, or strong family history) — routine screening is not recommended and can cause harm through unnecessary lifelong anticoagulation.

## Arterial thromboembolism

**Stroke** is the archetype. Emboli arise from:
- **Carotid atherosclerotic plaque** — the commonest cause of large-artery ischaemic stroke.
- **Atrial fibrillation** — stasis in the left atrial appendage produces emboli; [[Atrial Fibrillation]] is the single most important preventable cause of embolic stroke and the indication for anticoagulation.
- **Cardiac sources** — recent myocardial infarction with mural thrombus, prosthetic and rheumatic valves, infective and non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, atrial myxoma.
- **Aortic atheroma** and aortic dissection.

**Myocardial infarction** in the great majority of cases is atherothrombotic, though embolisation from a proximal aortic source and coronary embolism account for a minority.

## Virchow's triad, restated mechanistically

All the above reduce to three variables:

1. **Endothelial injury** — surgical or traumatic endothelial disruption, atherosclerotic plaque rupture, smoking, hypertension, vasculitis, homocysteinaemia, sepsis. Endothelium normally expresses thrombomodulin, [[Nitric Oxide]], prostacyclin and heparan sulfate, all antithrombotic; injury removes that shield.
2. **Abnormal blood flow** — stasis in the venous sinuses of the calf, the left atrial appendiceal cavity, and aneurysmal and dilated chambers. Turbulence at bifurcations and damaged valves is the arterial analogue.
3. **Hypercoagulability (thrombophilia)** — genetic (factor V Leiden), acquired (malignancy, antiphospholipid syndrome, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, hyperhomocysteinaemia, liver disease, dehydration and haemoconcentration, pregnancy), or drug-related (oestrogens, tamoxifen, chemotherapy, erythropoietin and other erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, antipsychotics).

The prothrombotic state of malignancy is now formalised in the Khorana score, which stratifies VTE risk by cancer type, stage and chemotherapy exposure, and which is used to guide extended anticoagulation after cancer treatment.

## Management

**Anticoagulation is the default for VTE.** Options are:
- **Low-molecular-weight [[Heparin]]** and unfractionated heparin — parenteral, fast onset, reversible; preferred over warfarin when cancer-associated and in pregnancy. Unfractionated heparin is preferred in renal failure and unstable patients because it is not renally cleared. [[Antithrombin III]] potentiates heparin, which is why heparin resistance occurs in AT deficiency and is corrected by AT supplementation.
- **Direct oral anticoagulants** (factor Xa inhibitors and dabigatran) — oral, no routine monitoring, and now the preferred option for most non-cancer VTE.
- **[[Warfarin]]** — a vitamin K antagonist requiring INR monitoring, with a narrow therapeutic window and a large interaction and dietary-warfarin problem. It remains necessary where a mechanical valve is present, and it is the agent underlying the long-lived plasma-binding story the warfarin note covers.
- **Thrombolysis** for massive PE; **thrombectomy** or **catheter-directed therapy** for selected patients.
- **Inferior vena cava filters** when anticoagulation is contraindicated — complication rates with modern retrievable filters are low, but they carry their own access and thrombosis risks.

**Antiplatelet therapy** (aspirin, clopidogrel, and dual antiplatelet therapy) is the correct primary and secondary prevention for arterial thromboembolism — stroke and myocardial infarction — and is *not* adequate treatment for VTE.

> [!warning] Clinical caveats
> - **DOACs and antiphospholipid syndrome** — DOACs are associated with higher arterial event rates than warfarin in triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome; warfarin is preferred there. This is a genuine, clinically important exception.
> - **Extended prophylaxis in cancer** — extended anticoagulation (up to 6–12 months) improves outcomes in cancer-associated VTE, but confers bleeding risk, and decisions must be individualised.
> - **Bleeding is the price.** Every anticoagulant trades thrombosis for haemorrhage, and major bleeding in anticoagulated patients is frequently life-threatening. Anticoagulation is a decision about which risk you prefer.
> - **Prophylaxis vs. treatment are not the same evidence.** Effective prophylaxis in hospitalised patients does not mean effective treatment of an established clot at the same dose.

## Connections

- [[Heparin]] — Heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin are the default acute treatment for venous thromboembolism and the standard of care in cancer-associated and pregnancy-associated VTE. Its antithrombin dependence is also why heparin resistance is a clinical signal of antithrombin deficiency or a marker of high inflammatory and antithrombin-consumptive states.
- [[Warfarin]] — Warfarin is the long-acting oral anticoagulant for VTE and remains mandatory in mechanical heart valves and antiphospholipid syndrome. Its narrow index of therapeutic range makes it the source of much iatrogenic bleeding, which is the main motivation for the direct oral anticoagulants.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — Thromboembolism is the indication that defines the entire anticoagulant class. Everything in that note — mechanism, monitoring, reversal, drug interactions — is downstream of the clinical question this note addresses.
- [[Antithrombin III]] — Antithrombin III is the physiological inhibitor of thrombin and factor Xa, and the cofactor that heparin potentiates. Its deficiency is an inherited thrombophilia, so it sits at the intersection of clot risk and the drug used to treat it.
- [[Prothrombin]] — Prothrombin is the precursor of thrombin, the enzyme that makes fibrin; vitamin K-dependent prothrombin is why warfarin works at all, and prothrombin time/INR is the warfarin monitoring assay.
- [[Thrombin]] — Thrombin is the effector enzyme of coagulation and anticoagulation alike. It is a procoagulant when acting on fibrinogen and an anticoagulant when bound to thrombomodulin, which is why it is the target of essentially every anticoagulant class.
- [[Platelet]] — Platelets dominate the *arterial* limb of thromboembolism: plaque rupture, platelet adhesion and aggregation, and the efficacy of antiplatelet rather than anticoagulant therapy. The platelet-rich, tightly adherent character of arterial clots is why they embolise occlusively.
- [[Arterial Thrombosis]] — Arterial thrombosis and arterial thromboembolism are the same pathology viewed at the formation site and the landing site, and they are managed with the same antiplatelet agents. This note separates them; that note should be read alongside it.
- [[Pulmonary Embolism]] — Pulmonary embolism is the lethal endpoint of venous thromboembolism and the reason VTE prophylaxis is a hospital quality metric. It is also the indication where thrombolysis is indicated and where delay is measured in minutes.
- [[Stroke]] — Cardioembolic and atherothrombotic stroke are the major arterial thromboembolic presentations, and stroke prevention is anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation and antiplatelet therapy for atherothrombotic disease.
- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] — Atrial fibrillation is a stasis-driven source of arterial emboli and the leading indication for long-term oral anticoagulation to prevent stroke. The CHA₂DS₂-VASc score converts AF stroke risk into a treatment decision.
- [[Hypertension]] — Hypertension is a major risk factor for arterial thromboembolism (stroking, plaque rupture) and for the vascular endothelial injury that underlies it; it is also the reason some anticoagulants and anti-VEGF agents affect blood pressure.
- [[Inflammation]] — Inflammation and coagulation are a single intertwined system: IL-6 and tissue factor, neutrophil extracellular traps, and the loss of anticoagulant endothelial phenotype in sepsis. The C-reactive protein / fibrinogen relationship is a longstanding clinical marker of this coupling.
- [[Cancer]] — Malignancy is one of the strongest acquired VTE risk factors, via tissue factor expression, chemotherapy, indwelling catheters and tumour-related stasis. Khorana risk stratification and extended-duration anticoagulation in cancer are direct clinical consequences.
- Inflammation-driven prothrombotic state is one route by which innate immune activation contributes to thrombosis, independent of classical Virchow triad. The link is real but mechanistically less settled than the classical triad.
- [[Nitric Oxide]] — Endothelial nitric oxide is a direct antithrombotic: it inhibits platelet activation and smooth muscle proliferation. Endothelial dysfunction in the atherosclerotic plaque is a principal reason the surface becomes thrombogenic.
- Atherosclerosis — Atherosclerotic plaque rupture and erosion is the substrate for most arterial thromboembolism, making atherosclerosis prevention the primary approach to arterial events.
- - Dehydration — Haemoconcentration and reduced flow raise VTE risk, which is why dehydration is a routine precipitant in clinical risk assessments and a common reason for VTE in heatwaves and endurance events.
Pregnancy — The prothrombotic state of pregnancy and the puerperium is a major driver of VTE in women of reproductive age, and it is also the situation in which [[Heparin]] rather than warfarin is used, because warfarin is teratogenic.
