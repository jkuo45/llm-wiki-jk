# Foodborne disease

Foodborne disease covers any illness acquired through ingestion of contaminated
food or drink. It is not one disease but a syndrome category spanning bacterial
infection, viral infection, parasitic infection, intoxication by preformed
toxin, and toxin-mediated syndromes such as botulism. Its unifying feature is
the **oral route of entry and a food vehicle**, which is what makes it
preventable by a small number of well-understood control points.

## Burden

WHO estimates put the global burden at approximately **866 million illnesses
(circa 1 in 9 people) and 1.5 million deaths per year**, with children under
five bearing nearly a third of cases and about 57 million healthy life-years
lost. Older estimates of 600 million cases and 420,000 deaths circulated widely
for years and are now superseded; the discrepancy is a reminder to check the
vintage of any burden figure quoted.

The CDC's most recent US estimate (emerging infections 2025, for illnesses
circa 2019) gives, for seven major pathogens:

| Pathogen | Illnesses | Hospitalisations | Deaths |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Norovirus | 5,540,000 | 22,400 | 174 |
| Campylobacter spp. | 1,870,000 | 13,000 | 197 |
| Nontyphoidal Salmonella | 1,280,000 | 12,500 | 238 |
| Clostridium perfringens | 889,000 | 338 | 41 |
| Shiga toxin-producing E. coli | 357,000 | 3,150 | 66 |
| Toxoplasma gondii | (not estimated) | 848 | 44 |
| Invasive Listeria monocytogenes | 1,250 | 1,070 | 172 |

> [!info] Case counts and severity diverge sharply
> The ranking by illness count (norovirus, Campylobacter) is not the ranking by
> severity. *Listeria* causes about 1,250 US illnesses per year but 1,070
> hospitalisations - a hospitalisation rate above 80% - because infection in
> older, pregnant, and immunocompromised hosts is frequently fatal. Salmonella
> causes the most deaths. This is why food-safety policy is not organised by
> incidence.

## Principal agents and their vehicles

**Bacteria**

- *Salmonella enterica* (nontyphoidal) - eggs, poultry, meat, unpasteurised
  milk and juice. Also a leading cause of reactive arthritis and (a subset)
  Guillain-Barre syndrome as post-infectious sequelae.
- *Campylobacter jejuni* - undercooked poultry, unpasteurised milk, untreated
  water. A leading antecedent of Guillain-Barre syndrome.
- Shiga toxin-producing *Escherichia coli* (STEC, including O157:H7) - ground
  beef, leafy greens, raw sprouts; causes haemolytic uraemic syndrome, a
  leading cause of acute kidney injury in children.
- *Listeria monocytogenes* - deli meats, soft cheeses, ready-to-eat foods;
  survives refrigeration, causes meningitis, and causes stillbirth.
- *Clostridium perfringens* - meat and rice left at ambient temperature;
  causes food service-associated outbreaks with very low attack rates but large
  numbers of exposed people.
- *Vibrio* species (including *V. parahaemolyticus* in raw oysters and *V.
  vulnificus* in seafood) - note *V. vulnificus* causes disproportionate
  sepsis in iron-overloaded patients and those with liver disease.
- *Bacillus cereus* and *Staphylococcus aureus* - preformed emetic and enterotoxin
  syndromes with incubation under 6 hours and no infectious period at all.
- *Shigella* and *Campylobacter* are the main causes of dysentery and
  malnutrition in children in high-burden settings.

**Viruses** - norovirus dominates by case count and is the leading cause of
acute gastroenteritis outbreaks in closed institutions; it is highly
contagious, environmentally hardy, and is the classic cause of cruise ship and
care-home outbreaks. Hepatitis A is a food- and shellfish-borne viral hepatitis
that is largely preventable by vaccination.

**Parasites** - the foodborne parasitic diseases are largely helminthic and
zoonotic: *Trichuris trichiura* (whipworm) and *Ascaris lumbricoides* from
contaminated produce and soil; *Taenia* species from undercooked pork or beef;
*Gnathostoma spinigerum* from raw freshwater fish, which causes
[[Gnathostomiasis]] and is the vault's specific example of a foodborne
parasite; [[Strongyloides]] from contaminated soil; and *Toxoplasma gondii* from
undercooked meat or cat-associated contamination, which causes
encephalitis in the immunocompromised and congenital disease in pregnancy.

> [!info] Raw fish and the anisakiasis principle
> The [[Gnathostomiasis|gnathostomiasis]] case illustrates why "foodborne
> parasite" is a mechanism category and not a species: Anisakis, Clonorchis,
> Opisthorchis, Diphyllobothrium, and Gnathostoma share the same route
> (raw or undercooked fish, crustacean, or watercress) and the same
> pathophysiological template - a larval helminth that penetrates the gastric or
> intestinal wall and then migrates through tissue. The shared biology is
> [[Nematode]] and [[Cestode|helminth]] eosinophil recruitment, not the
> organism.

**Preformed toxins and mycotoxins** - botulism from home-canned food,
scombroid and ciguatera poisoning from fish, and mycotoxicoses such as
aflatoxin B1 exposure from mould-contaminated crops, which is a major
hepatocarcinogen.

## Why the burden persists

Foodborne disease is a failure of five specific control points, and each maps
to a distinct group of pathogens:

1. **Contamination of raw ingredients** - soil, manure, and water (nematodes,
   *E. coli*, *Campylobacter*).
2. **Survival of organisms in food** - heat-resistant spores survive
   inadequate cooking (*C. perfringens*, *Bacillus cereus*).
3. **Growth after cooking** - improper holding temperatures in food service is
   the single most common outbreak setting.
4. **Contamination after cooking** - the cooked-food pathway, where
   already-cooked food is handled by an infected handler, is the classic
   norovirus route.
5. **Failure of the host barrier** - *Listeria*, *Toxoplasma*,
   *Campylobacter*, and STEC cause severe disease predominantly in the
   elderly, pregnant, and immunocompromised.

> [!warning] Clinical caveats
> - Most cases are never laboratory-confirmed. CDC estimates put laboratory
>   confirmation for the major pathogens at under 10%, and for norovirus far
>   below that. Stool culture will not identify the majority of acute
>   gastroenteritis, and empiric antibiotic therapy for a self-limited watery
>   illness is not indicated.
> - **Antibiotic treatment is not always the right answer.** Shiga toxin-producing
>   *E. coli* is made worse by antibiotics, which appear to increase toxin
>   release and the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome.
> - **Chronic carriage is a distinct clinical problem.** Non-typhoidal
>   *Salmonella* causes a self-limited gastroenteritis in most patients but
>   persistent colonisation of the gallbladder in a substantial minority,
>   transmitting infection to others for years. Typhoid is the extreme case.
> - **Underreporting is severe** and all burden estimates - including the WHO
>   figures above - rest on models with wide credible intervals, not on
>   surveillance counts.

## The microbiome and foodborne disease

Foodborne illness is a disturbance of [[Microbiome]] ecology as much as an
infection. Antibiotics used to treat enteric infection further deplete the
colonic flora, and the resulting [[Dysbiosis]] is associated with carriage
persistence and with post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome. Probiotics and
fermentation are the corresponding protective interventions, and the link
between diet, gut flora composition, and colonisation resistance is now a
substantive field rather than a hypothesis.

## Connections

- [[Gnathostomiasis]] — the vault's worked example: a zoonotic nematode
  acquired by eating raw freshwater fish, with tissue-migrating larvae and
  marked eosinophilia, making it the bridge between foodborne disease and
  helminth immunology.
- [[Strongyloidiasis]] — acquired from soil-contaminated food or water, and
  notable for its potential to hyperinfect, which is a form of
  foodborne-adjacent transmission worth separating from classic foodborne
  illness.
- [[Ascaris]] and [[Trichuris]] — the two soil-transmitted helminths with the
  largest global foodborne burden, transmitted through contaminated produce.
- [[Salmonella enterica]] — the single most important bacterial foodborne agent
  globally, spanning asymptomatic carriage, gastroenteritis, and invasive
  disease.
- [[Nematode]] — the phylum that dominates foodborne parasitic disease and
  supplies the eosinophil-recruitment mechanism.
- [[Dysbiosis]] and [[Microbiome]] — the ecological framing of foodborne illness
  and the reason post-infectious states can persist.
- [[Inflammation]] — the shared downstream mechanism, from epithelial
  cytokine release to systemic inflammatory response in severe cases.
- [[Eosinophils]] — recruited by migrating helminth larvae, the defining
  host response in the foodborne parasitic diseases.
- [[Antibiotic resistance]] — driven substantially by agricultural antibiotic
  use and by the enteric reservoir that foodborne pathogens provide.
- [[Hepatitis A]] — the foodborne viral hepatitis, largely vaccine-preventable.
