# Verapamil

**Verapamil** is a phenylalkylamine-class calcium channel blocker and class IV antiarrhythmic that blocks L-type calcium channels in the heart and vasculature. It is used for supraventricular tachycardia, atrial flutter/fibrillation rate control, and hypertension. Its second, clinically important pharmacology is inhibition of P-glycoprotein, and its third is its interference with mitochondrial calcium handling — the basis of both its chemosensitisation activity and its narrow therapeutic index.

## Overview

- **Class:** phenylalkylamine (alongside diltiazem) among the dihydropyridines (nifedipine, amlodipine). Chemically it is a tertiary amine, a tertiary butyl ester and a veratryl group.
- **Formulations:** immediate-release oral, extended-release (including a slow-release gastrointestinal-transit-dependent formulation), and intravenous for acute termination of supraventricular tachycardia.
- **Stereochemistry:** the R-enantiomer is the active calcium channel blocker; the S-enantiomer contributes to blockade of P-glycoprotein at lower potency but similar clinical weight, so both enantiomers matter for multidrug resistance reversal.

## Chemical Properties & Classification

- **Chemistry:** a substituted phenylalkylamine; molecular weight ~454.9 (free base), formula C27H38N2O4. Structurally it combines a 3,4-dimethoxyphenyl group (which confers vasodilator activity), a nitrophenyl group (a weak calcium channel blocker in its own right), a tertiary aminoethyl group (responsible for the nodal and negative inotropic actions and for the P-glycoprotein interaction), and a veratryl group.
- **Salt forms:** the hydrochloride is the standard oral preparation; an intravenous formulation and several extended-release oral formulations exist.
- **Related agents:** diltiazem shares the phenylalkylamine class; amlodipine, nifedipine and other dihydropyridines belong to the same therapeutic class but differ in vascular versus cardiac selectivity.

## Pharmacodynamics & Pharmacokinetics

- **Calcium channel block:** preferentially blocks L-type (Cav1.2) channels in nodal tissue and vascular smooth muscle. At therapeutic levels it reduces the phase 4 depolarization slope in the AV node (negative dromotropic), slows SA node rate (negative chronotropic) and decreases myocardial contractility (negative inotropic), while producing vasodilation via reduced calcium-dependent smooth muscle contraction. The AV nodal effect is the basis of its use in AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia and for rate control.
- **P-glycoprotein inhibition:** verapamil competes for and inhibits the ABCB1 efflux pump, reversing multidrug resistance in tumour cells and increasing exposure to substrate drugs such as doxorubicin, vinblastine and paclitaxel. It is also a classic probe for MDR1/P-gp activity in pharmacology.
- **Mitochondrial calcium effects:** by inhibiting plasma-membrane L-type channels and by direct action on the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, verapamil reduces mitochondrial calcium uptake through the [[Mitochondrial calcium uniporter]]. Lower matrix calcium protects against permeability transition and improves mitochondrial function; higher matrix calcium favors permeability transition and cell death. This biphasic, dose- and context-dependent behaviour underlies both its cardioprotective use in ischaemia and its cytotoxicity in combination with chemotherapy.
- **Pharmacokinetics:** extensively metabolised by CYP3A4 and CYP1A2 (notably N-dealkylation to norverapamil, which retains activity), with a large volume of distribution and first-pass metabolism. Bioavailability is ~10% for regular oral verapamil (an exception among calcium channel blockers) and ~90–100% for the extended-release formulation. Elimination is hepatic, so exposure rises in hepatic impairment; dose reduction is needed in the elderly and in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, where it can worsen congestion.
- **Interactions:** strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, azoles, grapefruit) and P-gp inhibitors raise levels; beta blockers, digoxin (P-gp-mediated increase in digoxin levels) and statins add risk of bradycardia or myopathy.

## Applications

- **Supraventricular arrhythmia:** acute IV conversion of AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia; rate control in atrial fibrillation; prevention of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia.
- **Hypertension:** blood-pressure control, particularly in pregnancy (with labetalol or nifedipine) and where beta blockers are contraindicated.
- **Cluster headache:** high-dose oral verapamil is a preventive mainstay; requires ECG monitoring for slow AV conduction.
- **Oncology:** as a P-glycoprotein inhibitor to restore chemotherapy sensitivity in multidrug-resistant tumours (usually investigational or off-label); also studied for radiosensitization and for overcoming tumour hypoxia-driven resistance.
- **Other:** off-label use for cluster headache prophylaxis, migraine prophylaxis, and hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy in selected patients (with care, as it can worsen obstruction).

## Adverse Effects & Toxicology

- **Cardiac:** bradycardia, AV block, worsened heart failure, hypotension, and — with rapid IV administration — severe hypotension or asystole.
- **Neurological:** headache, dizziness, constipation (a characteristic and often dose-limiting effect via inhibition of gut smooth muscle calcium-dependent contraction).
- **Overdose:** severe hypotension, conduction block, and metabolic acidosis; management is supportive with calcium, vasopressors and pacing; [[Calcium]] administration reverses the channel blockade.
- [[Arrhythmias]] and [[Heart Failure]] are the principal clinical risks in patients with conduction disease or reduced ejection fraction.
