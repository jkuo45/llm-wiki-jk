# Deep Vein Thrombosis

**Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)** is formation of a thrombus in a deep vein of an extremity — usually the calf or thigh — or the pelvis. It is the primary cause of pulmonary embolism and, together with PE, forms venous thromboembolism (VTE), which affects roughly 1–2 per 1,000 people per year (300,000–600,000 events annually in the United States) and contributes an estimated 60,000–100,000 deaths each year. About one third of patients with a new VTE diagnosis have accompanying PE. Untreated, DVT propagates proximally and can embolise.

## Etiology & Pathophysiology

**Virchow's triad** (1856) compresses the contributing factors into stasis, vessel-wall injury, and hypercoagulability. Stasis is the most consequential of the three alone, but stasis combined with either of the others greatly raises risk.

- **Stasis** — prolonged immobility, surgery, trauma, pregnancy, congestive heart failure, obesity, varicose veins, advancing age. DVT characteristically begins at low-flow sites: the soleal sinuses and behind venous valve cusps.
- **Endothelial injury/dysfunction** — trauma, surgery, prior DVT. Endothelial activation drives local cytokine production and leukocyte adhesion.
- **Hypercoagulability** — the balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant systems tips as the procoagulant-to-anticoagulant protein ratio rises. Anticoagulant pathways are the protein C/S-thrombomodulin system, the heparin-antithrombin system, and tissue factor inhibitor. Inherited predispositions include factor V Leiden (activated factor Va resistant to protein C inactivation; affects up to 5% of Caucasians with a ~7-fold risk increase), prothrombin 20210, protein C/S and antithrombin deficiency. Acquired causes include cancer, oral contraceptives, pregnancy and hyperinsulinaemia.

> [!info] The anatomy of the thrombus matters
> Venous thrombi are **red** thrombi — relatively platelet-poor, with an inner platelet-rich white core showing **lines of Zahn**, surrounded by an outer red-cell-dense fibrin clot. Fibrin complexed with extracellular DNA and histones forms the outer scaffold and influences susceptibility to tissue plasminogen activator and thrombolysis. Factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and prothrombin are particularly influential in tipping the balance toward coagulation; prothrombin also inhibits activated protein C's anticoagulant function.

> [!warning] DVT is not merely a marker of atherosclerosis
> A correlation between DVT and atherosclerosis has been proposed — shared endothelial dysfunction would predict higher subsequent atherosclerotic risk after DVT — but the mechanistic relationship is contested and the direction of causality is not established. Treat this as an association, not an equivalence.

## Clinical Presentation & Biomarkers

- **Symptoms** are frequently absent or nonspecific: unilateral leg pain, swelling, warmth, erythema; pitting oedema; tenderness along the deep veins; dilated superficial veins. Upper-extremity DVT (catheter-associated) presents similarly in the arm.
- **Wells criteria** quantify pretest probability from symptoms, risk factors, alternative diagnoses and bedside findings.
- **D-dimer** has high sensitivity and poor specificity; a negative result excludes DVT in low- and moderate-probability patients, whereas a positive result requires confirmatory imaging.
- **Imaging:** compression duplex ultrasound is first-line. CT and MR venography extend coverage to pelvis, iliac veins and the chest, and are the modalities of choice where ultrasound is non-diagnostic.

## Therapeutic Landscape

- **Anticoagulation is the cornerstone** — essentially all patients are treated, with isolated distal thrombi without severe symptoms the main exception (requiring serial imaging only).
  - **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) preferred over vitamin K antagonists** for primary treatment of VTE (ASH conditional recommendation, moderate certainty). Rivaroxaban and apixaban need no heparin bridge; dabigatran and edoxaban require 5–10 days of parenteral anticoagulant first. Major bleeding is ~30% lower in relative terms with DOACs than with VKAs.
  - **LMWH preferred** in pregnancy, in severe renal impairment, in triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome, and historically in active malignancy — though apixaban was non-inferior to dalteparin (CARAVAGGIO) for cancer-associated VTE, and DOACs are now often first-line there.
  - **Warfarin** requires a ≥5-day heparin overlap and an INR target of 2.0–3.0; it is the agent of choice for mechanical heart valves, significant renal dysfunction and poor adherence.
- **Thrombolysis** (catheter-directed or systemic) in <5% of patients, reserved for extensive iliofemoral DVT in young (<60 years), low bleeding-risk patients, limb-threatening iliofemoral DVT (phlegmasia cerulea dolens), or life-threatening PE with haemodynamic compromise. Routine thrombolysis added to anticoagulation in proximal DVT is **not** recommended; a catheter-directed trial did not reduce post-thrombotic syndrome versus anticoagulation alone.
- **Inferior vena cava filters** are not recommended in acute DVT; they are for patients with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation and high bleeding risk.
- **Home treatment** is preferred over hospital admission for uncomplicated, low-risk DVT.
- **Prevention:** extended-course prophylaxis with LMWH, fondaparinux, DOACs, aspirin or warfarin in at-risk inpatients; apixaban has the most favourable bleeding profile in network meta-analysis. Reduced UFH use at health-system level is itself an effective HIT-prevention strategy.

> [!warning] Long-term sequelae are common, not rare
> **Post-thrombotic syndrome** develops in 20–50% of patients after DVT and is severe in up to 5%. **Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension** may develop in up to 5% of patients after PE. Recurrence risk after unprovoked VTE is ~10% by 2 years and up to 30% by 10 years — which is why indefinite anticoagulation is recommended for recurrent unprovoked VTE. Anticoagulation is highly effective but is among the highest-risk drug classes in clinical use, with major bleeding in ~1–3% per year on VKAs.

## Connections
- [[Heparin]] — UFH and LMWH are the historical first-line anticoagulants for DVT and the standard bridge to warfarin; the same exposure creates [[Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia]] as a paradoxical complication.
- [[Anticoagulant]] — anticoagulation is the therapeutic cornerstone of DVT, and the class that both prevents propagation to PE and prevents recurrence; the field has shifted from heparin/warfarin to DOACs.
- [[Warfarin]] — the vitamin K antagonist long used for DVT after heparin bridging, now largely superseded by DOACs except in renal failure, mechanical valves, antiphospholipid syndrome and pregnancy-unfit patients.
- [[Pulmonary Embolism]] — the immediately dangerous complication and the reason DVT treatment is urgent; DVT and PE are two manifestations of one disease process.
- [[Fibrinogen]] — the precursor substrate of the fibrin scaffold that gives the venous thrombus its structural and thrombolysis-resistance characteristics.
- [[Prothrombin]] — a key procoagulant factor whose level increases tip the balance toward clotting, and which also inhibits activated protein C's anticoagulant function.
- [[Arterial Thrombosis]] — the arterial counterpart; the mechanisms (endothelial injury, hypercoagulability, reduced flow) overlap, but the platelet-rich white thrombus composition and tissue factor dependence differ fundamentally.
- [[Atherosclerosis]] — shares endothelial dysfunction as a risk substrate with DVT, and the two are associated, though the causal direction is unresolved.
- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] — a major source of arterial thromboembolism, useful as the comparative anticoagulation indication for which VKAs remain first-line.
- [[Obesity]] — a component of the metabolic risk cluster (with [[Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus]] and [[Metabolic Syndrome]]) that raises DVT risk through stasis and prothrombotic metabolic effects.
- [[Cancer]] — malignancy raises DVT risk by venous compression and by shedding tissue factor on membrane microparticles; cancer-associated VTE is a distinct management problem.
- [[Thrombocytopenia]] — a complication of anticoagulation rather than a cause of DVT, but it also complicates anticoagulant choice, since heparin may need to be swapped for fondaparinux if platelets fall below ~75,000.
