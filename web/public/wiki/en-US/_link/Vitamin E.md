# Vitamin E
**[[Vitamin E]]** is a group of eight fat-soluble compounds that include four [[Tocopherols|tocopherols]] and four [[Tocotrienols|tocotrienols]]. It acts as a potent antioxidant, preventing [[Lipid Peroxidation]] and protecting [[Cell Membranes]] and lipoproteins.

## Isoforms

Vitamin E comprises eight vitamers — four [[Tocopherols]] (α, β, γ, δ; saturated phytyl tail) and four [[Tocotrienols]] (α, β, γ, δ; unsaturated isoprenoid tail) — differing only in chromanol ring methylation and tail saturation:

| Vitamer | Ring methyls | Vitamin E activity | Where it dominates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| α-Tocopherol | 5,7,8-trimethyl | 100 (reference) | wheat germ, sunflower, olive; ~90% of plasma vitamin E |
| β-Tocopherol | 5,8-dimethyl | 25–50 | minor in most oils |
| γ-Tocopherol | 7,8-dimethyl | 10–30 | corn, soybean, canola |
| δ-Tocopherol | 8-methyl | ~1 | sesame, rice bran; also traps reactive nitrogen species |
| α-Tocotrienol | 5,7,8-trimethyl | ~30 | crude palm, rice bran |
| γ/δ-Tocotrienol | 7,8- / 8-methyl | ~30 | annatto, palm |

Only the α form is retained efficiently in human plasma and tissue, because the hepatic **α-tocopherol transfer protein (α-TTP)** selectively binds α-tocopherol and resecretes it in VLDL while other vitamers are ω-hydroxylated by CYP4F2 and excreted as CEHC metabolites. This is why plasma is α-dominated even when dietary intake is mostly γ, and why supplementation with pure α-tocopherol can displace the other isoforms from the same transport and membrane slots.

## Chain-Breaking Mechanism

Vitamin E terminates the propagation phase of [[Lipid Peroxidation]] by donating the phenolic hydrogen of its chromanol ring to a lipid peroxyl radical (LOO•):

> LOO• + TocOH → LOOH + TocO•

The rate constant is near 10⁶ M⁻¹s⁻¹ — fast enough to outcompete the LOO• + PUFA propagation step, but slow enough that tocopherol reacts selectively with peroxyl radicals rather than with the initiating radical. The resulting tocopheroxyl radical is resonance-stabilized and unreactive toward PUFA, so the chain stops.

> [!info] Source: [[_document_ - berrougui2009_resveratrol_as_atheroprotective_compound|Berrougui et al., Atherosclerosis 2009]]
> Vitamin E (α-tocopherol) was used as a positive control in DPPH free radical scavenging assays. At identical concentrations (10 and 25 µM), resveratrol exhibited equivalent DPPH scavenging activity to vitamin E. Vitamin E (10 µM) also restored HDL3-mediated cholesterol efflux in macrophages under oxidative stress.

The phytyl tail anchors the molecule in the bilayer or lipoprotein core while the ring sits near the membrane surface, positioned to intercept peroxyl radicals at the lipid–water interface — which is also where [[Vitamin C]] regenerates TocO• back to TocOH. [[Glutathione]] and [[Glutathione Reductase]] in turn regenerate ascorbate, closing the network. Vitamin E is thus not consumed 1:1 with radicals; it is a recyclable catalyst of chain termination, and its functional reserve is set by the whole network rather than by tocopherol alone.

## Why Edible Oils Are Rich in Vitamin E
Vitamin E is the only **fat-soluble chain-breaking antioxidant**, so it must sit in the same phase it protects: the phytyl tail dissolves in the oil while the chromanol ring donates a phenolic hydrogen to lipid peroxyl radicals, terminating the [[Lipid Peroxidation]] chain. Water-soluble antioxidants such as [[Vitamin C]] and glutathione cannot enter the oil phase to do this, and are instead spent on recycling the resulting α-tocopheroxyl radical.

Plants synthesize tocopherols and tocotrienols in plastids for a storage problem specific to oilseeds: seeds pack energy as [[Polyunsaturated fatty acids|PUFA]]-rich triacylglycerol and then lie dormant for months, where light, oxygen and transition metals would otherwise rancidify the reserve. Oil-bearing tissues therefore carry tocochromanols, and pressing the seed co-extracts them — you cannot get the oil without the vitamin E unless refining strips it out. Starchy endosperm (rice, white wheat) contains little. This is the same vulnerability humans face, since membranes and LDL carry the same [[Polyunsaturated fatty acids|PUFA]] load, which is why α-tocopherol transfer protein selectively retains the α isoform ([[Alpha-tocopherol]]).

Relative vitamin E content across common cooking oils (unrefined/cold-pressed, approximate mg per 100 g):

| Oil | Vitamin E | Dominant isoform |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wheat germ | 150–400 | α |
| Sunflower, safflower | 35–45 | α |
| Safflower (high-oleic), cottonseed | 18–35 | α |
| Corn, soybean, canola | 8–20 | mostly γ |
| Olive (extra virgin), avocado | 6–20 | mostly α |
| Red (crude) palm | 15–25 | tocotrienols |

> [!warning] Refining and label declaration
> Bleaching and deodorizing destroy 50–80% of tocopherols and essentially all carotenoids, so refined "palm olein" is pale and vitamin-E-poor compared with red palm oil. Because the FDA does not require declaring vitamin E (or vitamin K) unless fortified, common refined corn, canola and avocado oils appear to contain none on the label while still carrying meaningful amounts when cold-pressed. Sunflower and wheat germ oils are the most reliable α-tocopherol sources; corn and soybean supply mostly γ-tocopherol, which scores lower on conventional vitamin-E activity despite similar mass.

Only fat-soluble vitamins can occur in an oil. Beyond vitamin E, seed oils carry **[[Vitamin K|vitamin K1]]** (phylloquinone, a plastid quinone — relevant to warfarin dosing), crude red palm oil carries **provitamin A carotenoids** from the same photoprotective pigment pool, and **[[Vitamin D|vitamin D]]** is essentially absent outside fish-liver oils.

## Supplementation vs. Oil-Form Vitamin E: Mitohormesis
High-dose vitamin E supplements can suppress [[Mitohormesis]] because tocopherol removes the lipid-peroxyl radicals and their reactive breakdown products (e.g. [[4-Hydroxynonenal]]) that feed [[NRF2]] activation, and because chronically elevated antioxidant capacity flattens the redox signal upstream of AMPK–SIRT1–PGC-1α adaptation. The full argument and trial evidence are in [[Antioxidant Supplementation Paradox]] and [[Exercise Hormesis]].

Vitamin E arriving in cooking oil differs on four axes:

| Axis | Supplement (typical 400 IU capsule) | Oil form (per tablespoon) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dose | ≈180–270 mg α-tocopherol in one bolus — 12–18× the 15 mg adequate intake | ~0.5–27 mg (1–3 mg for most refined oils; wheat germ oil far higher) |
| Isoform | almost always pure α, often synthetic `dl-` racemate | mixed tocopherols/tocotrienols (α, γ, δ) plus other meal constituents |
| Pharmacokinetics | absorbed on an empty stomach, sustains supraphysiological plasma and liver α-tocopherol for days | absorbed slowly with dietary fat via chylomicrons; a modest postprandial rise that returns toward baseline |
| Redox context | a standing antioxidant load present whether or not a hormetic stimulus is occurring | transient, meal-coupled; not titrated against the exercise/fasting window |

Two mechanistic qualifiers make the difference more than just "pill vs. food":

- **Vitamin E does not scavenge the primary mitohormetic messengers.** Tocopherol reacts with lipid peroxyl radicals inside membranes; it does not react with the superoxide or matrix/cytosolic H₂O₂ (10–100 nM range) that oxidize the cysteine sensors driving [[Mitohormesis]]. Its interference with redox adaptation is therefore indirect — mostly through suppressing the lipid-peroxidation branch of redox signaling. A dietary dose is too small to move that branch at all.
- **Isoform mixture matters.** α-TTP selectively retains α and preferentially clears γ and δ, so the γ-tocopherol that dominates corn, soybean and canola oil is rapidly excreted and does not accumulate into a chronic tissue pool the way supplemental α does. γ also traps reactive nitrogen species that α-tocopherol does not, so oil-form vitamin E is not bioequivalent to a pure-α capsule even at equal mass.

> [!note] Evidence quality
> The canonical exercise-blunting result (Ristow et al., *PNAS* 2009) used **vitamin C (1000 mg) + vitamin E (400 IU)** together, so the effect cannot be assigned to vitamin E alone; isolated-vitamin-E mitohormetic data in humans are thin. Vitamin E harm is better established for chronic disease endpoints than for training adaptation: SELECT (400 IU/day) raised prostate cancer risk 17%, HOPE showed no cardiovascular benefit, while ATBC (50 mg/day in smokers) pointed the other way for prostate cancer — no clean dose-response. Conversely, observational dietary vitamin E intake is consistently neutral-to-favorable, and no trial has shown food-level tocopherols blunting exercise or fasting adaptation.

> [!tip] Practical read
> Get vitamin E from oil, nuts and seeds; skip the 400 IU capsule. If supplementing anyway, avoid dosing within a few hours of exercise, heat/cold exposure or fasting — that is when the ROS signal that vitamin E would quench is doing its work. See [[Xenohormesis]] for compounds (sulforaphane, curcumin) that do the opposite: mild electrophilic stress that *induces* [[NRF2]] rather than quenching it.

## Interaction with COMT
Research has shown that [[COMT]] variants can impact the efficacy and safety of [[Vitamin E]] ([[Alpha-tocopherol]]) supplementation. 
- **Slow [[COMT]] (rs4680 A/A):** Associated with a reduction in [[Cancer]] rates when taking Vitamin E.
- **Fast [[COMT]] (rs4680 G/G):** May experience a slight increase in cancer risk with Vitamin E supplementation.
