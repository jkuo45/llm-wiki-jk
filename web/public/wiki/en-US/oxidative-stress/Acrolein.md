# Acrolein

**Acrolein** (CH₂=CH–CHO, 2-propenal) is the simplest α,β-unsaturated aldehyde and one of the most electrophilic products of [[Lipid Peroxidation]]. Its vinyl group flanking a carbonyl makes it a potent Michael acceptor: it modifies protein cysteine, histidine, and lysine residues and forms DNA adducts, at concentrations well below those of [[Malondialdehyde]] or [[4-Hydroxynonenal]].

## Chemical Properties and Formation

Acrolein is a volatile, water-soluble liquid (boiling point 53 °C) with a characteristically acrid odor. Its biological sources are three:

- **Lipid peroxidation**: β-scission of hydroperoxides of [[Polyunsaturated fatty acids|PUFAs]] — notably ω-3 species such as [[Docosahexaenoic acid|DHA]] and eicosapentaenoic acid, whose chain-length and double-bond placement favor 3-carbon fragment release. Acrolein is therefore a marker of n-3-rich membrane oxidation, in contrast to [[4-Hydroxynonenal]] which reports n-6 oxidation.
- **Myeloperoxidase (MPO)–dependent amino acid oxidation**: [[Myeloperoxidase]]/H₂O₂/Cl⁻ systems oxidize threonine and other amino acids to acrolein, making neutrophil-rich inflammatory sites a major source.
- **Polyamine catabolism and exogenous exposure**: spermine/spermidine oxidation by amine oxidases generates acrolein; it is also the toxic metabolite of the alkylating prodrug [[Cyclophosphamide]] (via hepatic CYP2B6/3A4 activation to phosphoramide mustard + acrolein) and of the related oxazaphosphorines ifosfamide and trofosfamide. Cigarette smoke and automotive exhaust are significant exogenous sources.

Because it is both produced endogenously and delivered exogenously, acrolein adducts (3-HPMA, 3-HMPR in urine) are used as integrated exposure biomarkers.

## Reactivity and Detoxification

Acrolein's second-order rate constant with glutathione exceeds those of MDA and 4-HNE, so its clearance is largely thiol-dependent:

- **Conjugation**: [[Glutathione]] S-transferases (notably GSTP1 and GSTA4) form GSH-acrolein conjugates, exported via MRP transporters. Glutathione depletion therefore converts a clearance pathway into a liability.
- **Aldo-keto reductases (AKRs)**: reduce acrolein to allyl alcohol; AKR1A1/AKR7A2 are major hepatic routes.
- **Aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs)**: oxidize acrolein to acrylic acid, with ALDH3A1 and mitochondrial ALDH2 contributing in lung and heart.

The Michael adducts themselves are reversible at low occupancy (retro-Michael release), which is why acrolein behaves as a *transient* electrophilic signal at subtoxic doses — the basis of its Nrf2-activating hormetic effect — and as an irreversible cross-linker at high flux.

## Mechanism of Toxicity

Acrolein depletes protein thiols and inactivates enzymes with reactive cysteines, including glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, complex III subunits, and the Ca²⁺-ATPase SERCA. It forms 1,N²-propanodeoxyguanosine (Acr-dG) DNA adducts and cross-links DNA–protein complexes, and it stabilizes the p53/p21 axis at low doses. In the vasculature, acrolein-modified LDL is recognized by scavenger receptors, contributing to foam-cell formation alongside [[Oxidized LDL]].

## Pathological and Clinical Relevance

- **Cyclophosphamide urotoxicity**: acrolein accumulates in the bladder and causes hemorrhagic cystitis — the reason MESNA (sodium 2-mercaptoethane sulfonate) is co-administered as a thiol scavenger.
- **Cardiovascular disease**: elevated urinary 3-HPMA correlates with coronary risk; acrolein impairs endothelium-dependent vasodilation and promotes [[VCAM-1]] expression.
- **Neurodegeneration**: acrolein adducts are elevated in [[Alzheimer's Disease]] and [[Parkinson's Disease]] tissue; it modifies tau and α-synuclein and inhibits astrocytic glutamate uptake.
- **Respiratory**: inhaled acrolein drives COPD and asthma airway injury, depleting epithelial [[Glutathione]] and disrupting ciliary function.
- **Dietary**: acrolein forms during high-temperature lipid frying; the α,β-unsaturated aldehyde load of deep-fried oils is a plausible contributor to chronic oxidative burden.
