# Anticancer

**Anticancer** therapy is the systemic pharmacological treatment of [[Cancer]] — used for cure, adjuvant control, palliation, symptom relief, and quality-of-life improvement. The category is defined by intent rather than by chemistry: a drug is anticancer if it is deployed against malignancy, whatever its chemical class or original indication.

> [!info] A two-axis classification works better than one
> Modern agents frequently span classical categories, so the most usable scheme classifies on two independent axes: **site of action** (tumour cell nucleus, tumour cell signalling machinery, tumour vasculature, immune system, endocrine system) and **molecular mechanism**. A single taxonomy cannot hold antibody-drug conjugates, adoptive cell therapy, and hormone blockers at once.

## Cytotoxic chemotherapy

Cytotoxic agents kill proliferating cells by damaging macromolecules or blocking the cell cycle. The classical classes:

- **Alkylating agents** (cyclophosphamide, chlorambucil, platinum drugs such as [[Cisplatin]]) form covalent bonds with DNA, causing cross-links and mispairing.
- **Antimetabolites** (methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil, gemcitabine) mimic nucleotides or folate and halt DNA/RNA synthesis.
- **Anti-microtubule agents** — [[Taxanes]] and [[Vinca Alkaloids]] — stabilise or destabilise mitotic spindles, causing metaphase capture and mitotic catastrophe.
- **Topoisomerase inhibitors** ([[Topoisomerase]], camptothecins) convert enzymes that normally relieve supercoiling into DNA-cleaving toxins.

An analysis of 150 FDA-approved agents found 61 cytotoxic drugs versus 89 targeted drugs, with the balance having shifted decisively toward targeted agents since the 2000s.

## Targeted therapy

Targeted agents block a specific molecular dependency of the tumour. Four structural groups dominate:

- **Small-molecule inhibitors** of kinases and enzymes. Kinases are the single richest source of targets — EGFR, BRAF/MEK, HER2, PI3K — but because the same kinases serve normal tissues, small-molecule inhibitors carry systemic on-target toxicities (skin, gastrointestinal, fatigue) rather than clean selectivity.
- **Monoclonal antibodies** against extracellular or cell-surface targets, delivering blockade, [[Apoptosis]] induction, or antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.
- **Antibody-drug conjugates** linking a targeting antibody to a cytotoxic payload; the targeting step determines the tissue, the payload determines the mechanism.
- **Apoptosis inducers, proteasome inhibitors, and gene-expression modulators** such as BH3 mimetics and BET inhibitors.

Targeted therapy only works when a targetable driver is present. It is precision medicine in the literal sense: the drug choice is conditional on a biomarker, and the same agent is inert without it.

## Hormonal therapy

Steroid-hormone-receptor status defines treatment in breast, prostate, and gynaecologic cancers. Agents include oestrogen-receptor antagonists, aromatase inhibitors, androgen-receptor antagonists, and gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists. The receptors themselves are nuclear transcription factors ([[Estrogen Receptor]], [[Androgen Receptor]]) and are among the longest-established drug targets in oncology.

## Immunotherapy

Anticancer immunotherapy is the fastest-growing group. Checkpoint inhibitors against PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 release physiological brakes on T cells, converting tumour immunity from tolerance into attack. Alongside them sit adoptive cell therapy (CAR-T, TCR), therapeutic cancer vaccines, oncolytic viruses, and cytokine administration.

> [!warning] Class labels mislead
> "Targeted therapy" is a marketing-adjacent term: it groups drugs that share little but a rationale, and agents like sunitinib are frequently counted as targeted despite broad multi-target activity. Mechanism, not label, should drive interpretation.

## Resistance

Resistance is intrinsic or acquired and operates by several routes: target mutation or amplification, bypass signalling through parallel pathways, drug efflux and reduced uptake, sequestration of drug in acidic compartments, detoxification, and evasion of immune recognition. Because combination regimens attack the same clone through independent mechanisms, resistance to one agent frequently spares the other — which is the whole argument for combination therapy and for the growing use of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors alongside endocrine agents.

## Repurposing

A recurring theme in this vault is the repurposing of non-oncology drugs as anticancer agents — [[Ivermectin]], [[Fenbendazole]], [[Auranofin]], [[Apigenin]]. Their appeal is mechanistic novelty and established human safety; their ceiling is the pharmacokinetic problem of needing anticancer exposures that may exceed what is safely achievable. Claims of efficacy from in vitro or case-report evidence should not be read as clinical evidence.
