# Whole-Blood NAD+ as a Biomarker of Ageing — Viewpoint Differences Between the 2026 Nature Metabolism Cluster and the Vault

Date: 10_Nov_2026 02:19 PM PST

> [!warning] Access limitation
> The full text of Gardell & Baur (2026) is subscription content. Only the standfirst, the Figure 1 caption, the 15-item reference list, author affiliations and competing-interest declarations were accessible. The argument attributed to that Comment below is reconstructed from those components plus the two primary studies it cites. `src/notes/_link/_document_ - The bleeding edge of NAD in human ageing.md` is a flagged preview stub, not a full document.

## The 2026 Nature Metabolism publications

Three items published in *Nature Metabolism* in 2026 form one interpretive cluster.

**Trętowicz et al.**, "Human whole-blood NAD+ levels do not vary with age or lifestyle interventions", *Nat Metab* 8, 1282–1290 (14 May 2026), doi:10.1038/s42255-026-01537-5, PMID 42135539. A validated UHPLC–high-resolution mass spectrometry method accounting for pre-analytical and analytical variability quantified [[NAD+]] across seven independent human cohorts; cohort sample sizes are not quantified in the accessible abstract and metadata. Whole-blood NAD+ remained stable with age and across lifestyle interventions, and increased after [[Nicotinamide Riboside]] supplementation (twin-pair cohort escalated from 250 mg/day to 1 g/day over 5 months). Extended Data Figure 4 reports power calculations for detecting age-related differences at n = 20 per group; Extended Data Figure 7 relates whole-blood NAD+ to haematocrit in n = 20 (CardioHT) and n = 24 (twin-pair baseline). The authors state that the results challenge the utility of blood NAD+ as a [[Biomarker]] of ageing or lifestyle factors. An accompanying Research Briefing, "Whole-blood NAD+ levels do not reflect healthy ageing" (*Nat Metab* 8, 1260–1261, doi:10.1038/s42255-026-01540-w), states the same conclusion.

**Romenskaja-Avgul et al.**, "Whole-blood NAD levels track cardiovascular disease but not chronological age", *Nat Metab* (4 November 2026), doi:10.1038/s42255-026-01660-3, PMID 42983021, open access. Arm 1 was a cross-sectional study of 1,068 adults (636 females, 432 males; 1,340 measurements) sampled in Lithuania 2023–2025 under standardised morning fasting conditions, using dried blood spots (DBS) and an enzymatic cycling assay measuring total whole-blood NAD (NAD+ plus NADH). Arm 2 comprised three exploratory longitudinal protocols (n = 2 over 1 year; n = 9 sampled three times daily for 7 days; n = 2 over 19 days of self-administered [[NMN]] at 1,000 mg/day). Results: males had higher NAD than females (P = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³); healthy males showed no association with age; healthy females showed a positive slope of 0.07 µM year⁻¹ (sensitivity range 0.043–0.09 µM year⁻¹). In the pooled chronic-disease group (n = 243), NAD declined with age in males and showed no age-related change in females; stratification identified reduced NAD in [[Cardiovascular Disease]] and [[Hypertension]] in both sexes. Supplement users (10 males, 12 females) reached 38–58 µM, approximately twofold to threefold the unsupplemented range; 6 of 22 users (27%) reached that range, whereas no unsupplemented participant did, with one exception among more than 1,000. NMN raised NAD from approximately 19–20 µM to 51–56 µM with first-order kinetics (half-time 3.2–4.3 days). Within-person fluctuation over 7 days was approximately 2–3 µM. The authors state that their data "do not provide evidence for a universal whole-blood NAD deficit in healthy adults and therefore provide no broad rationale for NAD-boosting interventions", and that their findings do not contradict tissue-specific NAD decline reported in skeletal muscle, brain and skin.

**Gardell & Baur**, "The bleeding edge of NAD in human ageing", *Nat Metab* (4 November 2026), doi:10.1038/s42255-026-01663-0, PMID 42983020, Comment. The standfirst states that whole-blood NAD levels do not consistently decline with age, "challenging the notion that lower systemic NAD levels cause age-related diseases", but that blood NAD provides an incomplete view of systemic NAD metabolism and fails to capture heterogeneity across blood and solid tissues. Figure 1 states that red blood cells preferentially use [[Nicotinic acid]] for NAD synthesis via the [[Preiss-Handler Pathway]], whereas solid-tissue cells preferentially use [[Nicotinamide]]; solid-tissue cells, but not red blood cells, possess [[CD38]], PARPs, [[Sirtuins]] and other enzymes that degrade NAD to nicotinamide. The caption concludes that these differences undermine the assumption that whole-blood NAD reliably reflects NAD homeostasis in other tissues. Both authors declare patents and consulting or funding relationships with NAD-precursor developers (Eli Lilly, Pfizer, Elysium Health, Metro International Biotech, Cytokinetics, Sironax); the Comment's framing retains a rationale for tissue-directed NAD augmentation.

## Viewpoint differences

### Scope of the age-related decline claim

The vault states the decline as an unqualified organism-level fact. `src/notes/_link/NAD+.md` line 37 reads "NAD+ levels decline with age, linked to" mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced sirtuin activity and accumulated DNA damage. `src/notes/adrenochrome/_document_ - NAD+ Precursors Nicotinamide Mononucleotide (NMN) and Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) Potential Dietary Contribution to Health.md` line 41 calls low NAD+ "one of the hallmarks of physiological decline" and line 139 states "It is recognized the decrease of NAD+ levels in various tissues during aging". `src/notes/adrenochrome/_document_ - Nicotinamide Riboside—The Current State of Research and Therapeutic Uses.md` line 27 refers to "the systemic NAD+ decrease". `src/notes/sirtuins/Pan-sirtuin Activator.md`, `NMNH.md` and `NRH.md` describe precursor compounds as counteracting "the age-related NAD+ decline".

The 2026 cluster separates the compartment from the organism. Romenskaja-Avgul et al. explicitly retain tissue-specific decline in skeletal muscle (Janssens et al., *Nat Aging* 2, 254–263, 2022), brain (Guo et al., *Front Neurosci* 18, 1389111, 2024) and skin (Massudi et al., *PLoS ONE* 7, e42357, 2012) while stating that whole-blood NAD dynamics do not necessarily mirror those in tissues. Gardell & Baur supply a mechanistic basis for the divergence: erythrocytes and solid tissues differ in precursor route and in expression of NAD-consuming enzymes. The disagreement therefore concerns the inference from blood to tissue, not the existence of tissue-level decline. The vault's `src/notes/_link/SLC25A51.md` line 71 already states that mitochondrial NAD+ is maintained separately from the nuclear/cytosolic pool, and `src/notes/_link/NAD+.md` line 107 lists compartment as a determinant of parthanatos outcomes; the compartmentalisation principle exists in the vault at subcellular scale but is not applied to whole blood.

The 2025 review by Vinten et al., "NAD+ precursor supplementation in human ageing: clinical evidence and challenges" (*Nat Metab* 7, 1974–1990, doi:10.1038/s42255-025-01387-7), takes an intermediate position: "evidence for an age-related decline in NAD+ levels in humans has been consistently observed only in a limited number of studies", and human trials show heterogeneous outcomes. A 2026 open review, "Targeting NAD Homeostasis: Compartmentalization, Quantification, and Modulation" (PMC13208215, 18 May 2026), reports that circulating NAD+ is lower than in solid tissues, that NAD turnover rates differ by tissue, and that cross-study comparison is limited by methodological heterogeneity.

### Whole-blood NAD as a readout of status versus exposure

The vault treats circulating NAD-related metabolites as measures of NAD status and intervention efficacy. `src/notes/sirtuins/NAAD.md` line 32 states that blood [[NAAD]] is used as a pharmacodynamic biomarker in human trials of [[NMN]] and NR, "supporting dosing and efficacy assessment in interventions targeting NAD+ decline with [[Aging]]". The NR review at line 99 states that bioavailability "can be tested by measuring NAD+ levels or other relevant biomarkers such as NAAD in the cells of the target tissue or in the blood", and reports that 1,000 mg NR twice daily raised steady-state whole-blood NAD+ up to 2.7-fold after one dose.

The 2026 studies partition that claim. Trętowicz et al. confirm that whole-blood NAD+ responds to NR, so it remains a target-engagement marker, but reject it as a marker of ageing or lifestyle. Romenskaja-Avgul et al. report that whole-blood NAD tracks cardiovascular disease and hypertension rather than chronological age, and that supplement users reach concentrations not observed in unsupplemented individuals. Gardell & Baur reject blood NAD as a surrogate for tissue NAD homeostasis. The resulting position is that whole-blood NAD measures precursor exposure and cardiovascular disease burden, not tissue NAD content or biological age.

The phase 3 NOPARK randomised trial supplies a human Tier 1 test of the exposure-to-outcome link: 410 participants with early Parkinson disease at 11 Norwegian centres received oral NR 500 mg twice daily or placebo for 52 weeks; the adjusted between-group difference on the MDS-UPDRS was 2.72 points favouring placebo, and the trial reported no clinical benefit (*JAMA* 2026, doi:10.1001/jama.2026.18828; summarised in *Nat Aging*, 6 November 2026, doi:10.1038/s43587-026-01277-x). The senior author is quoted as stating that NR increased NAD metabolism but that this did not translate into clinical improvement. A PRISMA-guided systematic review (Gallagher & Emmanuel, *Ageing Res Rev* 116, 103057, 2026, PMID 41655607, doi:10.1016/j.arr.2026.103057) identified 113 eligible studies (33 human intervention studies, 28 randomised; 80 rodent studies); oral NR and [[NMN]] consistently produced biochemical target engagement in humans, while functional outcomes were heterogeneous and often null, and clinical effectiveness for anti-ageing or wellness outcomes remains inconclusive. Target engagement and clinical efficacy are therefore separate endpoints in the 2026 literature; the vault's "efficacy assessment" phrasing conflates them.

### Sex-stratified age trajectories

`src/notes/_link/NAD+.md` lines 59–92 build a sex-difference framework on a whole-blood trajectory: men 34.5 versus women 31.3 µmol/L, men showing progressive decline from age 60 (β = −2.16 for men ≥60) and women fluctuating without monotonic decline, cited to Yang et al., *Front Endocrinol* 13, 829658 (2022). That trajectory anchors the [[PARP1]]-driven male NAD consumption engine, oestrogen-associated PARP-1 restraint (labelled "hypothesis, not direct proof" in the same note), sex-specific NMN dosing recommendations, and the statement that post-menopausal women face a "double decline".

Romenskaja-Avgul et al. cite Yang et al. 2022 among the studies reporting decline restricted to males and report the opposite age pattern in healthy adults: flat in males, rising in females. The male > female baseline difference is retained (P = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³), but absolute concentrations are not directly comparable across the DBS enzymatic assay and the earlier methods. The authors offer a confounding explanation: hypertension prevalence rises with age, so cohorts not stratified for cardiovascular disease accumulate hypertensive participants, which may account for previously reported age-related NAD trajectories. In their chronic-disease group, males did show an age-related decline and females lost the healthy-ageing increase, consistent with that explanation. The vault's tissue-level sex evidence — PARP activity in male skin (Massudi et al. 2012), ovarian [[CD38]] in mice (Wang et al., *Nat Aging* 2023) — is not adjudicated by these blood measurements.

### Attribution of discrepant findings to measurement and confounding

The NAD+ notes in the vault contain no pre-analytical or assay-validity caveat for NAD quantification. Comparable caveats exist elsewhere in the vault for other biomarkers: `src/notes/oxidative-stress/Malondialdehyde.md` line 29 requires standardised sample handling and a more specific companion marker, `src/notes/oxidative-stress/F2-Isoprostanes.md` line 27 identifies ex vivo autoxidation as a source of artefactual elevation in older studies, and `src/notes/epigenetics/H4K16ac.md` line 43 flags antibody cross-reactivity.

The 2026 studies relocate part of the uncertainty from mechanism to measurement. Trętowicz et al. validated UHPLC–HRMS against real-world analytical variability, characterised pre-analytical handling (storage condition, methanol thaw, freeze–thaw), and quantified the sample size and power needed to detect age-related differences. Romenskaja-Avgul et al. report that the NAD+/NADH ratio collapses to equilibrium within the first hour of DBS drying while the total pool is conserved, so assays measuring only NAD+ from dried samples probably underestimate the liquid-blood NAD+ concentration; they interpret this as an explanation for apparent NAD+ loss in earlier DBS work. They report paired liquid-versus-DBS correlation r = 0.93 (n = 31) and three-site cross-method correlations r = 0.87–0.91. They also state that within-person variability of 2–3 µM can dominate a single measurement in small studies, and that whole-blood NAD derives almost entirely from red blood cells, so haematocrit differences could in principle contribute to group differences; for hypertension they argue the bias runs in the opposite direction, since hypertension is associated with higher haematocrit.

### Rationale for NAD precursor supplementation

The vault frames repletion as a general restorative strategy. `src/notes/sirtuins/_document_ - Sirtuins Guardians of Mammalian Healthspan.md` line 264 states that maintaining NAD+ at youthful concentrations may maintain sirtuin function and promote health during ageing; line 258 proposes that sirtuin overexpression compensates for declining NAD+ by mass action. `src/notes/sirtuins/SIRT1.md` line 296 hedges the corresponding claim as "hypothesized to be a key driver" of mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammaging. The vault's disease-model documents — `src/notes/senescence/NAD+ Metabolic Reprogramming.md`, `src/notes/senescence/FK866.md`, `src/notes/sirtuins/SRT1720.md`, and `raw/_document_ - NAD+ depletion drives age-related monocyte hyperinflammation after stroke and is reversed by nicotinamide riboside - Journal of Neuroinflammation.md` — establish intracellular NAD+ depletion as sufficient to drive CD8+ T-cell senescence in mice and age-related monocyte hyperinflammation after stroke in aged mice, with [[NMN]] or NR reversing the phenotype.

Romenskaja-Avgul et al. state that their data provide no broad rationale for NAD-boosting interventions in healthy adults, while retaining that condition-specific use in defined patient groups may retain a rationale, citing early trials of NR on systolic blood pressure and arterial stiffness in adults with hypertension. They state that the long-term consequences of sustained high NAD concentrations in humans are unknown, that most trials last weeks to months, and that [[NAMPT]] is an established oncogenic driver, [[CD38]] is upregulated in inflammatory disease, and NAD metabolism can drive pro-inflammatory reprogramming in neuroinflammation; they explicitly state that these findings do not show that high NAD is harmful but raise safety questions current research cannot answer. Gardell & Baur's mechanistic framing preserves a tissue-level rationale that blood measurements cannot test. The vault states supplementation benefits in humans with fewer qualifiers and does not carry a sustained-high-NAD safety question in its NAD+ notes.

## Vault claims not adjudicated by the 2026 studies

The 2026 whole-blood studies do not test intracellular or tissue NAD+ depletion in rodents, in vitro, or in human tissue biopsies. `src/notes/cancer/_document_ - Chemotherapy NAD+ Loss Pöllänen 2026.md` reports skeletal muscle NAD+ reductions of 14% after cisplatin and 18% after prolonged FOLFIRI in humans, independent of tumour burden; `src/notes/_link/NAD+.md` line 46 records these. These are tissue measurements, not whole-blood measurements. Romenskaja-Avgul et al. cite Janssens et al. 2022 (muscle) and Massudi et al. 2012 (skin), both already present in the vault's reference lists, as evidence of tissue decline they do not dispute. `src/notes/_link/CD38.md` line 22 describes elevated CD38 activity as a leading driver of age-related NAD+ decline, and `src/notes/_link/SARM1.md` describes SARM1-dependent axonal NAD+ depletion; these are consumer-enzyme mechanisms at tissue and cellular scale. The 2026 blood studies are silent on all of them.

## Notes requiring qualification

The following statements are the points at which the 2026 literature and the vault currently diverge; each is a candidate for a compartment qualifier or a citation update rather than a deletion.

- `src/notes/_link/NAD+.md` line 37: add tissue or intracellular compartment to the age-related decline statement.
- `src/notes/_link/NAD+.md` lines 62–92: add the 2026 replication of the male > female baseline and the opposite healthy-ageing trajectories; separate the whole-blood trajectory from the tissue-level PARP-1 and ovarian CD38 evidence.
- `src/notes/sirtuins/NAAD.md` line 32: restrict "efficacy assessment" to pharmacodynamic target engagement; whole-blood NAD+ and NAAD do not index tissue NAD status.
- `src/notes/adrenochrome/_document_ - NAD+ Precursors Nicotinamide Mononucleotide (NMN) and Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) Potential Dietary Contribution to Health.md` lines 41 and 139, and `src/notes/adrenochrome/_document_ - Nicotinamide Riboside—The Current State of Research and Therapeutic Uses.md` line 27: qualify "systemic NAD+ decrease" and "hallmarks of physiological decline" to specified tissues and models.
- `src/notes/sirtuins/Pan-sirtuin Activator.md`, `NMNH.md`, `NRH.md`: qualify "the age-related NAD+ decline" to tissue or intracellular contexts.
- `src/tasks/task_output_nad_metabolism_cd38_sarm1_20_Oct_2026.md` line 331: annotate the Yang et al. 2022 whole-blood figures as one of the contested trajectories.

## Unresolved items

- The full text of Gardell & Baur (2026) was not accessible. The Comment's treatment of the 15 cited references, including Pirinen et al. (*Cell Metab* 31, 1078–1090.e5, 2020), Janssens et al. (2022), Camacho-Pereira et al. (*Cell Metab* 23, 1127–1139, 2016) and McReynolds et al. (*Cell Syst* 12, 1160–1172.e4, 2021), is not reconstructed here.
- The Figure 1 caption states that red blood cells lack CD38, PARPs and sirtuins. A 2020 peer-review report on a separate NAD review (*Pharmaceuticals* 13, 247) cites CD38 expression on erythrocytes (PMID 8250903). The accessible material does not reconcile these statements.
- Romenskaja-Avgul et al. list limitations that remain open: DBS does not preserve the liquid-blood NAD+/NADH ratio, DBS does not permit direct haematocrit measurement, chronic-disease participants were on medication, disease-free adults over 70 were rare, Arm 2 was exploratory, and the programme was not designed to establish causality.
- Christen et al., "The differential impact of three different NAD+ boosters on circulatory NAD and microbial metabolism in humans" (*Nat Metab* 8, 62–73, 2026), reports precursor-specific circulatory NAD metabolomes. Whether the blood-versus-tissue divergence described by Gardell & Baur differs by precursor class remains untested in the accessible sources.
