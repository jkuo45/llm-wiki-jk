The Ripoptosome and c-FLIP

A cytosolic, death-receptor-independent signaling platform of RIPK1, FADD, caspase-8 and c-FLIP assembles whenever inhibitor-of-apoptosis proteins (IAPs) are lost — and a catalytically inactive caspase-8 paralog, c-FLIP (CFLAR), sets the rheostat that determines whether that platform drives apoptosis, is dismantled without cell death, or proceeds to necroptosis. This page assembles the mechanism from the two 2011 discovery papers, the structural biology of the caspase-8/c-FLIP heterodimer, and the knockout genetics that established the complex as a requirement for tissue homeostasis.

Framing

One platform, three settings: DED-chain dimer composition determines the outcome

In every cell that expresses TNF-family receptors, the same receptors that transduce survival and inflammatory gene transcription can, within minutes, generate apoptosis-executing complexes. In 2011 two groups showed that this decision does not even require a receptor. When the inhibitor-of-apoptosis proteins cIAP1, cIAP2 and XIAP are depleted — by genotoxic stress, by Smac-mimetic drugs, or by TLR3/dsRNA signaling — a large cytosolic complex of RIPK1, FADD, caspase-8, caspase-10 and c-FLIP assembles spontaneously. Tenev et al. named it the ripoptosome (also spelled riptosome in later literature); Feoktistova et al. measured it at ~2 MDa by gel filtration (Tenev et al. 2011; Feoktistova et al. 2011).

The component that sets the platform's homeostatic function is c-FLIP (FLICE-like inhibitory protein, gene CFLAR), a gene-duplicated, catalytically inactive paralog of caspase-8. Its three protein-expressed isoforms heterodimerize with caspase-8 inside the platform and set what Feoktistova et al. called a rheostat: caspase-8 homodimers output apoptosis; caspase-8/c-FLIPL heterodimers output survival with necroptosis actively suppressed; c-FLIPS/c-FLIPR heterodimers shut caspase-8 down and let the platform default to necroptosis. Knockout genetics established the physiological requirement: animals lacking caspase-8, FADD or c-FLIP all die mid-gestation, and the lethal defect is not failed apoptosis but unrestrained necroptosis or apoptosis that the caspase-8/FLIPL heterodimer normally holds in check.

Discovery

Two papers, one complex — August 2011

The ripoptosome was reported back-to-back in the same issue of Molecular Cell. Studying how the topoisomerase-II poison etoposide kills tumor cells, Tenev et al. found a ~2 MDa RIPK1–FADD–caspase-8 complex that formed on etoposide- or doxorubicin-induced IAP depletion, without TNF, FasL, TRAIL, death receptors or mitochondrial involvement. Feoktistova et al., studying TLR3 stimulation in keratinocyte models, independently identified the same cIAP-gated complex, added caspase-10 and the c-FLIP isoforms to its composition, and named caspase activity as the c-FLIP-controlled rheostat.

The disagreement over RIPK1's kinase requirement — one group finding necrostatin-1 blocks assembly, the other not — remains a genuine open point (see Open questions) and is consistent with assembly being cell-type- and stimulus-dependent. Both groups agreed on the core logic: IAPs constitutively suppress assembly, RIPK1 is the scaffold, and c-FLIP determines the quality of the outcome.

Architecture

Assembly: IAP loss releases RIPK1 to nucleate the platform

At steady state, cIAP1/cIAP2 (with TRAF2) constitutively ubiquitylate a compartmentalized pool of RIPK1, keeping the amount of "assembly-competent" RIPK1 low and targeting complex components for proteasomal degradation — proteasome inhibition with lactacystin or MG-132 alone is sufficient to trigger spontaneous ripoptosome formation (Tenev et al. 2011). When IAPs drop, deubiquitylated RIPK1 exposes its death domain (DD), recruits FADD by DD–DD interaction, and FADD's death effector domains (DEDs) nucleate a DED chain of procaspase-8, caspase-10 and c-FLIP — the same DED-chain logic used at the death-receptor DISC, but executed in the cytosol, without a receptor.

Documented assembly triggers

Genotoxic stress (etoposide, doxorubicin, teniposide) · Smac mimetics / IAP antagonists · TLR3–TRIF signaling (dsRNA, poly(I:C)) · proteasome inhibitors (lactacystin, MG-132) · siRNA knockdown of cIAP1 alone in cells lacking cIAP2. Spontaneous assembly in the absence of stress is detected in transformed cells but not in primary human keratinocytes, which carry high endogenous FLIPL (Feoktistova et al. 2011).

The pseudo-caspase

c-FLIP: a gene-duplicated, catalytically inactive caspase-8 paralog

CFLAR sits on chromosome 2q33.1 immediately beside its paralogs CASP8 and CASP10 — a local duplication that produced the only true pseudo-caspase in the human proteome (Smyth et al. 2020). The long isoform, c-FLIPL (55 kDa, 480 amino acids), mirrors procaspase-8 exactly: tandem N-terminal DEDs for chain recruitment, then large and small caspase-like domains. The active-site substitutions account for the loss of catalytic activity — the catalytic dyad of caspase-8 (His/Cys) is replaced in c-FLIPL by Arg315 and Tyr360 (human numbering), substitutions that are conserved across vertebrates and therefore functional, not decay. A cleavage site at Asp376 lets caspase-8 trim FLIPL to the DISC-retained p43-FLIP fragment.

The short isoforms — c-FLIPS (26 kDa) and c-FLIPR (24–25 kDa, expressed mainly in T and B cells, e.g. Raji cells and primary T cells) — are just the two DEDs plus a short C-terminal tail that drives their rapid ubiquitination and proteasomal turnover. Which of the two a human cell makes is decided by a single-nucleotide polymorphism in a 3′ splice site of CFLAR. Notably, mice do not express c-FLIPS at all — the murine short isoform is c-FLIPR.

Levels of every isoform are set on two timescales. Transcriptionally, CFLAR is an NF-κB target — so TNFR1 complex-I signaling pre-loads the cell with FLIPL before any death complex can form, a feed-forward survival loop. Post-translationally, all isoforms are short-lived proteasome substrates of E3 ligases (Itch, Cbl/Cbl-b, Mind bomb 1, TRAF7, CHIP, TRIM21), with half-life further tuned by PKC phosphorylation (Ser193), ROS-dependent phosphorylation (Thr166) and S-nitrosylation (Cys254/259) — which is why the FLIPL:FLIPS ratio, not total c-FLIP, is the predictive parameter, and why the isoform ratio can change within minutes (Tsuchiya et al. 2015; Smyth et al. 2020).

Decision logic

The rheostat: three settings of one caspase-8 switch

The fate decision is made by which dimers form inside the DED chain. Crystal structures of the caspase-8 catalytic domain in complex with the FLIPL pseudo-catalytic domain (PDB 3H11 / 3H13) explain the first asymmetry: the heterodimer forms with lower activation energy than the caspase-8 homodimer, so at low receptor occupancy the platform is heterodimer-dominated. The heterodimer retains a single caspase-8 active site whose substrate pocket is narrowed — it performs the interdimer cleavage of neighboring dimers but cannot complete its own intradimer processing, so it stays platform-bound and never releases the p18/p10 heterotetramer that would start apoptosis (Micheau et al. 2002; Pop et al. 2011).

"When cIAPs are absent, caspase activity is the rheostat that is controlled by cFLIP isoforms in the Ripoptosome and decides if cell death occurs by RIP3-dependent necroptosis or caspase-dependent apoptosis."
— Feoktistova et al., Molecular Cell 43:449–463 (2011)

A 2024 knockin study pinned the mechanism to a single side chain: replacing mouse FLIPL's Tyr362 (Tyr360 in humans) with the cysteine found in caspase-8 does not restore FLIP protease activity — instead it abolishes the heterodimer's ability to cleave RIPK1, releasing the necroptosis blockade; FLIPL-Y362C embryos die of unconstrained necroptosis around E10.5–E11.5 (Cell Rep. 2024). The dead active site is not dead weight: its aromatic ring shapes the heterodimer's restricted catalytic cleft. Conversely, the heterodimer needs no interdomain proteolysis at all — non-cleavable caspase-8 (D387A) cannot drive apoptosis but still blocks necroptosis when partnered with FLIPL (Oberst et al. 2011; Pop et al. 2011).

Physiology

Knockout genetics: the platform is required for development and adult tissue maintenance

Evidence that the ripoptosome functions between insults, not only during them, is genetic. Caspase-8-, FADD- and c-FLIP-null mice all die around embryonic day 10.5 with defects in yolk-sac and vascular development — and the rescue crosses dissect why. Deleting RIPK3 (or MLKL) makes caspase-8- and FADD-deficient embryos viable: their lethal defect was never "failure to apoptose" but unrestrained necroptosis from a ripoptosome-like platform with no caspase-8/FLIPL heterodimer to cleave RIPK1/RIPK3 (Kaiser et al. 2011; Oberst et al. 2011). c-FLIP deletion, by contrast, is not rescued by RIPK3 loss alone — without FLIP, the FADD–caspase-8 axis runs apoptosis unchecked; only removing FADD as well (triple knockout) restores normal development (Dillon et al. 2012).

Barrier tissues: skin, gut, liver, endothelium

Tissue-specific deletions map the same logic onto adult organs. Acute caspase-8 deletion in the gut causes enterocyte death, barrier breakdown, inflammation and sepsis-like mortality — rescued by RIPK3 loss; acute deletion in skin does the same locally (Weinlich et al. 2013). Acute c-FLIP deletion in adult epidermis instead produces TNF-dependent caspase-3-positive apoptosis, full-thickness epidermal loss and severe dermatitis that RIPK3 loss does not rescue — mirroring the developmental genetics exactly (Weinlich et al. 2013; Panayotova-Dimitrova et al. 2013). In that skin model, one wild-type Cflar allele is sufficient to protect the epidermis, and epidermal c-FLIP protein is lost in patients with toxic epidermal necrolysis / Stevens–Johnson syndrome, the human diseases of massive keratinocyte apoptosis (Panayotova-Dimitrova et al. 2013). Endothelial caspase-8 deletion phenocopies the germline knockout, tying the E10.5 checkpoint to vascular development.

The immune system: heterodimer-dependent T-cell activation and proliferation

In T lymphocytes the heterodimer functions as a proliferation signal. TCR stimulation drives caspase-8 and FLIPL into lipid rafts, where limited cleavage generates p43-FLIP; p43-FLIP recruits RIPK1, TRAF2 and TRAF6 to launch NF-κB and ERK signaling and support clonal proliferation. c-FLIP-deficient T cells proliferate poorly, produce less IL-2, and fail the double-positive → single-positive thymic transition; FLIPL overexpression enhances proliferation and CD25 induction after weak stimulation (Zhang & He 2005; Kavuri et al. 2011; Cell Death Dis. 2026 review). The isoform ratio is part of immune equilibrium itself: in mice, c-FLIPR overexpression yields Fas-resistant T cells and lupus-like autoimmunity, while in casp8−/−ripk3−/− survivors, age-dependent accumulation of abnormal B220+CD3+ T cells reproduces a Fas-deficiency phenotype (Kaiser et al. 2011).

The homeostatic reading

When IAPs are lost, RIPK1 is free to nucleate the platform. The caspase-8/FLIPL heterodimer restrains it: an enzyme with sufficient activity to cleave RIPK1 and RIPK3 and disassemble the platform, and insufficient activity to process the executioner caspases. Every tissue that has been tested — yolk-sac endothelium, gut, epidermis, hepatocytes, lymphocytes — requires this restraint continuously. Reduced c-FLIP causes inflammatory tissue destruction and developmental failure. Increased c-FLIP (the cancer setting) blocks death-ligand-induced apoptosis in tumors. Tissue homeostasis requires c-FLIP levels between the two extremes.

Pathology

Disease relevance and therapeutic modulation

Cancer. c-FLIP is overexpressed across many tumor types and confers resistance to FasL/TRAIL and to DNA-damaging chemotherapy; lowering c-FLIP protein — via proteasome-sensitive turnover, HDAC inhibitors, or the cisplatin–Itch–p53 axis — re-sensitizes tumor cells to death ligands (Safa & Kanthasamy 2012; Tsuchiya et al. 2015). Smac mimetics deliberately induce ripoptosome formation; whether the tumor then dies by apoptosis or by inflammatory necroptosis depends on its FLIPL:FLIPS ratio and RIPK3 expression — a proposed stratification biomarker. Kinase-dead RIPK3 (D161N) and RIPK3 inhibitors can redirect the platform toward apoptosis in a FLIPL-enhanced manner (the RIPK3-inhibitor paradox). Small molecules termed FLIPins target the caspase-8/FLIPL heterodimer allosterically to re-tune DISC signaling (Cell Death Differ. 2020).

Inflammatory disease. The keratinocyte data connect c-FLIP loss directly to human toxic epidermal necrolysis / Stevens–Johnson syndrome, and the TNF-dependence of epidermal c-FLIP loss (Geserick et al. 2020) places the platform upstream of cytokine-driven skin inflammation. Necroptotic output of the platform, when FLIPS dominates, releases DAMPs and drives sterile inflammation — the mechanism behind the "differential quality of cell death" that Feoktistova et al. flagged as its pathophysiological consequence.

Genetic proof of principle. The FLIPL-Y362C knockin (Cell Rep. 2024) is a single-residue test: the Tyr362 side chain maintains the heterodimer's restricted catalytic cleft for RIPK1 cleavage; its loss produces unconstrained necroptosis and embryonic lethality (E10.5–E11.5).

Open questions

What remains unsettled

  • Is RIPK1 kinase activity required for assembly? The two discovery papers disagree (Nec-1 sensitivity differs by system); assembly may be kinase-dependent in some transformed lines and kinase-independent in others.
  • Nomenclature. "Ripoptosome" vs "riptosome" vs TNFR1 "complex IIb" — related but distinguishable platforms; the field has not converged.
  • Physiological assembly. Spontaneous formation is documented mainly in transformed cells; when and where primary tissues assemble the platform in vivo — outside pharmacological IAP inhibition — is not fully mapped.
  • In-vivo stoichiometry. The FLIPL:FLIPS:procaspase-8 ratios inside a native platform have not been measured; the rheostat's quantitative midpoint is still inferred from cell lines and mathematical models.
  • Caspase-10's role in the human complex (it accumulates when c-FLIP is knocked down) remains comparatively uncharacterized.
