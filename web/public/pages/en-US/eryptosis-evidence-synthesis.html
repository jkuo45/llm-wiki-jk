Eryptosis — excess death signalling, under-clearance, and the adrenochrome crossover

Mature erythrocytes are anucleate and organelle-free and cannot execute mitochondrial apoptosis. Eryptosis is their regulated death: Ca²⁺ entry through cation channels activates the Gardos channel (KCNN4), scramblase, and calpain; phosphatidylserine (PS) is externalized; and the PS-positive cell is removed by efferocytosis within minutes, before it lyses. This page covers the signalling core, the quantified and unquantified shares of erythrocyte turnover, and the two failure modes of the system — excess eryptosis (anemia with erythropoiesis-stimulating-agent hypo-responsiveness, PS-driven thrombin generation, endothelial adhesion with ferroptotic endothelial uptake, microcirculatory impairment) and insufficient eryptosis / under-clearance (the hemolysis cascade, persistent procoagulant PS-positive cells, lost Plasmodium clearance). It closes with the adrenochrome crossover — erythrocytes convert adrenaline to aminochromes and are simultaneously a reducing sink and an oxidative target of the same chemistry — and the resulting position for longevity research. Evidence tiers: Tier 2 human cohort · Tier 3 rodent/in-vivo · Tier 4 in-vitro/ex-vivo human cells · Tier 5 hypothesis. No Tier 1 evidence exists for eryptosis modulation as an intervention.

Mechanistic core — one ion, three execution arms

Eryptosis is defined by Ca²⁺-dependent execution in a cell that has no ER and no mitochondrial store, so extracellular Ca²⁺ entry is the initiating signal rather than a MOMP amplifier. Oxidative stress, osmotic shock, Cl⁻ depletion, energy depletion, ceramide, and uremic toxins open non-selective cation channels (TRPC6 in human erythrocytes; TRPC4/5 in mouse; also PIEZO1, NMDA- and AMPA-type glutamate receptors, Cav2.1). Cytosolic Ca²⁺ then runs three parallel arms: activation of the Gardos channel (KCNN4) with K⁺/Cl⁻/water efflux and cell shrinkage; inhibition of flippase with activation of scramblase and phosphatidylserine externalization; and calpain-mediated cytoskeletal proteolysis with blebbing and microvesicle shedding. Regulation is exclusively post-translational — no transcriptional layer exists. Kinase polarity is cell-type-set: PKC, CK1α, JAK3, p38 MAPK and CDK4 promote eryptosis here although several are canonically anti-apoptotic, while AMPK, cGKI, PAK2, MSK1/2 and PDK1 restrain it. The principal brakes are the NO → soluble guanylate cyclase → cGMP → cGKI axis, AMPK, and N-acetylcysteine; erythropoietin is bidirectional — acutely inhibitory, but sustained high EPO increases the eryptosis-prone fraction (Tier 2 cohort / Tier 4).

The physiological rationale

Eryptosis is a quality-control clearance arm: it removes compromised erythrocytes — including Plasmodium falciparum-infected cells — before they lyse. PS externalization and macrophage uptake are anti-inflammatory (HO-1 up-regulation, cytokine suppression); hemolysis is the pro-inflammatory accident whose prevention by prior eryptotic clearance is the principal physiological justification for the program. Host defence bounds how far suppression can go: G6PD deficiency, sickle trait and thalassemia trait confer partial malarial protection precisely by lowering the eryptotic threshold (Tier 2).

What fraction of erythrocytes die by this route — not quantified

A normal-population eryptotic fraction is not quantified in the literature and should not be cited as one. What is established: erythrocyte lifespan is ≈ 120 days, so turnover is ≈ 1% of red-cell mass per day (commonly reported as ~2–3 × 10⁶ cells · s⁻¹); eryptotic removal is the stressed-cell subset of this flux, and the split between eryptotic clearance and senescent-antigen/IgG clearance at steady state is unpublished. Baseline PS exposure in reference cohorts is ≈ 0.5–0.6% of erythrocytes — and this is not an eryptosis-specific assay, since annexin V binding also marks other membrane states. Aged cells are more eryptosis-prone, and PS-positive senescent cells are by convention scored as eryptotic (Tkachenko 2025).

What disease does to the measured fraction is quantified in several contexts, with an important caveat: the numbers below come from different markers (annexin V, lactadherin), antibodies and flow-cytometry protocols, so between-study comparison is assay-dependent. Within studies — same marker, same protocol — the contrasts are informative.

Quantification limits

Three limits bound every downstream claim. First, no eryptosis-specific marker exists — assay standardization is an active recommendation of Tkachenko 2025. Second, PS-positive cells are cleared within minutes, so steady-state blood sampling undercounts the eryptotic flux; the measured fraction is a lower bound on activity. Third, eryptosis correlates with MPO (ρ = 0.77, p = 0.01), IL-6 (ρ = 0.52, p = 0.05) and eGFR stratum (p < 0.001) in CKD cohorts (Clementi 2022) — these are associations, with confounding by uremia, inflammation and ESA dose rarely fully adjusted (Tier 2).

Failure mode 1 — excess eryptosis converts protection into pathology

When oxidative, uremic or inflammatory load drives eryptosis faster than compensatory erythropoiesis and clearance can absorb, the quality-control program becomes a disease mechanism. Four consequence classes are documented.

Anemia and ESA hypo-responsiveness

In CKD/ESRD, erythrocyte lifespan shortens from ≈ 120 days to 60–90 days (Deng 2025); anemia prevalence rises with CKD stage and exceeds 90% of dialysis patients, and ESA/iron correction is suboptimal in roughly half of treated patients — eryptosis is one component of this hypo-responsiveness (Lang 2017). Anemia sequelae reported in association cohorts include impaired quality of life, cardiovascular complications, disease progression, cognitive impairment and higher mortality (Bissinger 2019) — associational, not causal (Tier 2). Eryptosis contributes to the anemia of chronic disease across cancer (cisplatin, topotecan, tamoxifen, sorafenib, sunitinib are pro-eryptotic), heart failure, hepatic failure, sepsis and malaria.

PS-mediated procoagulant activity

Externalized PS is the catalytic surface for prothrombinase assembly; in some pathological conditions erythrocytes contribute up to ~40% of thrombin generation (Tkachenko 2025; report). RBC-derived microvesicles trigger thrombin generation via factor XII — the contact pathway, tissue-factor-independent (van der Meijden 2012). PS-exposing cells bind platelets (CD36) and endothelium via CXCL16/SR-PSOX and thrombospondin-1, with adhesion demonstrated ex vivo under arterial shear and attenuation by annexin V or CXCL16 blockade (Borst 2012). In cirrhosis, PS on blood and endothelial cells shortens coagulation time (Wu 2016).

Endothelial dysfunction and a two-compartment inflammatory split

Eryptotic cells adhere to endothelium via PS/CXCL16 and are subsequently internalised by endothelial cells with induction of endothelial ferroptosis (Tkachenko 2026; Tier 4→3, human outcome data absent). The macrophage compartment responds oppositely: efferocytosis of eryptotic cells reprograms macrophages toward a pro-resolving phenotype (HO-1-dependent, demonstrated in intracerebral hemorrhage models, Tier 3). The same PS signal is therefore pro-injury at the endothelium and pro-resolution in the immune compartment; the net in-vivo sign is untested in humans.

Microcirculatory impairment and a feed-forward loop

Adhesion produces microaggregates and reduced capillary flow, documented in sickle cell disease and diabetes and implicated in hemolytic uremic syndrome; in ESRD the risk is amplified when ESA dosing is forced to high targets (Lang 2017). Mechanistically the arms form a loop: oxidative stress / uremic toxins / energy depletion → cation-channel opening → Ca²⁺ → PS exposure → accelerated clearance → anemia → hypoxia and ESA escalation → more eryptosis-prone cells, while PS-mediated adhesion → microvascular compromise → local hypoxia → further channel activation. Each link is individually reported; the end-to-end loop is not experimentally closed in humans — it is a mechanistic description, not a clinical entity (Tier 4/2 links).

Failure mode 2 — insufficient eryptosis and under-clearance

Two distinct deficits are often conflated and must be separated. Insufficient death signalling: damaged cells fail to initiate eryptosis, or the program is suppressed pharmacologically. Under-clearance: eryptosis executes — PS exposure, shrinkage — but efferocytosis is saturated (massive synchronized cell death, as after oxidant exposure) or impaired (Kupffer-cell dysfunction, stabilin-1/2 signalling defects, EV clearance overload), so PS-positive cells remain in circulation. The documented consequences fall into two arms.

Damaged cells persist → the hemolysis cascade

Hemolysis releases Hb, heme, ATP, IL-33 and HSP70. Free Hb scavenges nitric oxide → vasoconstriction, platelet activation, endothelial dysfunction. Free heme drives Fenton chemistry, TLR4/NF-κB endothelial activation, TNF-α release, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, complement recruitment and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and heme-driven programmed death of endothelial and immune cells (necroptosis, HO-1-dependent ferroptosis, pyroptosis). Renal filtering of released hemoproteins causes tubular injury; haptoglobin and hemopexin buffering is saturable. This pathophysiology is well established in human disease — sickle cell, thalassemia, transfusion reactions, mechanical valves (Tier 4/3; human disease physiology).

PS-positive cells persist → procoagulant and pro-adhesive burden

Eryptotic PS-positive cells do not clear locally: hepatic sinusoid stabilin-1/2 recognition and Kupffer-cell uptake dominate (80% of RBC-derived vesicles removed within 5 min experimentally, Tier 3–4). If clearance is saturated or impaired, the cells retain the procoagulant surface and CXCL16/CD36 adhesive phenotype described above, so thrombotic and microcirculatory risk persists instead of resolving within minutes (Tier 4; direct human outcome data absent). Transfusion of stored red-cell concentrates — which accumulate PS-positive microvesicles and DAMP-carrying EVs — can prolong PS exposure in recipients; clinical consequences are unquantified (Tier 3–4/2, storage-lesion association). CD47–SIRPα is the constitutive "don't-eat-me" brake; pathological CD47 retention would delay clearance, but direct clinical correlates are unverified (Tier 5/4).

The host-defence cost of suppression

Plasmodium-infected erythrocytes depend on eryptosis for clearance; suppressing the program leaves infected cells circulating. This is the principal in-vivo cost of anti-eryptotic therapy, alongside unresolved interference with cytostatic tumour-cell apoptosis (Tier 3/2, epidemiology of the protective G6PD, sickle and thalassemia traits). In hepatic failure the two failure modes coexist: bilirubin and bile-acid excess drives erythrocyte Ca²⁺ influx and ceramide formation — more eryptosis, not less.

Clinical status of the under-clearance arm

A defined human "under-eryptosis" syndrome is not established. The failure mode manifests as hemolysis (when cells lyse rather than being cleared) or, less often measured, as persistent PS-positive cells after massive oxidative or uremic insult. No biomarker-based diagnostic threshold and no interventional trial exist. Treat this arm as a mechanistic counterweight to the excess arm, not a recognized indication.

The adrenochrome crossover — shared nodes, undemonstrated link

Epinephrine is oxidized to adrenochrome non-enzymatically under oxidative stress (superoxide, H₂O₂, hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry with Fe²⁺/Cu²⁺) and enzymatically via neutrophil myeloperoxidase. The human erythrocyte plasma membrane converts adrenaline to aminochromes including adrenochrome in a two-phase reaction (PMID 8937691), and hemoglobin catalyzes oxidation of adrenaline in blood — both Tier 4. Erythrocytes are therefore simultaneously a minor site of adrenochrome formation, a reducing sink that limits its persistence (diaphorase and ascorbate reduce adrenochrome back, holding steady-state levels in the nanomolar range), and a target of its oxidative chemistry: membrane lipid peroxidation, methemoglobin formation, and hemolysis at high concentrations.

The two systems share mechanistic nodes: adrenochrome semiquinone redox cycling regenerates the parent quinone while generating superoxide; adrenochrome depletes glutathione; and both molecules' chemistry consumes the same NADPH/GSH reserve maintained by G6PD and the pentose phosphate pathway — the very reserve whose depletion lowers the eryptotic threshold. But the specific step "adrenochrome → eryptosis" is mechanistically supported and experimentally undemonstrated: no in-vitro dose–response of adrenochrome on human erythrocytes with annexin V / Ca²⁺ / hemolysis readouts was identified, adrenochrome is not among the tested eryptosis inducers in the current reviews, and whether sub-lytic adrenochrome preferentially triggers eryptosis over hemolysis is not quantified and untested (Tier 5).

Hemostasis — opposing directions from one molecule family

Adrenochrome derivatives (adrenochrome monoaminoguanidine, carbazochrome) have historical clinical use as capillary hemostatics (Tier 2, limited modern validation). Eryptotic PS-positive cells provide the catalytic surface for prothrombinase assembly, contributing up to ~40% of thrombin generation in some pathological conditions. These are opposite coagulation-relevant phenotypes of the same compound family; they do not cancel and should not be conflated.

Parkinson's disease as shared context — and no more

COMT inhibitors used in Parkinson's disease shift catecholamine flux toward oxidation products; adrenochrome forms as an intermediate in catecholaminergic neuromelanin synthesis. Independently, altered calpain and ceramide production trigger eryptosis in Parkinson's disease, and amyloid-β causes oxidative erythrocyte damage in Alzheimer's disease (Tier 4–2). Neither axis establishes a causal chain from peripheral adrenochrome burden to eryptosis-driven anemia; both are documented in the same disease context. The proposed graph trace adrenochrome → DGCR8/Drosha microprocessor modification → pri-miRNA processing → senescence/SASP → inflammaging is a vault artefact, not experimentally validated (Tier 5).

What a decisive experiment would look like

Ordered by information yield per unit effort — none has been performed:

  1. Direct dose–response. Washed human erythrocytes, adrenochrome 0.1–100 µM, readouts: annexin V–FITC (PS), Fluo-4 (Ca²⁺), forward scatter (shrinkage), hemolysis (Hb, 414 nm). Blocks: extracellular Ca²⁺ exclusion, charybdotoxin (Gardos), NAC. If adrenochrome is a bona fide eryptosis inducer, PS exposure should precede lysis and be Ca²⁺-dependent.
  2. G6PD-stratified sensitivity. G6PD-deficient versus normal erythrocytes under identical exposure; prediction: left-shifted dose–response in deficient cells.
  3. The reducing-sink arm. Intact versus diaphorase-inhibited (or reconstituted) cells, to quantify how much adrenochrome the erythrocyte buffering system disposes of before oxidative damage accrues.
  4. Ferroptosis discrimination. GPX4 arylation assay; rescue with ferrostatin-1/liproxstatin-1 would separate ferroptosis from eryptosis.
  5. In-vivo half-life. Mouse RBC half-life after sub-toxic adrenochrome/carbazochrome exposure, with and without NAC pre-treatment.

Position for longevity and anti-aging research

Eryptosis is a quality-control clearance arm, not a senescence regulator in the strict sense. The defensible framings are three. First, prevention of lytic, pro-inflammatory disposal: eryptosis removes compromised cells before hemolysis releases Hb, heme and DAMPs into the NO-scavenging, TLR4-activating, complement-recruiting cascade (Tier 4–3). Second, systemic redox and erythrocyte energy metabolism as a longevity readout: the G6PD/PPP → NADPH → glutathione reductase budget, the NO → sGC → cGMP → cGKI axis (eNOS-null mice clear RBCs faster, Tier 3), AMPK (AMPKα-null mice: splenomegaly, anemia, Tier 3), and EPO (acute inhibition, chronic pro-eryptotic) all set the eryptotic threshold, and susceptibility rises with cell age — NAC blocked eryptosis in vitro and prolonged mouse RBC half-life in vivo (Tier 3–4; human translation unreported). Third, biomarker framing: eryptosis indexes oxidative, uremic and inflammatory load, correlating with MPO, Cu/Zn SOD and IL-6 (Tier 2) — useful as an exposure and severity marker, not validated as a longevity surrogate endpoint.

Adrenochrome occupies the complementary position: a marker and amplifier of oxidative, catecholamine-rich, inflammatory states — the same states that accelerate eryptosis (CKD/ESRD, sepsis, diabetes, heart failure, hepatic failure). The defensible claim is limited: adrenochrome burden and eryptosis acceleration are both plausibly elevated in these states, and both are measurably associated with adverse vascular and hematologic outcomes. Neither is an established longevity or healthspan target; all intervention evidence is Tier 3–4 or absent. Treated as biomarkers of systemic oxidative load, they are useful stratification tools; treated as intervention targets, they are unvalidated.

Host defence additionally bounds intervention: Plasmodium-infected erythrocytes are cleared by eryptosis, and the traits that lower the eryptotic threshold (G6PD deficiency, sickle trait, thalassemia trait) are geographically selected precisely because they protect against malaria (Tier 2). Any anti-eryptotic strategy must price in this cost, alongside unresolved interference with cytostatic tumour-cell apoptosis.

Evidence limits and translation gaps

  • No human RCT of anti-eryptotic therapy exists; efficacy of eryptosis inhibitors (NAC, the NO/cGMP axis, ion-channel modulators, EPO) in ESRD or any indication is unestablished. Interventional claims are Tier 3–4 only.
  • The normal eryptotic fraction is not quantified. Annexin V also binds non-eryptotic states; markers give discordant results (lactadherin vs annexin V); assay standardization is an active recommendation of Tkachenko 2025.
  • The under-clearance arm is inferred, not demonstrated in an interventional human cohort: saturation of hepatic/Kupffer clearance and transfusion-related PS/EV burden are unquantified clinically.
  • Causality: most disease associations are cohort/ex-vivo; confounding by uremia, inflammation and ESA dose is rarely fully adjusted.
  • The adrenochrome step is untested: no adrenochrome dose–response on human erythrocytes; no evidence that physiological adrenochrome concentrations reach cation-channel activation thresholds; no RCT of any adrenochrome-scavenging intervention. The schizophrenia erythrocyte–adrenochrome literature is unreplicated and should not be cited as support.
  • The feed-forward loop is not closed end-to-end in humans; each link is individually reported.
Bottom line

Eryptosis is the non-lytic, Ca²⁺/PS-executed quality-control clearance of damaged erythrocytes. It fails in two directions: excess produces anemia with ESA hypo-responsiveness, PS-driven thrombin generation, endothelial adhesion with ferroptotic injury, and microcirculatory compromise (documented across CKD/ESRD, diabetes, heart failure, cirrhosis, sepsis); insufficient signalling or under-clearance leaves damaged or PS-positive cells to lyse or persist — NO scavenging, heme/TLR4 inflammatory injury, renal hemoprotein injury, sustained procoagulant burden, and loss of Plasmodium clearance (mechanistically supported, no defined human syndrome). The normal eryptotic fraction is not quantified, and all intervention data are preclinical. For longevity purposes, eryptosis — with adrenochrome as a convergent oxidative-stress marker — is best treated as a biomarker of systemic load and an upstream driver of vascular and hematologic decline, not as an established anti-aging target.
