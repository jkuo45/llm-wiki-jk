Lipid Peroxidation — radical chain oxidation of membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids

Lipid peroxidation (LPO) is a free-radical chain reaction. An initiating radical abstracts a bis-allylic hydrogen from a polyunsaturated fatty acid in a membrane phospholipid or lipoprotein; the carbon-centered radical reacts with O₂ to form a peroxyl radical that abstracts hydrogen from an adjacent PUFA, propagating the chain and yielding lipid hydroperoxides. Hydroperoxide fragmentation generates reactive aldehydes, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE). Aldehyde adducts on proteins and DNA and accumulation of oxidized phospholipids are associated in the vault literature with atherosclerosis, ferroptosis, and neuronal injury. At lower flux, lipid hydroperoxides and 4-HNE are reported as second messengers in cell-based assays ([[Oxidative Stress]]); the flux threshold separating signaling from adduct formation is not quantified in the vault note.

Chain amplification from a single initiating radical

Membrane phospholipids and circulating lipoproteins contain PUFAs with bis-allylic methylene hydrogens of low bond dissociation energy (~75 kcal/mol, as cited in the vault note). Hydroxyl radical, peroxynitrite, and ferryl complexes abstract these hydrogens at near-diffusion-limited rates. The resulting carbon-centered radical reacts with O₂ on a microsecond timescale to form a peroxyl radical, which abstracts hydrogen from an adjacent PUFA and regenerates a carbon-centered radical. One initiating event therefore yields dozens to hundreds of oxidized lipid molecules before termination.

Kinetics of chain propagation

Initiation is rate-limiting; propagation proceeds rapidly (L• + O₂ at k ≈ 10⁸ M⁻¹s⁻¹). Fenton decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides generates alkoxyl radicals that initiate additional chains, increasing the number of propagating chains until radical recombination or interception of LOO• by a chain-breaking antioxidant terminates them.

Double-bond count and oxidizability

Oxidizability scales with the number of bis-allylic positions: DHA (22:6) is ~10-fold more oxidizable than linoleic acid (18:2) in the cited comparison. DHA-rich neuronal membranes and arachidonate-rich signaling pools therefore carry the highest density of peroxidizable substrate.

Electrophilic aldehyde adducts

Hydroperoxide fragmentation yields electrophilic aldehydes (MDA, 4-HNE, acrolein) that form covalent adducts with protein cysteine, histidine, and lysine residues and with DNA (M₁dG lesions). These adducts persist after the initiating radicals have been consumed and are quantified as TBARS, HNE–protein adducts, and M₁dG.

Dose-dependent signaling and adduct formation

At low concentrations, 4-HNE activates the NRF2/ARE transcriptional program in cell-based assays, increasing expression of glutathione-synthesis enzymes, heme oxygenase-1, and phase-II detoxification genes. At higher concentrations, the same electrophiles form protein and DNA adducts and are associated with ferroptosis and apoptosis in experimental models. The concentration separating these two outcomes is not quantified in the vault note ([[Oxidative Stress]]).

Initiation, propagation, and termination

The reaction is described in three phases: initiation, propagation, and termination. Rate constants below are as cited in the vault Lipid Peroxidation note.

Fenton decomposition of lipid hydroperoxides

LOOH is not a terminal product. Fenton chemistry (LOOH + Fe²⁺ → LO• + OH⁻ + Fe³⁺) converts each hydroperoxide into an alkoxyl radical that can initiate a new chain, increasing the number of propagating chains. This iron dependence is the mechanistic basis of ferroptosis, defined as iron-dependent lipid peroxidation; iron chelation with deferoxamine suppresses the cascade in cellular ferroptosis models.

Source: Berrougui et al., Atherosclerosis 2009

Lipid peroxidation is characterized by the formation of conjugated dienes, lipid hydroperoxides, TBARS, and aldehydes. In LDL, oxidation of PUFAs produces conjugated dienes (measured at 234 nm) and alters the apoB protein moiety. In HDL, lipid peroxidation alters phospholipid fluidity, modifies apoprotein structure, and decreases PON1 activity.

Major products and reactivity

Primary products are lipid hydroperoxides (LOOH). Fragmentation yields secondary carbonyl species; several of these are quantified as biomarkers of lipid peroxidation.

Electrophilic aldehyde adducts on proteins and DNA

4-HNE and MDA are electrophiles that form covalent adducts with protein cysteine, histidine, and lysine residues and with DNA (M₁dG lesions). Aldehyde cross-linking of apoB is a step in oxidized LDL formation. 4-HNE adduction of the astrocytic glutamate transporter GLT-1 (EAAT2) is associated with reduced glutamate uptake in experimental models, a proposed route to excitotoxic injury.

NRF2/ARE activation by 4-HNE

At low concentrations 4-HNE activates the NRF2/ARE pathway in cell-based assays, increasing expression of glutathione-synthesis enzymes, heme oxygenase-1, and phase-II detoxification genes. At higher concentrations 4-HNE forms protein and DNA adducts. The concentration threshold between these outcomes is not quantified in the vault note.

Enzymatic and non-enzymatic oxidation — PUFA structural determinants

Enzymatic peroxidation

Stereoselective oxidoreductases convert arachidonic acid to defined signaling products. Lipoxygenases (LOX) insert O₂ at specific positions, producing leukotrienes and hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs). Cyclooxygenases (COX-1/COX-2) catalyse bis-dioxygenation to prostaglandin G₂, initiating the prostanoid cascade. Products are position- and stereo-defined.

Non-enzymatic peroxidation

Radical attack produces isomeric product mixtures, including isoprostanes from arachidonic acid. Position and stereochemistry are not enzyme-controlled, so F₂-isoprostane formation rate tracks radical flux rather than enzyme expression; F₂-isoprostanes are quantified by GC-MS/LC-MS as in vivo markers of oxidative damage.

Bis-allylic methylene count and oxidizability

Bis-allylic methylenes are the sites of hydrogen abstraction. Arachidonic acid (20:4, n-6) and docosahexaenoic acid (22:6, n-3) carry several; DHA, with six double bonds, is ~10-fold more oxidizable than linoleic acid (18:2) in the cited comparison. Neuronal membranes are enriched in DHA, which increases the local concentration of peroxidizable substrate. Vitamin E and plasmalogens are present in neural tissue and act as chain-breaking and sacrificial antioxidants.

Substrate-pool control — the acid ceramidase / Lands-cycle axis

The extent of lipid peroxidation depends on the abundance of oxidizable PUFA in membrane phospholipids. Soriano-Castell et al. (Cell Death Dis 2026) reported acid ceramidase (ACase / ASAH1) overexpression of 5–20× in replicatively senescent fibroblasts in vitro. Ceramide catabolism increased the pool of peroxidizable phospholipids, conferring ferroptosis sensitivity through a route reported as independent of GPX4, GSH, and labile iron.

The Lands-cycle loading mechanism

Ceramide synthesis sequesters free saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids away from phospholipid PUFA. ACase cleaves ceramide (→ sphingosine + free fatty acid), and the liberated SFA/MUFA enter the Lands cycle, where they are exchanged into the sn-2 position of membrane phospholipids, increasing PUFA-containing species (arachidonate-PLs, PC-LA, PC-DHA, PE-DHA, PG-DHA). The resulting PL-PUFA pool is the substrate for iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. ACase knockdown or ARN14794 inhibition reduced the PL-PUFA pool and protected against RSL3-induced ferroptosis in the reported cell model.

Effects independent of GPX4, GSH, iron, and ACSL4

In ACase-knockdown cells, protection persisted while GPX4 protein decreased; protection also survived BSO-mediated GSH depletion despite elevated GSH, and labile iron increased alongside ferritin. ACSL4 protein was unchanged despite the reduced PL-PUFA pool. These results place ACase upstream of the GPX4/GSH/Fe²⁺ machinery, acting on substrate-pool size.

SASP-mediated bystander effects and ACase inhibitor concentrations

In conditioned-medium experiments, SASP cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 at a 2:1 ratio increased ACase expression, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis sensitivity in neighboring proliferative cells (SASP → ACase → pro-ferroptotic lipid profile). ACase also generates sphingosine-1-phosphate, which is anti-apoptotic in senescent cells; the same metabolic shift is associated with increased ferroptosis sensitivity. Reported inhibitor IC₅₀ values: ARN14794 ~100 nM, ARN14974 (brain-penetrant) 79 nM, carmofur (clinically approved) 29 nM. In the reported model, ACase inhibition reduced ferroptosis sensitivity (senomorphic action) while the senescence arrest and SASP remained intact; effects on bystander cells were not quantified.

Physiological and pathological consequences

Peroxidation inserts polar groups into the hydrophobic core of the bilayer, increasing permeability, altering membrane fluidity, and increasing fusion tendency. Secondary aldehydes form covalent adducts with proteins and DNA.

Atherosclerosis

LDL lipid peroxidation produces oxidized LDL, which is taken up by macrophage scavenger receptors (SR-A, CD36) without feedback regulation → foam cells. 4-HNE-modified plaque proteins activate NF-κB in experimental models. HDL peroxidation reduces cholesterol efflux and PON1 activity. Concentrations of LPO markers correlate with plaque progression in the cited literature.

Mitochondria & apoptosis

Cardiolipin peroxidation, driven by ROS at Complex III, releases cytochrome c from the inner mitochondrial membrane and activates the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Oxidized cardiolipin is also reported as a mitophagy signal, linking membrane peroxidation to organelle clearance.

Ferroptosis

Iron-dependent lipid peroxidation is the execution mechanism of ferroptosis, a regulated necrotic death blocked by radical-trapping antioxidants (ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1) and iron chelators but not by caspase inhibitors. ACSL4/LPCAT3 incorporate oxidizable PUFA into phospholipids; GPX4 reduces the resulting phospholipid hydroperoxides to the corresponding alcohols. Substrate supply is regulated: the senescence-associated acid ceramidase axis increases PUFA-phospholipid abundance via the Lands cycle (see Pathways).

Neurodegeneration

Elevated MDA and 4-HNE concentrations are reported in CSF and tissue in Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and ALS. 4-HNE adduction of astrocytic GLT-1 (EAAT2) is associated with reduced glutamate uptake in experimental models; aldehyde adduction of tau has been reported to promote oligomerization in vitro. DHA-rich membranes are a proposed basis for selective neuronal vulnerability.

Catecholamine-driven myocardial LPO

Catecholamine autoxidation is an initiating source: epinephrine oxidation yields superoxide and H₂O₂ → Fenton •OH → myocardial membrane peroxidation, ion-homeostasis failure, and contractile dysfunction. Aminochromes (adrenochrome) redox-cycle with NADPH and consume glutathione. An association with catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy and Takotsubo syndrome is reported in the vault catecholamine section.

Glycation crosstalk

Lipid peroxidation-derived dicarbonyls contribute to the advanced glycation end-product (AGE) pool; glucose autoxidation generates radical and carbonyl species that contribute to lipid peroxidation. The two pathways share carbonyl chemistry, and their products converge on RAGE-driven inflammation and protein cross-linking.

Role of lipid peroxidation in atherosclerosis and ferroptosis

Lipid peroxidation converts radical flux into structural membrane damage: hydroperoxides propagate the chain, and secondary aldehydes form covalent adducts with proteins and DNA. Oxidized LDL formation and iron-dependent ferroptotic cell death are the disease-linked outcomes documented in the vault ([[Atherosclerosis]], [[Ferroptosis]]).

Speculative bridge — adrenochrome as a dual ferroptosis inducer

A task-output analysis (Research Scientist agent, 17 Jul 2026) describes a direct chemical route from catecholamine oxidation to lipid peroxidation and proposes a second mechanism: electrophilic modification of GPX4 by adrenochrome. The redox-cycling chemistry is documented in the vault notes; GPX4 inhibition by adrenochrome is unverified experimentally.

Redox-cycling o-quinone

Adrenochrome is reduced to the semiquinone radical and leuco-adrenochrome and re-oxidized to adrenochrome. Semiquinone oxidation transfers an electron to O₂ → superoxide (SOD → H₂O₂ → Fenton •OH). The cycle is catalytic, not stoichiometric: a single molecule can generate multiple superoxide equivalents while NADPH and GSH re-reduce the quinone. The number of ROS equivalents per molecule is not quantified in the vault note.

GSH depletion and adrenochrome redox cycling

4-HNE and MDA are reported to impair mitochondria and deplete glutathione. Reduced GSH availability slows adrenochrome reduction and detoxification, prolonging redox cycling and •OH generation, which increases lipid peroxidation. This positive feedback is described in the vault catecholamine section; the flux magnitudes are not quantified.

Dopaminochrome redox cycling

Dopamine oxidation in the substantia nigra yields dopaminochrome, which undergoes the same o-quinone redox cycling and accumulates in neuromelanin with age. A parallel route — catecholamine o-quinone → lipid peroxidation → cell death — is proposed for dopaminergic neuron loss in Parkinson's disease and remains untested in humans.

Proposed dual mechanism — unverified

Hypothesis: in catecholamine-rich cells (cardiomyocytes, dopaminergic neurons), adrenochrome (1) supplies •OH-initiated phospholipid peroxidation through redox cycling and (2) as an electrophilic o-quinone, arylates the GPX4 selenocysteine active site by analogy with RSL3, reducing GPX4 activity. Proposed test panel: C11-BODIPY oxidation, TBARS, recombinant GPX4 activity, and a rescue matrix (ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1, necrostatin-1, Z-VAD-FMK) to assign the death modality. Contexts reported in the vault note: catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy, Takotsubo syndrome, and ischemia-reperfusion injury, where neutrophil-derived myeloperoxidase converts locally elevated epinephrine to adrenochrome in reperfused myocardium.

Graph connectivity of the adrenochrome–lipid peroxidation edge

Inhibition of lipid peroxidation

Inhibitors act at three points: interception of the chain-carrying peroxyl radical (radical trapping), reduction of lipid hydroperoxides, and chelation of Fe²⁺ to limit Fenton decomposition of LOOH.

Dietary lipophilic antioxidants — reported oil composition

Interception of LOO• and quenching of ¹O₂ in the lipid phase require lipophilic compounds incorporated into the bilayer. A task output (Queen Palm biochemical profile, 23 Sep 2026) groups fruit-pulp oils by carotenoid and tocopherol content co-delivered in a lipid matrix, a different metric from water-soluble antioxidant capacity. Values below derive from a Gemini conversation export and are not validated composition data.

Tier 1 · Highest reported carotenoid and tocotrienol densities

Buriti palm (Mauritia flexuosa) — carotenoid density reported at 5–10× that of Queen Palm pulp oil. Red palm oil (Elaeis guineensis) — tocotrienols reported at 40–60× the efficiency of α-tocopherol in the cited membrane assay; assay model not specified in the task output. Sea buckthorn — highest lipophilic ORAC in the task-output ranking (value not quantified) and palmitoleic acid (omega-7).

Tier 2 · Queen Palm pulp oil and comparable pulp oils

Queen Palm (Syagrus romanzoffiana, jerivá) pulp oil: up to ~1,200 μg/g total carotenoids (β-carotene predominant) and >320 mg/kg α-tocopherol, co-delivered in an oleic/linoleic lipid fraction. Comparable oils in the task-output ranking: jelly palm, rosehip, açaí. Açaí has higher reported hydrophilic anthocyanin content; Queen Palm has higher reported provitamin-A carotenoid content, relevant to ¹O₂ quenching.

Tier 3 · Standard agronomic crops

Carrots and sweet potatoes contain more β-carotene by weight but lack an intrinsic lipid matrix; absorption and membrane incorporation require added dietary fat. Common cultivated fruits (apples, plums, grapes) contain water-soluble polyphenols; LOO•-trapping lipophilic capacity was not quantified in the task output.

Membrane-dependent processes affected by lipid peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation affects several membrane-dependent processes: Na⁺/K⁺- and Ca²⁺-ATPase ion-pump gradients required for neuronal and muscle excitability; cardiolipin-dependent cristae organization and ETC supercomplex assembly (mitochondrial uncoupling and mPTP opening are reported failure modes); oxidized lipids and 4-HNE as DAMP signals in TLR4/NF-κB and NLRP3–IL-1β signaling; eNOS-coupled NO availability in vascular endothelium; and 4-HNE adduction of amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein, reported in vitro to accelerate misfolding. Endogenous inhibitors (GPX4, vitamin E) and dietary tocopherols and carotenoids act through the same chain-termination and ¹O₂-quenching chemistry. Effects of dietary intake on these outcomes remain untested in humans.
