## Abstract

Senile osteoporosis (SOP) is an age-related metabolic bone disease characterized by reduced bone quality and increased fracture risk, closely associated with immune senescence. Among immune components in the bone marrow niche, macrophages undergo age-associated phenotypic and functional changes that may impair bone homeostasis; however, whether these changes reflect bona fide senescence, inflammatory activation, or polarization remains unclear. This review summarizes the biological characteristics of senescent macrophages, including alterations in senescence markers, senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), epigenetic modifications, and telomere dynamics. It then outlines their functional changes, focusing on oxidative stress, autophagy, and metabolic dysregulation. Importantly, we discuss how senescent or senescence-like macrophage states may regulate macrophage–bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) crosstalk through SASP-related factors, the grancalcin–plexin-B2 axis, mitochondrial transfer, exosome-mediated signaling, and metabolic reprogramming, while distinguishing direct ageing-related evidence from indirect mechanistic evidence.

## Introduction

With the global population aging at an accelerating rate, the prevalence of age-related chronic diseases, including osteoporosis (OP), is steadily increasing \[[^1]\]. Senile osteoporosis (SOP) is a disorder of bone metabolism characterized by disruptions in bone microstructure, reduced beam spacing, and thinning of cortical bone, leading to compromised bone strength and heightened fracture susceptibility. Owing to increasing life expectancy, SOP has become a significant social issue faced by middle-aged and elderly individuals \[[^2], [^3]\].

Aging is accompanied by a progressive decline in the structure and function of the immune system, a process commonly referred to as immunosenescence \[[^4]\]. Both infiltrating and tissue-resident macrophages are major components of the aging microenvironment, indicating that macrophages play a crucial role in age-related immune remodeling \[[^5]\]. As highly active innate immune cells in bone tissue, macrophages are essential for maintaining bone metabolic homeostasis \[[^6]\]. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward macrophage senescence and age-associated macrophage dysfunction in OP, suggesting that altered macrophage states may contribute to the remodeling of the bone immune microenvironment in SOP.

Several recent reviews have provided important insights into macrophage-related mechanisms in osteoporosis and skeletal aging. For example, Pappert et al. recently discussed the influence of aged bone marrow macrophages on skeletal health and senescence, highlighting the emerging relevance of macrophage ageing in skeletal degeneration \[[^7]\]. In addition, Wang et al. summarized macrophage polarization in bone resorption and formation \[[^8]\], Xing et al. discussed macrophage immunometabolism and precision therapies in osteoporotic fractures \[[^9]\], and Wang et al. reviewed immune microenvironment remodeling during bone aging \[[^10]\]. Moreover, the recently proposed concept of “immunoporosis” further emphasizes that ageing immune cells may disturb the balance between bone formation and resorption through senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors, inflammaging, and other immune-related pathways \[[^11]\]. While these studies have advanced the field, the potential role of senescent macrophages in shaping the bone immune microenvironment in senile osteoporosis remains insufficiently addressed. In particular, their interactions with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and other bone-resident cells via SASP factors, mitochondrial transfer, exosomes, and metabolic reprogramming have not been systematically integrated. Therefore, this review focuses on macrophage senescence- and senescence-like state-associated cellular cross-talk in SOP, aiming to clarify how altered macrophage states may reshape the aged bone marrow niche and contribute to impaired osteogenesis, increased inflammation, and bone loss.

To define the scope of this narrative review, we addressed macrophage senescence and senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction in SOP. Relevant studies were identified from PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using terms related to “senile osteoporosis”, “bone ageing”, “macrophage senescence”, “aged macrophages”, “SASP”, “macrophage polarization”, “BMSCs”, “mitochondrial transfer”, and “exosomes”. Given the limited direct evidence on senescent macrophages in SOP, we classified the literature according to model relevance and evidence strength, including ageing/SOP-related studies, stress-induced senescence models, inflammatory or polarized macrophage models, and therapeutic or biomaterial studies related to macrophage dysfunction.

### Origin, distribution, and categorization of macrophages

Macrophages are highly plastic innate immune cells that contribute to tissue homeostasis, immune surveillance, inflammation resolution, and tissue repair \[[^12], [^13], [^14], [^15]\]. In bone tissue, macrophages are important components of the bone immune microenvironment and participate in the regulation of bone remodeling through interactions with osteoblasts, osteoclasts, BMSCs, and other stromal or immune cells \[[^16], [^17]\].

Macrophages generally arise from tissue-resident populations and recruited monocyte-derived cells \[[^18], [^19], [^20]\] (Fig. [1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Fig1)). Their phenotypes are shaped by local microenvironmental cues and are often simplified into pro-inflammatory M1-like and reparative M2-like states \[[^21]\]. However, this binary classification does not fully capture macrophage heterogeneity in ageing bone marrow. In particular, macrophages in aged or diseased bone may acquire senescence-associated features that are distinct from ordinary inflammatory activation or classical polarization states \[[^22], [^23]\]. Therefore, a clear distinction between macrophage polarization, inflammatory activation, and macrophage senescence is essential when interpreting macrophage-related mechanisms in SOP.

### Distinguishing macrophage senescence from inflammatory activation and polarization

A recent review described macrophage senescence as a state of stable cell-cycle arrest, SASP-driven chronic inflammation, and widespread functional changes in metabolism, autophagy, polarization, phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and tissue infiltration \[[^22]\]. A clear distinction should be made between senescent macrophages and other related macrophage states, including aged macrophages, inflammatory macrophages, and polarized macrophages. Aged macrophages are generally isolated from aged organisms and may exhibit functional decline, metabolic remodeling, and increased inflammatory responses; however, ageing alone does not necessarily indicate cellular senescence. Similarly, M1-like polarization or LPS-induced inflammatory activation is characterized by enhanced inflammatory cytokine production, but it should not be considered equivalent to macrophage senescence.

Bona fide macrophage senescence should ideally be supported by a composite panel of senescence-associated features rather than by a single marker \[[^24], [^25]\]. These features may include SA-β-gal positivity, increased p16, p21, p53 or γH2AX expression, persistent DNA damage response, SASP production, telomere dysfunction, metabolic impairment, and/or stable cell-cycle arrest. H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress models may mimic certain aspects of stress-induced senescence, but they should be interpreted cautiously unless multiple senescence markers are validated. Therefore, in this review, evidence derived from aged macrophages, stress-induced macrophages, inflammatory macrophages, or polarized macrophages is discussed according to its model relevance and evidence strength, rather than being treated as direct evidence of senescent macrophages in SOP.

### Biological characteristics of macrophage senescence

The notion of macrophage senescence was initially proposed in 2000, revealing that macrophages have the capacity to enter a state of aging in response to signals from neighboring cells \[[^26], [^27]\]. Although this concept has been the subject of debate for an extended period, accumulating evidence has begun to define several biological attributes associated with macrophage senescence (Fig. [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Fig2)).

### Changes in expression levels of senescent markers

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) is widely recognized as a prevalent indicator of cellular senescence \[[^28], [^29]\]. In the context of macrophage senescence, there is a notable elevation in SA-β-gal activity, leading to its detectable accumulation within the cell through staining techniques. This staining typically results in a discernible blue or green reaction product, thereby facilitating the visualization of distinct color changes in the cells when examined under a microscope \[[^30]\].

Although p21, p53, p16, and γH2AX are all key markers of cellular aging, they play different roles in cells and reflect distinct aging-related processes \[[^31]\]. p21, p53, and p16 are associated with signaling pathways that respond to damage and determine cell fate, such as maintaining homeostasis, inducing cell senescence, or triggering apoptosis \[[^32]\]. Conversely, γH2AX is primarily involved in detecting and repairing DNA damage \[[^33], [^34]\]. At present, the evidence supporting cellular senescence in macrophages has been insufficient. Several studies have demonstrated an increase in the expression of senescent markers such as p53, p21, and p16 in models of macrophage senescence \[[^30], [^35]\]. Nevertheless, the involvement of γH2AX in the process of macrophage senescence remains relatively understudied.

### Senescence-associated secretory phenotype

The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) comprises a broad spectrum of secreted factors, including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases \[[^36]\]. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in studying the SASP of macrophages. Su et al. discovered that the secretions of aging bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) contain a diverse range of cytokines involved in the regulation of pulmonary fibrosis and inflammation, such as TGF-β, Arg-1, IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, Ccl2, Cxcl10, Ccl17, Mmp2, Mmp9, and Mmp12 \[[^37]\]. Recent research has demonstrated that aging macrophages and neutrophils in the bone marrow play a significant role in age-related bone loss by secreting grancalcin (GCA) \[[^38]\]. GCA has been found to upregulate and inhibit plexin-b2 receptor signaling on mesenchymal cells with aging, leading to a decrease in osteogenesis and impaired bone formation \[[^38]\]. These findings offer valuable insights into the mechanisms of bone aging and suggest potential targets for future research and the development of intervention strategies.

### Alteration of epigenetic modifications

Macrophage aging also involves a variety of epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin remodeling, which can affect gene expression, chromatin structure and function, thereby regulating macrophages. DNA methylation: One characteristic of aging is widespread DNA hypomethylation, although hypermethylation can also occur at specific sites [^39]. For example, aging is associated with hypermethylation of autophagy gene promoters, leading to transcriptional suppression [^40]. Hany Khalil’s data suggest that the low expression of autophagy molecules in elderly macrophages is caused by high levels of DNA methyltransferase 2 (DNMT2), which is highly correlated with levels of methylated autophagy molecules Atg5 and LC3B [^40]. Histone modification: Studies have shown that in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated mouse macrophages, histone demethylase Jmjd3 can remove the trimethylation mark from H3K27, thereby regulating cell differentiation and inflammatory response [^41]. Additionally, IL-4 stimulation can enhance Jmjd3 expression in macrophages [^42], and Jmjd3 appears to play a critical role in regulating the trimethylation of Irf4 during the development of normal BMDM and subsequent M2 polarization [^43]. Chromatin remodeling: Macrophage senescence may also involve changes and remodeling of chromatin structure. Jmjd3 is recognized as a significant mediator of cellular senescence due to its upregulation in response to stress signals, which connects chromatin remodeling to the activation and expression of the senescent marker p16[^44]. These epigenetic alterations may interact synergistically to govern the aging progression of macrophages. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of the intricate mechanisms and functions of epigenetic regulatory modifications in senescent macrophages requires additional investigation.

### The appearance of telomere depletion

Telomere depletion, another hallmark of cellular senescence, has been observed in macrophages and occurs during both aging and disease. BMDMs demonstrated a notable decrease in telomere length in aged mice (19–24 months) compared to young mice (6 weeks) \[[^45]\]. Additionally, progressive telomere shortening and cell cycle arrest have been observed in rat microglia, a specific type of macrophage located in the brain \[[^46]\]. In this particular case, this finding suggests a possible association between cell-cycle arrest and cellular senescence, although further characterization is required.

Telomerase is known to be able to maintain telomere length by adding new telomere repeats, enabling cells to undergo a limited division cycle \[[^47]\]. However, with the aging of cells, the activity of telomerase will gradually decline, leading to ineffective repair of the shortened telomere sequence. When the telomere sequence is shortened enough, the cell enters a state called “replicative senescence induced by telomerase loss“ \[[^48]\]. Peritoneal macrophages isolated from first-generation telomerase reverse transcriptase defective mice significantly upregulated SA-β-gal activity and expression of cell cycle arrest genes p16, p21 proteins \[[^49]\]. In addition, the loss of telomeres is the cause of enhanced oxidative stress, reduced oxidation and phosphorylation of Stat5a, and ultimately affects GM-CSF-dependent macrophage proliferation \[[^45]\]. Consequently, the shortening of telomeres may serve as a significant marker of macrophage senescence. However, it should be noted that telomere shortening is not the exclusive determinant of macrophage senescence, as various internal and external factors may also exert influence.

### The functional alterations of senescent macrophages

#### Macrophage senescence and oxidative stress

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are by-products of cell metabolism and an essential defense mechanism of immune cells against pathogens \[[^50]\]. Macrophages, as phagocytes, recognize and phagocytose pathogens, mobilizing NADPH oxidase complexes and mitochondria to release a significant amount of ROS into phagocytic vesicles for the killing and elimination of pathogens \[[^51]\]. However, excessive ROS accumulation can result in oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, leading to cellular senescence \[[^52]\]. Therefore, achieving a balance between ROS production and removal to prevent ROS-induced damage is crucial for maintaining macrophage function and attenuating senescence-associated dysfunction.

Wang et al. discovered that Hippo kinase Mst1/2 has the ability to sense intracellular ROS and then be recruited and activated. Through the phosphorylation of Keap1, Mst1/2 prevents its polymerization, thereby inhibiting the ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2, a key transcription factor involved in antioxidant defense \[[^53]\]. Overexpression of Nrf2 can rescue the senescence phenotype of macrophages caused by Mst1/2-null, including reducing oxidative damage, decreasing apoptosis, and preventing telomere loss. This study uncovers a novel mechanism by which macrophages sense ROS via the Mst1/2-Nrf2 axis and maintain cellular redox balance, thereby safeguarding macrophages against ROS-induced damage and senescence \[[^53]\] (Fig. [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Fig2)). These findings provide new insights into how macrophages respond to oxidative stress and offer potential targets for the development of anti-aging therapeutic strategies.

#### Macrophage senescence and autophagy

Autophagy serves as a crucial intracellular degradation mechanism that regulates cellular and organismal homeostasis. Dysfunctional autophagy can result in the accumulation of cellular waste, triggering inflammatory responses, cell death, metabolic dysregulation, and the onset of various diseases \[[^54]\]. Prior research has predominantly concentrated on examining the influence of autophagy on macrophage function \[[^55]\]. However, as knowledge of macrophage aging has advanced, the significance of autophagy in macrophage senescence has become a topic of growing interest.

Several mechanisms by which ageing reduces autophagy may involve alterations in the lysosomal system’s capacity to effectively clear autophagic material and disruptions in autophagy signaling pathways \[[^56]\]. For instance, dysfunctional lysosomes accumulate non-degradable substances such as lipofuscin \[[^56]\]. Bai et al. discovered that a concentration of 0.1µM ICA notably boosts the expression of autophagy-related genes LC3B/A, Atg5, and Atg7 in senescent macrophages, while decreasing the expression of P62 \[[^57]\]. Additionally, Stranks et al. reported that macrophages lacking Atg7 displayed elevated levels of inflammatory cytokine production, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, in the absence of stimulation. Furthermore, these macrophages exhibited altered metabolic profiles, characterized by increased mitochondrial content, mitochondrial ROS production, and glycolysis rates \[[^58]\]. Through a comparison of macrophages derived from young and aged mice, the researchers noted a marked decrease in autophagic activity in senescent macrophages, which resembles the characteristics displayed by Atg7-deficient macrophages \[[^58]\]. Nevertheless, the researchers did not conduct additional assessments to confirm the senescent status of autophagy-deficient macrophages, such as utilizing SA-β-gal staining or evaluating alterations in specific protein expression levels.

#### Macrophage senescence and metabolism

Previous studies have demonstrated the importance of cellular energy metabolism in regulating the activation status and function of the immune system \[[^59], [^60]\]. It has been found that senescent macrophages show significant reductions in glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which leads to immune dysfunction \[[^61]\]. Furthermore, a study published in Nature by Andreasson’s team revealed that aging immune cells, such as macrophages and microglia in the brain and other parts of the body, undergo metabolic changes, with increased conversion of glucose into glycogen storage within the cells. The underlying mechanism driving this altered energy metabolism is the inflammatory mediator prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) \[[^62]\]. The study found a substantial increase in PGE2 signaling in aging macrophages, which activates the AKT-GSK3β-GYS1 pathway, leading to the conversion of glucose to glycogen and reducing ATP production. However, inhibition of PGE2 or its corresponding receptor EP2 can restore intracellular glycolysis, mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR), membrane potential, ROS, and glucose flux to levels observed in youthful cells. Furthermore, this inhibition also improves the chronic inflammatory state and phagocytic function \[[^62]\].

In addition, Rajendra S. Apte and colleagues discovered that the upregulation of miR-33 in senescent macrophages results in impaired cholesterol excretion by suppressing ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), ultimately contributing to pathological vascular hyperplasia \[[^63]\]. Expanding upon prior research indicating lipid metabolism disturbances, the investigators have also uncovered a novel function of miR-150 in modulating cholesterol metabolism and lipid transport genes in macrophages through RNA-Seq analysis \[[^64]\]. The upregulation of miR-150 in senescent macrophages disrupts lipid metabolism in cell membranes, consequently modulating the inflammatory state of these macrophages and predisposing them to a pro-disease and pro-angiogenic phenotype. The mechanism underlying this effect involves the targeted downregulation of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 2 (SCD2) by miR-150 \[[^64]\]. These findings pertaining to metabolism have significant implications for understanding the mechanisms of macrophage senescence and can contribute to a better comprehension of and intervention in immune dysfunction and age-related diseases.

Emerging studies also suggest a potential association between altered macrophage states and skeletal metabolism. For instance, macrophage senescence or senescence-like macrophage states have been implicated in osteoporotic bone loss and impaired fracture repair \[[^65], [^66]\]. These studies contribute to the advancement of our understanding of bone health and related diseases, offering new insights for future treatment and prevention strategies.

#### Other directions of macrophage senescence

Liang et al. reported a regulatory link between miRNA-34b and macrophage senescence. Moreover, miRNA-34b binds to its target gene E2f3, resulting in a decrease in E2f3 activity and subsequently affecting downstream genes associated with proliferation \[[^67]\]. Additionally, analysis of upper respiratory tract mucosal samples from patients with severe COVID-19 infections has revealed the presence of senescent macrophages \[[^68]\]. Lee’s utilization of single-cell RNA-seq to analyze these samples provided evidence of cellular aging transformation in COVID-19 patients \[[^68]\]. They found that both ciliated epithelial cells susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 and macrophages exhibited induced transcription levels of p16<sup>INK4a</sup> and multiple SASP factors. The supernatant of Virus-induced senescence (VIS) cells was able to enhance senescence in macrophages, as indicated by SA-β-gal positivity \[[^68]\]. These findings suggest that viral infection may induce or amplify senescence-like macrophage phenotypes within inflammatory tissue microenvironments. However, because this evidence derives from an infectious disease context rather than skeletal ageing or SOP models, its relevance to macrophage senescence in bone requires further validation.

#### The importance of macrophage senescence in SOP

Macrophages, being among the most prominent innate immune cells in bone tissue, are integral to the maintenance of bone metabolic homeostasis (Fig. [3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Fig3)). Their significance within the bone microenvironment is underscored by their interactions with a variety of cell types, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and BMSCs. Macrophages can secrete bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) \[[^69]\], oncostatin M (OSM) \[[^70], [^71]\], transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) \[[^72]\] and other factors, which promote extracellular matrix formation and new bone formation. Moreover, macrophages have the capability to stimulate osteoclastogenesis through the secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β, as well as indirectly through the induction of RANKL production \[[^73], [^74]\]. Additionally, macrophages exhibit heterogeneity in their differentiation as immune cells, being able to polarize into either pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages or anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. These cells play a significant role in the regulation of bone metabolism and contribute to various aspects of bone homeostasis \[[^75], [^76]\]. M1 macrophages exhibit the ability to differentiate into mature osteoclasts and produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, which facilitate bone resorption \[[^77]\]. In contrast, M2 macrophages secrete osteogenic factors that enhance the differentiation and mineralization of osteoblast precursors and BMSCs, thereby supporting bone formation \[[^78], [^79]\].

Macrophages play a significant role in maintaining bone metabolic balance. As pivotal cellular regulators of bone immunity, macrophages can polarize into pro-inflammatory M1-like or anti-inflammatory/pro-reparative M2-like phenotypes in response to microenvironmental cues. This phenotypic plasticity is crucial for the regulation of bone metabolism and contributes to multiple facets of bone homeostasis. M1-like macrophages promote osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas M2-like macrophages secrete osteogenic and pro-reparative factors that facilitate the differentiation and mineralization of BMSCs and pre-osteoblasts, thereby supporting bone formation

The accumulating evidence indicates that the imbalance of the bone immune microenvironment is a key pathological process in SOP \[[^80], [^81]\]. Traditionally, studies have focused on the effects of macrophages on the differentiation of osteoblasts or osteoclasts. However, recent discoveries suggest that the status of macrophages is closely related to the interaction of BMSCs within the bone marrow microenvironment and determines different outcomes \[[^82], [^83]\]. Linda Vi et al. discovered that macrophages in young mice can secrete factors that promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, while this ability is diminished in aged mice \[[^84]\]. Furthermore, older mice or elderly individuals displayed a marked increase in M1-polarized macrophages, with senescent macrophages exhibiting heightened production of inflammatory mediators \[[^85], [^86], [^87]\]. Similarly, Li et al. observed a significant augmentation of senescent macrophages in the bone marrow microenvironment of aging rats and mice, along with elevated levels of M1 macrophage markers, leading to an imbalance in BMSC osteogenic/adipogenic differentiation and a significant decline in bone quality \[[^38]\]. Consistently, recent single-cell transcriptomic analysis of osteal macrophages in an experimental osteoporosis model identified a distinct macrophage subset with senescence- and inflammation-related features, further supporting the involvement of senescence-associated macrophage states in osteoporotic bone microenvironment remodeling \[[^23]\].

Collectively, these findings suggest that senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction may remodel the bone immune microenvironment, with macrophage–BMSC crosstalk representing one of the most extensively studied and mechanistically relevant interactions, as detailed below.

### Mechanisms of macrophage senescence in SOP

#### Regulation of autophagy

In recent years, there has been a growing focus on the involvement of macrophages in bone immunomodulation during the process of normal bone regeneration \[[^88], [^89]\]. Tao et al. found that the activation of autophagy in BMDMs can hinder the development of osteoclasts induced by RANKL through inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway, ultimately improving SOP \[[^90]\]. Furthermore, autophagy activation may help attenuate macrophage-associated inflammatory aging in OP. In general, age-associated macrophage dysfunction is often accompanied by a shift toward a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype and a reduction in pro-reparative M2-like features, contributing to a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, commonly referred to as inflammaging \[[^57]\]. Although autophagy activation has demonstrated notable anti-aging effects in various contexts, its precise involvement in macrophage senescence-associated inflammaging during SOP progression remains incompletely elucidated.

Bai et al. provided evidence that the inflammaging state of macrophages can induce senescence-like changes in young BMSCs, leading to a significant reduction in their osteogenic potential \[[^57]\] (Table [1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Tab1)). It was found that an optimal concentration of Icariin (ICA) can attenuate the inflammatory aging process of macrophages by up-regulating the expression of autophagy-related genes LC3B/A, Atg5, and Atg7 through the activation of autophagy. ICA reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory M1-like markers, including CD86, iNOS, IL-1β, and TNF-α, while increasing the expression of pro-reparative M2-like markers, such as CD163, CD206, and IL-10. Concurrently, ICA decreased the levels of SASP-related factors, including TNF, Csf1, IL-1α, Mmp9, and Ccl2 \[[^57]\] (Fig. [4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Fig4)). Taken together, the available evidence indicates that enhancing autophagy may represent a potential strategy to mitigate osteoporotic bone loss by attenuating macrophage-associated inflammation and senescence-like dysfunction.

#### Secreting new SASP factors: GCA

Prior research has demonstrated that macrophages in young animals facilitate fracture healing through the secretion of regenerative factors, including Lrp1, whereas macrophages transplanted from older mice hinder the healing process \[[^84]\]. With advancing age, the accumulation of senescent cells in the bone marrow leads to the secretion of SASPs, which are increasingly associated with age-related bone degenerative conditions such as SOP \[[^91], [^92]\]. Li et al. analyzed bone marrow supernatants from young and old rats using mass spectrometry and identified GCA as a novel SASP-associated factor involved in skeletal aging \[[^38]\]. The expression of GCA in neutrophils and macrophages, particularly M1-like macrophages, was approximately 10- to 15-fold higher in aged rats than in younger rats. Further studies revealed that GCA partially inhibits the phosphorylation of its downstream signaling pathway, FAK-SRC-YAP, by binding to Plxnb2 receptors in BMSCs, thereby inhibiting bone formation and promoting adipogenesis \[[^38]\]. However, subsequent in vivo studies indicated that the improved osteoporotic phenotype observed in GCA knockout mice was abrogated upon the occurrence of loss of heterozygosity in Plxnb2 within BMSCs, implying that Plxnb2 is essential for mediating the skeletal effects of GCA \[[^38]\]. These observations support the role of the GCA–Plxnb2 axis as an important myeloid cell–BMSC communication pathway in age-related bone loss and provide a potential basis for SASP-targeted therapeutic strategies. Among the currently available studies, the GCA–Plxnb2 axis provides relatively direct evidence linking senescent or ageing-associated myeloid cells, including macrophages, to skeletal ageing. Nevertheless, the precise macrophage-specific contribution of GCA in human SOP requires further validation.

#### Releasing novel lipolysis factors: reticulocalbin-2

The phenomenon of aging is commonly associated with a progressive reduction in bone formation, immune function, and lipolysis. Research has demonstrated that physical activity can mitigate the decline in immune response and bone formation that occurs with advancing age \[[^93], [^94], [^95]\]. The intricate interplay among different cell types within the bone marrow microenvironment is frequently facilitated by soluble cytokines or growth factors \[[^96]\]. In a recent 2022 study, Peng et al. discovered a new factor involved in bone marrow adipose tissue (BMAT) lipolysis, known as reticulocalbin-2 (RCN2), within the bone marrow of aged mice \[[^97]\]. The researchers observed a notable decrease in RCN2 expression in bone BMDMs as a result of aging. To further elucidate this mechanism, they employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), proteomic analysis, and molecular biology methodologies \[[^97]\]. The investigation revealed that RCN2 interacts with the NRP2-ITGB1 receptor complex in bone marrow adipocytes, leading to the activation of the cAMP-PKA pathway and subsequent promotion of BMAT lipolysis and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) production. This cascade ultimately enhances the osteogenic potential of BMSCs \[[^97]\].

The main emphasis of this research has been on investigating the function of RCN2 in macrophages under conditions of exercise or mechanical stimulation. However, the identification of a BMSC osteogenic factor released by macrophages during the aging process constitutes a noteworthy and innovative advancement. This discovery highlights the critical role of RCN2 as a regulator in the adipose-bone formation/immune axis within the local environment.

#### Regulation of ageing-related signaling pathway

Lipoteichoic acid (LTA), a constituent of the cell wall of gram-positive bacteria, plays a role in the regulation of cellular senescence. Studies have demonstrated that LTA diminishes the secretion of SASP, improve the overall appearance of aged mice, reduces ROS production in fibroblasts, and mitigates skin photoaging \[[^98]\]. Furthermore, LTA has been observed to stimulate bone formation in a murine model of femoral defect \[[^99]\]. The phosphorylation status of mTOR is recognized as a critical determinant in the aging process, as it promotes cellular and tissue senescence through the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles, as well as depletion of stem cells \[[^100]\]. Studies have demonstrated that inhibiting mTOR signaling can effectively retard aging, mitigate SASP, and prolong lifespan \[[^101]\]. As mentioned above, the accumulation of senescent macrophages has been implicated in SOP through the secretion of SASP factors. Hence, these findings raise the question of whether LTA can inhibit macrophage senescence by regulating mTOR phosphorylation in macrophages.

Cheng et al. have conducted a thorough investigation and elucidation of this scientific query, revealing that LTA treatment can diminish macrophage aging in the bone marrow and ameliorate SOP \[[^66]\]. Moreover, they have mechanistically illustrated that LTA diminishes mTOR phosphorylation levels via the β-catenin/FOXO1/REDD1 pathway, thereby hindering macrophage senescence \[[^66]\]. Although they observed changes in mRNA expression of aging-related genes p16, p21, and p53 in senescent macrophages treated with LTA in cell experiments, they did not further measure the levels of SASP factors or verify the effects on osteogenic differentiation of other cells such as BMSCs, which could provide better evidence of the impact of this mechanism on SOP.

#### Mitochondrial transfer-mediated macrophage–BMSC communication: indirect evidence from inflammatory macrophage models

Intercellular communication is a critical component in the aging process, a multifaceted biological phenomenon influenced by various factors and mechanisms. While past research has predominantly examined electrical coupling, paracrine signaling, and gap junctions as primary modes of communication, recent attention has shifted towards a novel form of communication involving the exchange of organelles between cells. Notably, the transfer of functional mitochondria from donor to recipient cells has emerged as a significant determinant of the cell fate in this context \[[^102], [^103]\].

This phenomenon has gained particular interest in the field of OP due to its revelation of a unique pattern of cell-cell interactions within the bone marrow microenvironment. Specifically, mitochondrial transfer from macrophages to BMSCs has been implicated in the regulation of bone homeostasis and BMSC differentiation \[[^65]\]. However, in the context of OP, macrophages with altered phenotypic and metabolic profiles release oxygen-damaged mitochondria. The increased transference of mitochondria from LPS-treated proinflammatory M1 macrophages to BMSCs induces metabolic reprogramming in the latter, characterized by aberrant succinate accumulation, increased ROS levels, and Hif-1𝛼 activation. This leads to the upregulation of proinflammatory genes such as IL-1𝛽, subsequently impacting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs \[[^65]\]. In murine models, the administration of mitochondria derived from M1 macrophages resulted in osteoporotic symptoms, whereas the infusion of healthy mitochondria alleviated bone loss manifestations \[[^65]\].

Collectively, these observations indicate that macrophage-to-BMSC mitochondrial transfer may represent an important form of metabolic communication in the bone microenvironment. However, most evidence supporting this mechanism is derived from inflammatory or activated macrophage models. Although LPS-treated macrophages may exhibit aging-related features, including organelle damage, DNA damage, cell-cycle arrest, and apoptosis, the relevant studies did not systematically validate canonical markers of macrophage senescence. Therefore, mitochondrial transfer should currently be interpreted as an indirect mechanism with potential relevance to SOP, rather than as direct evidence of senescent macrophage-driven bone loss.

#### Exosome-mediated communication by polarized macrophages: implications for senescence-related studies

Exosomes secreted by macrophages are integral to intercellular communication through the transfer of bioactive cargoes such as mRNAs, miRNAs, and proteins to recipient cells \[[^104], [^105]\]. Prior research has demonstrated that the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype facilitates the differentiation of pre-osteoblasts and improves bone mineralization \[[^106]\]. Additionally, exosomes derived from M2 macrophages have been found to promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs \[[^107]\]. The high expression of miR-486-5p in exosomes derived from M2 macrophages promotes the osteogenic potential of BMSCs through the miR-486-5p/SMAD2/TGF-β signaling pathway \[[^108]\]. In contrast, exosomes derived from M1 macrophages exacerbate bone loss by upregulating miR-98 expression in MC3T3-E1 cells, targeting DUSP1, and activating the JNK signaling pathway \[[^109]\]. Considering the distinct polarization states of M1 and M2 macrophages, the manipulation of macrophage phenotypes via pharmacological interventions may present a promising therapeutic approach for OP management.

These studies support the role of macrophage-derived exosomes in osteogenic regulation, but they mainly involve M1/M2-polarized rather than bona fide senescent macrophages. Whether senescent macrophage-derived exosomes directly affect BMSC fate in SOP remains unclear. Recent evidence shows that aged bone marrow monocytes/macrophages can spread paracrine senescence via extracellular vesicles, suggesting exosome-mediated communication as a key mechanism influencing tissue ageing and the bone marrow microenvironment \[[^110]\].

Overall, these mechanisms discussed above differ in evidence strength and mode of action. SASP-related pathways, especially the GCA–Plxnb2 axis, provide relatively direct ageing-related evidence, whereas mitochondrial transfer and exosome-mediated communication are mainly indirect, based on inflammatory or polarized macrophage models. Despite these differences, they converge on common outcomes, including inflammation, impaired osteogenesis, enhanced bone resorption, and bone loss. Their relative contributions to human SOP remain to be clarified.

#### Therapeutic strategies targeting macrophage senescence or senescence-associated dysfunction in SOP

Current therapeutic strategies involving macrophage senescence in SOP remain largely exploratory, and most are not specifically designed to target bona fide senescent macrophages. Some approaches function as general senolytics, whereas others modulate oxidative stress, autophagy, inflammation, macrophage polarization, or broader osteoimmune remodeling. Accordingly, these strategies are discussed according to their mechanistic relevance to macrophage senescence or senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction, rather than being presented as macrophage senescence-specific therapies (Table [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Tab2)).

#### Modulating senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction

Research has shown that resveratrol has the ability to induce autophagy in senescent macrophages, improve cell survival and proliferation, reduce intracellular ROS levels, and encourage polarization towards a healing pro-M2 phenotype, ultimately facilitating osteogenesis and angiogenesis \[[^111]\]. In further exploration of resveratrol’s potential, Peng et al. have developed a new nanoplatform known as RSV@DTPF, which specifically targets macrophage folate receptors using folate groups. Upon detection of elevated intracellular ROS levels, RSV@DTPF is designed to efficiently release resveratrol \[[^112]\] (Fig. [5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12979-026-00586-4#Fig5)). This strategy seeks to achieve equilibrium between bone resorption and formation through the modulation of the bone immune microenvironment, thereby facilitating bone regeneration and providing a potential therapeutic approach for bone metabolic disorders such as SOP.

#### Autophagy activation in senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction

As a constituent of traditional Chinese medicine, ICA demonstrated notable benefits in the process of bone regeneration \[[^57]\]. The results indicate that ICA can attenuate senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction through the activation of autophagy, while also restoring the osteogenic capacity of senescent BMSCs, ultimately mitigating bone mass reduction in osteoporotic mice \[[^57]\]. This evidence supports the development of autophagy-targeted bioactive compounds or biomaterials as potential therapeutic strategies for OP and other age-related disorders.

#### Targeting specific factors of SASP

Several studies have targeted specific molecules in SASP factors, such as pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. For instance, IL-6 is associated with increased bone resorption and decreased bone formation, while overexpression of TNF-α and IL-1β is linked to OP development \[[^113], [^114]\]. IL-6 antagonists and TNF-α inhibitors have demonstrated potential in improving bone mineral density (BMD) in inflammatory-associated diseases \[[^115], [^116]\]. However, further research and validation are needed to ascertain the current clinical viability of these medications in impeding the advancement of OP. Utilizing an enhanced selective lymphocyte antibody methodology \[[^117]\], the research team at Xiang Hang Luo produced a series of anti-GCA monoclonal antibodies in mice \[[^38]\]. Through rigorous screening, they successfully identified a neutralizing antibody named GCA-NAb that hinders the interaction between GCA derived from senescent or ageing-associated myeloid cells and its functional receptor Plxnb2 in BMSCs. This antibody effectively mitigated the inhibitory effects of phosphorylation on FAK (Tyr925), SRC (Tyr416), and TAZ (Ser89), consequently enhancing bone health in elderly mice \[[^38]\]. This therapeutic approach offers new perspectives on potential targets for the advancement of innovative treatments for SOP.

#### General senolytic and senescence-modulating strategies

ABT263, a known inhibitor of the anti-apoptotic proteins BCL-2 and BCL-xL, has been commonly utilized for the eradication of senescent cells \[[^118]\]. A recent study by Cai et al. in 2020 introduced a novel prodrug, SSK1, that is selectively activated by the enzyme SA-β-gal. The researchers illustrated the effectiveness of this prodrug in selectively targeting senescent cells, mitigating inflammation, and enhancing functional outcomes \[[^119]\]. Moreover, the upregulation of MDM2 has demonstrated encouraging outcomes in diminishing p53 levels in senescent cells and facilitating osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs \[[^120], [^121]\]. These findings suggest that MDM2 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for SOP by facilitating the degradation of p53. However, it is crucial to recognize that although ABT263, SSK1, or MDM2 have been employed for the elimination of senescent cells, they exhibit limited specificity in targeting senescent macrophages. Considering the pivotal role of macrophages in the immune system and their importance in the aging process, further research is warranted to explore customized therapeutic strategies for addressing macrophage senescence.

#### Modulating inflammatory macrophage phenotypes

Treatment of BMDMs from aged mice with Maresin 1 (MaR1) led to a mitigation of the pro-inflammatory state and a promotion of the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Furthermore, the secretions of MaR1-treated macrophages were found to promote osteogenesis and enhance the differentiation of osteoblasts from BMSCs \[[^122]\]. Nevertheless, additional validation is necessary to confirm the effectiveness of MaR1 in regulating and preserving the equilibrium of the bone microenvironment in SOP. Recent research conducted by Li et al. has demonstrated that the electrospun naringin-loaded microspheres/sucrose acetate isobutyrate (Ng-m-SAIB) exhibit favorable biocompatibility and have the ability to induce macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, thus creating a microenvironment that supports bone formation \[[^123]\]. This biomaterial shows potential for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects through its osteoimmunomodulatory properties.

#### Other senescence-associated or macrophage-related targets

In the preceding analysis of mechanisms, it was observed that the decrease in activity of the β-catenin/FOXO1/REDD1 signaling pathway in senescent or senescence-like macrophages may play a significant role in the aging process of macrophage and subsequent bone degradation. Studies have demonstrated that LTA can effectively restore the β-catenin/FOXO1 signaling pathway, thereby hindering macrophage senescence and age-related OP, a condition that may be exacerbated by the suppression of β-catenin \[[^66]\].

Additionally, the utilization of novel small molecule inhibitors, such as JQ-1 or I-BET762, has been shown to effectively inhibit BRD4 in senescent macrophages, leading to a reduction in the expression of autocrine and paracrine SASP. This inhibition ultimately prevents cell senescence and LPS-induced lipid accumulation in macrophages \[[^124]\]. Accordingly, the exploration and development of novel inhibitors targeting BRD4 may present a promising therapeutic approach for mitigating macrophage senescence.

#### Summary and prospect

Macrophages are widely distributed across diverse tissues and organs and play essential roles in immune surveillance, tissue homeostasis, and repair. During aging, macrophages undergo progressive phenotypic and functional alterations, including senescence-associated or senescence-like changes.This review initially presents the origin, distribution and categorization of macrophages, followed by a discussion of the biological characteristics of macrophage senescence, including alterations in senescence markers, SASP profiles, epigenetic regulation, and telomere dynamics. We then outline the functional changes associated with macrophage senescence or senescence-like dysfunction, particularly those related to oxidative stress, autophagy, metabolic remodeling, and other ageing-related processes.

SOP is a degenerative systemic bone disease characterized by decreased bone mass, microstructural imbalance, and increased bone fragility. With the growing trend of increased life expectancy, SOP is increasingly being recognized as a significant health concern due to its association with severe pain, heightened risk of fractures, and potential fatality. Macrophages are pivotal in the maintenance of bone homeostasis and are crucial for upholding normal bone metabolic equilibrium. Nevertheless, the precise contribution of macrophage senescence to SOP has only recently begun to be clarified. In this review, we consolidate current evidence on the mechanisms through which macrophage senescence or senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction may contribute to SOP, including autophagy dysregulation, SASP-related factors, lipolytic factors, ageing-related signaling pathways, mitochondrial transfer, and exosome-mediated communication. We also summarize therapeutic strategies related to macrophage senescence or senescence-associated dysfunction in SOP, while emphasizing that many of these mechanisms and interventions are supported by different levels of evidence.

Despite growing interest in macrophage senescence in SOP, several limitations remain. Direct human evidence is limited, with most data derived from animal models or in vitro systems. Different macrophage states—aged, inflammatory, polarized, and stress-induced—are related but not equivalent and should be interpreted cautiously. Common senescence markers lack specificity when used alone, requiring combined validation. Future investigations should also prioritize spatially resolved analyses of human osteoporotic bone marrow, macrophage-specific interventions, and advanced techniques such as single-cell analysis and lineage tracing to clarify whether senescent macrophages drive or merely accompany SOP progression and to better define their role in bone ageing and osteoporosis.
