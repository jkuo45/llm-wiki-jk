## Summary

Urolithin A (UA, Mitopure), a postbiotic that promotes mitophagy and mitochondrial function following oral administration, was evaluated for its effects on skin health in human skin models and when applied topically to healthy subjects in randomized clinical studies. UA tested in 2D and 3D reconstructed human skin and *ex vivo* human skin explants showed enhanced mitochondrial respiration, activated mitophagy markers, reduced inflammatory mediators and senescence-associated markers, and increased proteins involved in extracellular matrix structure and epidermal differentiation. Safety was confirmed in a human repeat-insult patch test. In randomized, placebo-controlled clinical studies, UA reduced periorbital wrinkle depth after 8 weeks and attenuated UVB-induced erythema. Skin transcriptomics supported increased extracellular matrix and epidermal development programs together with decreased inflammatory and senescence-associated signaling. These findings indicate that topical UA engages cellular pathways associated with skin resilience to promote skin health and longevity.

## Introduction

Skin is the body’s largest organ and serves vital functions as a barrier against injuries, UV light, and pathogens. Chronological aging brings changes in skin appearance and a decline in its protective functions. This is further accentuated following exposure to UV radiation by a process called photoaging. The most common visual signs of aging skin are the appearance of wrinkles, skin thinning, hyperpigmentation, sagginess, and dryness. This derives from a combination of biological changes, including a decline in the quantity and quality of structural components of the skin, such as collagen and elastin, as well as dysfunctions in cellular bioenergetics. Indeed, most of the energy to support skin cell health is derived from mitochondria, making mitochondrial dysfunction a key hallmark of skin aging. The aging process impacts immune cells in the skin. This reduces the ability of the skin to cope with excessive inflammation and significantly contributes to the emergence of photoaging. Finally, cellular senescence is emerging as a key additional hallmark that affects skin health as we age, which can be counteracted by removal of the senescent cells (i.e., senolytic effect) or blunting of their detrimental secretome (i.e., senomorphic effect).

Current “anti-aging” skincare strategies remain largely sign-corrective and rely on a set of well-established actives, such as retinoids (retinol, retinaldehyde, and tretinoin), alpha-hydroxy acids (glycolic and lactic acids), vitamin C, niacinamide, peptides, and hyaluronic acid (HA). While these ingredients can visibly improve wrinkles, uneven tone, and texture, they mostly address downstream manifestations of aging rather than its underlying biological drivers. In addition, some active formulations and combinations can trigger irritation (erythema, stinging, dryness, and peeling), limiting tolerability and long-term use, particularly in photoaged or sensitive skin. New innovative bioactives are needed to improve skin health and longevity, ideally with a dual benefit profile, impacting both intrinsic aging and extrinsic aging. Intrinsic aging involves the natural, genetic, and biological processes (BPs) that contribute to skin health decline over time. Extrinsic aging of the skin is due to external factors such as UV exposure, pollution, and lifestyle choices. These new bioactives should work without causing the irritations often observed. Urolithin A (UA) is a promising molecule to explore for its impact on skin longevity, considering its origins as a postbiotic of the gut microbiome that has been shown to both improve mitochondrial health and reduce age-associated inflammation, i.e., inflammaging, when administered orally. UA increases mitophagy, the process of recycling faulty mitochondria, and enhances biomarkers associated with improved mitochondrial function in several models of aging and age-associated conditions. In humans, oral supplementation of UA was shown to be safe, improve mitochondrial function, and increase muscle strength, endurance, and immune decline in clinical studies in middle-aged and elderly subjects. Topical application of UA represents a compelling approach to support skin health and extend skin longevity through its combined effect on multiple hallmarks of cellular aging.

We hypothesized that topical application of UA, similar to its well-characterized impact via oral administration, would activate mitophagy and dampen inflammaging in skin cells and *ex vivo* models, and impact both visible and molecular signs of aging in controlled settings of human clinical trials. The present studies aimed to assess the impact of UA on skin cells utilizing both 2D and 3D human skin cell models, evaluate human skin safety upon topical application, and determine the efficacy of topically applied UA through clinical assessments and skin biopsies focused on skin aging and longevity pathways. Our multifaceted approach allowed us to analyze the benefits of UA application and identify potential mechanisms contributing to its observed effects on skin health.

The studies are registered in clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05079607, NCT05300984, and NCT05300542.

## Results

### UA beneficial impact on human keratinocytes and reconstituted human epidermis

We assessed the impact of UA on the epidermis by investigating several hallmarks of skin health in both normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEK) and 3D skin models. Mitochondrial function is a key hallmark of skin health, known to be improved by UA in various cells and tissues. Mitochondrial respiration was used as a readout to determine the most effective dose of UA. NHEK cells were treated with UA at doses ranging from 0.5 μM to 2.5 μM for 24 h. The dose response analysis indicated that UA increases both basal and maximal mitochondrial respiration with the most robust and significant effects at the 1 μM dose (A). The beneficial effects of UA on mitochondria were further confirmed in a complex full-thickness skin model. To mimic intrinsic skin aging, models were reconstructed using both fibroblasts and keratinocytes derived from an aged donor. The study compared 1 μM UA treatment during the skin reconstruction phase. Immunofluorescence quantification showed a remarkable increase in the mitochondrial content biomarker, TOMM20, in the epidermal layer. This indicates a significant increase in mitochondrial biogenesis by UA in the epidermis. UA also increased the gene expression of mitophagy markers in both 2D NHEK cells following systemic UA treatment (A) and in reconstructed human epidermal model, with topical application of a cream containing 1% UA (C). UA’s anti-inflammatory effects—already reported across cells and species—were confirmed in keratinocytes, as shown by the significant decrease in the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin 6 (IL-6) in NHEK (D). Finally, we observed that UA counteracts a hallmark of cellular senescence by markedly reducing p16 protein abundance in the full-thickness skin model (E).

Indeed, UA exposure significantly improved both the thickness and overall quality of the reconstructed skin, promoting a well-differentiated epidermis while preserving structural integrity (B). These results indicate that UA has a beneficial impact on the epidermis, from cellular signatures to functional features of skin health.

### UA beneficial impact on human fibroblasts and models of human dermis

UA’s impact on dermal health was assessed using both the human cell lines and 3D models described previously. Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were first used to look at UA’s impact on mitochondrial respiration. The impact of UA on HDFs from healthy young (23 years) and old (69 years) donors was investigated. As expected, HDFs from the old donor exhibited impaired basal and maximal cellular respiration compared to a younger donor. UA treatment was able to rescue both basal and maximal mitochondrial respiration of old fibroblast cells (A). UA treatment for 24 h also increased the levels of phospho-ubiquitin—a marker that reflects the activation of the PINK1-Parkin-dependent mitophagy pathway—to similar extent to a 30 min exposure to carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), used as positive control (B). Consistent with the epidermis data, UA reduced IL-6 secretion in HDF (A). An established model of chronic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) exposure was employed to study senescence. UA treatment in this model reduced the expression of CXCL1 (C). This gene encodes a chemokine that is part of the senescence associated secretory profile (SASP). The downregulation of SASP-related chemokines supports a senomorphic activity of UA, whereby it attenuates the detrimental secretory profile of senescent cells without eliminating them. On the other hand, UA did not modulate the expression of p21, a marker associated to a senolytic effect (C).

The functional benefits of UA on dermis were assessed by investigating its impact on cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization. During skin aging, a relaxation of the dermal ECM is observed, accompanied by a decrease in fibroblast-matrix interactions. This phenomenon is associated with a disorganization of focal adhesions, macromolecular membrane complexes composed notably of integrins, paxillin, and vinculin. These focal adhesions connect the extracellular environment to the intracellular cytoskeleton and mediate mechanical and biochemical signal transduction. Alterations in the ECM organization contribute to a reduced regenerative capacity as well as fibroblast senescence and degradation of dermal properties.

Consistent with prior work, HDF from an old donor exhibited a striking decrease in focal adhesions compared to a young donor (D). UA treatment significantly rescued age-associated focal adhesion defects as indicated quantitatively by a measuring vinculin focal adhesion particle number (E) and the ratio of diffused over aggregated vinculin (F). UA also rescued the disassembly of the actin polymer, another cytoskeleton disorganization associated with aging (G and 2H). Complementing these effects on the ECM, UA treatment reduced the expression of matrix metalloproteinase I (MMP1) (B), which encodes for a key enzyme leading to degradation of collagen, a major skin ECM component. These intracellular structure improvements provide evidence that UA stimulates skin cells to maintain and repair their ECM. This beneficial effect of UA on ECM was further studied by employing a full-thickness skin model and analyzing ECM markers in the dermis by immunohistochemistry. UA treatment increased the dermal protein content of collagen III (A), HA (B), and elastin (C), major ECM components and contributors to skin firmness and hydration, both known to decline with skin aging. UA also increased the protein abundance of collagen VII (D), which forms anchoring fibrils that secure adhesion between the basement membrane and the underlying dermis at the dermal-epidermal junction, preserving skin integrity and resistance. Together, these findings demonstrate that UA robustly enhances dermal ECM quality, supporting improved skin structure and resilience.

### Topical application of UA is safe and does not induce skin irritation or sensitization

In the human repeat insult patch test (HRIPT) skin safety study, no adverse reactions (ARs) were reported during the study period. There were also no skin reactions observed by any of the subjects to any of the test articles during the sensitization challenge phase (days 38 and 40 of HRIPT). These results indicate that UA-containing topical products have a low potential for causing skin irritation and sensitization. Similarly, in the “aging efficacy study”, no ARs linked to the study product use were reported by any subject ().

### Subject demographics in trials with topical UA

Supported by positive *in vitro* and *ex vivo* results as well as favorable human skin tolerability, UA was then assessed for its impact on skin aging, in a randomized, placebo-controlled trial (aging efficacy study). A total of 55 participants were screened. Of them, *n* = 48 middle aged women subjects with mean age of 58.6 ± 6.6 years were randomized () into two study arms with a split-face/arm study design with a lower-dose active group compared to placebo and a second higher-dose active group compared to placebo. All participants completed the trial and there were no dropouts. Ethnicity of all participants was Caucasian.

### Application of a 1% UA cream reduces visible features and molecular biomarkers of skin aging

In aging efficacy study facial wrinkle reduction was measured after 2 and 8 week of test treatment application and gene expression changes were assessed from arm biopsies after 8-week treatment (A). Reduced wrinkle depth was the most impacted visible benefit following 1% UA treatment as compared to placebo, with the effects already visible at 2-week () of continuous daily topical application. Wrinkle depth showed further significant reduction at 8-week of application with the 1% UA treatment side compared to the placebo-treated side (B and 5C; *p* = 0.04). Application of 0.5% UA, however, did not show any significant effect compared to the placebo at the time points evaluated (data not shown). The biological pathways impacted by topical UA following an application period of 8-week were studied by performing RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) transcriptomics of forearm skin biopsies from the efficacy aging study. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was applied to identify significant pathways changed by UA, comparing sample results at the study end to baseline and normalizing over the vehicle. Analysis was paired, as each subject was exposed to both 1% UA and placebo on opposite forearms. “collagen fibril organization” was the pathway most significantly upregulated by UA (D). The findings of a publicly available clinical study focused on skin aging revealed a significant association between the collagen fibril organization and skin age. UA topical application improved collagen fibril organization, while the same pathway was downregulated as the skin aged, as indicated in E. The specific skin health genes enriched by UA in the collagen fibril organization pathway were further assessed. This gene set includes collagen genes encoding for fibrillar type I and III collagen (F), known to decline in skin with chronological aging, photoaging and smoking. This translates in humans into the collagen-inducing properties of UA observed in 3D reconstructed skin (). Among other genes involved in skin homeostasis, UA upregulated *EMILIN-1*, regulating 3D structure and elasticity, and *AEBP*, encoding for a collagen-interacting protein required for optimal tissue repair (F). UA application also significantly upregulated other epidermis specific gene sets including “Epidermis development”. Among downregulated pathways, UA suppressed several gene sets associated with inflammation responses, such as “response to chemokines”, “positive regulation of cytokine production” and “leukocyte mediated immunity” (D). Additionally, UA significantly downregulated a manually curated senescence gene set built on the Mayo Clinic SenMayo gene set (SenMayo, D). A further look into the downregulated core genes using gene concept analysis highlighted an interconnected impact of UA on inflammation and senescence genes, by acting on several metalloproteinases (MMPs), chemokines (CCLs and CXCLs) and ILs, among the others (G). These data are consistent with the senomorphic effect of UA shown in HDF (C).

### Topical UA 1% induces mitophagy, reduces inflammation, and dampens erythema in photodamaged skin

The reconstructed human epidermis (RHE) model was employed to investigate potential UA mitigation of the inflammatory response triggered by UV irradiation. RHE samples were topically treated with either a basal formulation or a formulation containing 1% UA. Following a 24-h incubation period, the RHE tissues were exposed to UVB irradiation and then subjected to an additional 24-h treatment with the same topical formulation. A subset of RHE samples remained untreated and non-irradiated to serve as negative controls. Prostaglandin G2 (PGE2) concentration in the culture supernatant was quantified as a biomarker of inflammation. As expected, irradiated RHE exhibited a significant increase in PGE2 release compared to non-irradiated tissues (*p* = 0.0002; A). Application of UA at 1% to irradiated RHE resulted in a significant reduction in PGE2 levels, indicating a potent anti-inflammatory effect of UA (*p* = 0.0257; A). Human skin explants were used to analyze additional biomarkers modulated by UA and linked to stress-induced skin defects. Skin morphology was assessed on 5 μm cryosections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), which revealed preserved tissue architecture across conditions, with no detectable morphological alterations in UA-treated explants compared with controls (A), demonstrating local tolerability. Oxidative damage to nuclear proteins was assessed by quantifying carbonylated proteins within a histone-enriched fraction. The PM10 + UV-A challenge induced a significant increase in the carbonyl score compared with control (B). UA treatment produced a statistically significant attenuation of this stress-induced histone carbonylation.

Overall, the combined *ex vivo*, epidermal model, and human clinical data demonstrate that topical UA confers broad benefits by reducing inflammation, preserving mitochondrial quality control (QC), and mitigating oxidative damage in human skin.

## Discussion

Implementing protective measures against skin aging is essential for enhancing skin longevity. Extensive cell-based, *ex vivo* analysis was employed to delineate molecular pathways impacted with UA on different human skin cell types and on skin tissue models. This work shows the first proof that UA topical application is efficient in combating multiple biological hallmarks of aging in human skin.

Mechanistic analyses in primary human cells, 3D human skin models, and *ex vivo* skin explants revealed that exposure to UA positively impacts biomarkers associated with enhanced mitochondrial health and mitophagy. UA was already shown to support cellular health by activating mitophagy in different tissues, including skeletal muscle, cartilage cells, and in the CNS. Mitophagy is crucial to remove defective mitochondria that accumulate with aging. In turn, it stimulates the regeneration of new mitochondria to produce enough energy to preserve cellular health. Mitochondria and cell respiration had been described as the principal way of ATP generation in these mechanisms. Therefore, mitophagy activation can contribute to support energy demanding processes such as epidermal differentiation and collagen production. In this work, UA stimulated mitophagy protein markers in both the skin cells and in *ex vivo* skin explants exposed to a pollution-like stress. UA also increased mitochondrial content in epidermis models, as expected consequence of its mitophagy activation.

The benefits on mitochondrial quality and quantity improvements by UA were associated with significant increase in both epidermal markers of proliferation/differentiation and collagen protein levels in the dermis, in human cell models. Further studies are needed to understand the precise link between mitophagy and collagen production. Nevertheless, these data show that mitophagy enhancement by UA helps to reactivate and maintain optimal cellular function in the skin cells to cope against natural aging and environmental stressors.

Beyond UA’s benefits against mitochondrial dysfunction, combined evidence from cell culture models and human skin biopsies showed that UA modulates additional hallmarks of skin aging, specifically by reducing inflammation and attenuating markers of cellular senescence. This latter effect is consistent with a senomorphic activity, whereby UA suppresses the detrimental SASP secretion of senescent cells, thereby preventing the propagation of senescence signals to neighboring healthy cells.

Collectively, the robust mechanistic evidence obtained across cell culture systems, 3D skin models, and human skin explants justified advancing UA into clinical studies to determine whether these biological benefits translate into measurable improvements in human skin. Safety assessments demonstrated that UA can be applied topically to human skin without significant adverse effects in study participants. These comprehensive safety evaluations underscore the excellent tolerability of UA, even for sensitive skin, reinforcing its suitability for widespread use in skincare formulations without posing risks of irritation or sensitization.

The efficacy of UA when translating findings into humans for counteracting visible signs of skin aging was evident in randomized, placebo-controlled trials. Skin wrinkles are clinical signs of aging, particularly noticeable on facial skin. They originate from a combination of factors, including the breakdown of collagen and elastin fibers within the skin’s microstructure, repeated facial expressions, and environmental damage such as UV exposure. This degradation disrupts the skin’s architecture, leading to the formation of wrinkles. Beyond their impact on appearance, wrinkles contribute to the overall aging process associated with cellular stress and a pro-inflammatory state in skin cells. This inflammation can further deteriorate skin microstructure and accelerate aging, potentially increasing the risk of skin-related conditions. Preventing wrinkles is not only about maintaining a youthful look but, importantly, about preserving the integrity and health of the skin’s architecture. Topical application of UA impacted skin wrinkles and fine lines, particularly in the crow’s feet area, which were significantly diminished following an 8-week daily regimen.

At the tissue level, ECM changes have been defined as a key hallmark of skin longevity. Collagens are crucial for providing structural support within the skin and ensures the skin’s firmness, elasticity, hydration, as well as supporting the processes of cell regeneration and wound healing. As the body ages, it loses its ability to produce collagen by about 1% per year and this loss increases up to 2% per year post-menopause for women. Exposure to UV increases the breakdown of collagen fibers. Topical application of UA upregulated the expression levels of specific collagen genes, as evidenced by unbiased skin biopsy RNA-seq. Detailed analysis revealed that the topical application of UA led to an increase in the expression of the spectrum of collagen proteins, notably including skin’s most abundant collagen I and III species. These are typically downregulated during natural aging, and UA effectively counteracts this process at the gene level by the maintenance of a youthful skin ECM. The RNA-seq data indicate an impact of UA also at the level of the epidermis, with a significant upregulation of genes primarily involved in differentiation and production of proteins key for improved barrier function. We also confirmed *in vivo* that UA significantly reduces the expression of genes associated with inflammation and cellular senescence, in line with the results from *in vitro* models supporting UA’s senomorphic properties. Overall, these results highlight the multi-faceted protective role of UA in human skin, mediated by its action against several hallmarks of aging, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, disabled macroautophagy, inflammation, and cellular senescence.

In addition to its protective effects against intrinsic cellular aging, UA demonstrated efficacy in mitigating biological changes in the epidermis and dermis that are a consequence of photoaging. Excessive UVA- and UVB- radiation exposure is known to accelerate skin aging through cellular stress. UVA is actually highly relevant to mitochondrial dysfunction because it penetrates deeper into the skin and interacts strongly with endogenous chromophores, including mitochondrial components. UA showed promise in protecting against UVB-induced photoaging in 2D HDFs, through the activation of mitophagy. In the current report, for the first time in humans, topically applied UA was demonstrated to provide a strong protective benefit to both UVA and UVB-exposed skin. This is particularly significant given the limitations of current remedies for post-UV/sunburn exposure and also to protect skin against environmental stressors like UVA and particulate matter. Specific doses of active UA were identified to significantly mitigate UVB-induced erythema formation. The mechanistic benefits of UA were explored by employing a 3D human epidermis model, and a notable reduction in the production of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a pro-inflammatory molecule induced by UV irradiation was observed. These data are in line with the anti-inflammatory profile of UA seen by RNA-seq in the “photo-damage study.” UA also protected against UVA and PM10 exposure that compromised mitophagy which was rescued via UA treatment.

Current post-sunburn treatments often involve the application of aloe vera, corticosteroids, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) to alleviate pain and inflammation. These treatments do not prevent cellular damage caused by UV exposure but merely provide temporary relief. In contrast, these findings reveal that UA not only reduces inflammation but also protects the skin at a molecular level, reducing the formation of sunburn-related damage before it occurs. This represents a breakthrough in skin care, as UA could potentially shift the paradigm from reactive to proactive treatment, offering a scientifically validated method to protect and heal the skin.

Overall, these findings indicate that UA offers a safe profile and a comprehensive protection and rejuvenation of the skin by acting on various key hallmarks of skin aging, especially those linked to collagen degradation, mitochondrial health decline, senescence, and chronic inflammation. The results of these clinical studies highlight the potential of UA as a leading candidate in dermatological applications, offering a holistic approach to maintaining and improving skin health and longevity.

### Limitations of the study

One of the limitations of the current study is the absence of *ex vivo* skin penetration data with UA. However, the mechanistic effects observed in this study in both different skin cell types and *ex vivo* human skin explant models provide clues that the impact of UA is inherently penetration-dependent. Also, clinical data from randomized clinical trials with topical UA supplementation demonstrated statistically significant improvements in skin aging and inflammation parameters, suggesting that UA reaches skin tissue at biologically active concentrations. Taken together, while we acknowledge that formal quantification of UA in skin tissue *ex vivo*, with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) would add dermato-pharmacokinetic precision and represents a valuable direction for future work, the absence of such data does not create an inferential gap in the mechanistic or translational conclusions presented in this study. Additional mechanistic work in the future would enrich the understanding of UA’s further impact on mitochondrial function, by investigating cellular readouts such as mitochondrial morphology, mitochondrial membrane potential capacity and electron transport chain activity. Further proteomics studies in humans would also allow to identify the specific biomarkers related to mitophagy which are mainly modulated by post-transcriptional mechanisms and not represented by RNA-based analyses. Future longer-duration clinical trials with post-treatment follow-up in clinical settings would also help establish clinical benefit in dermatological regimens as topical UA formulations move toward broader dermatological and cosmetic use.

#### Hydrogen peroxide fibroblast senescence model

Senescence induction is based on the previously published methods. In particular, human dermal fibroblasts (HDF) were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured in DMEM GlutaMAX containing 1 g/L D-glucose (Gibco, ref. 21885108), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; PanBiotech, ref. P30-3033), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Biowest), and 1% HEPES (Biowest). Cells were pre-treated for 24 hours with either DMSO or urolithin A (UA) at the indicated doses. Following pre-treatment, the medium was replaced and cells were exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H _2_ O _2_) at a final concentration of 200 μM for 2 h. Cells were then washed once with fresh culture medium and incubated in medium containing either DMSO or UA. After 48 h, treatments were removed and cells were exposed to a second H _2_ O _2_ treatment (200 μM), followed by washing. Cells were subsequently incubated for an additional 72 hours in medium supplemented with either DMSO or UA.

#### Western blot

Fibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 70/80% confluence before protein extraction. For lysis, culture medium was removed and cells were rinsed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were then lysed directly in the wells with 150ul of ice-cold RIPA buffer per well 50mM Tris-HCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 150mM NaCl, 2mM EDTA), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Cells were scraped thoroughly to collect lysates, which were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and incubated on ice for 10–15 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 x g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatants were collected into fresh pre-chilled tubes and kept on ice, and the pellets were discarded. Protein concentration was determined using the DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad, Cat. No. 5000112) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Samples were adjusted to equal protein concentration by dilution in RIPA buffer. Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) containing dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to each sample and samples were mixed and heated (e.g. 10 minutes at 70°C) before loading. A total of 10ug of protein per lane was loaded onto 15% TGX Stain-Free precast polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad, Cat. No. 4568086) and proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto suitable membranes using the Trans-Blot transfer system (Bio-Rad, Cat. No. 1704156), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Membranes were subsequently processed for immunoblotting with Phospho-ubiquitin (Milipore, ref. ABS1513-I), VDAC (Abcam, ref. ab14734) primary at both 1:1000 dilution and secondary antibodies at 1:5000 dilution (Goat anti-rabbit, Azure, Ref. AC2114, Goat anti-mouse Azure, Ref. AC2115) both diluted in 5% BSA. Membranes were developed after 2 minutes incubation in standard chemiluminescent reagent (Biorad, ECL clarity Ref. 170-5060). Analysis were performed with ImageLab software and data were normalized over total proteins.

#### Vinculin focal adhesions and actin depolymerization

Fibroblasts derived from a young donor (26 years old) and an older donor (69 years old) were analyzed for actin disorganization and focal adhesions using the Cytoskeleton kit (Sigma, ref. FAK-100). Cells were seeded on Labtek slides (ref. 154534) and treated for 24 hours with either DMSO or Urolithin A at the indicated doses. Following treatment, cells were fixed and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS for 1–5 minutes. After several washes, a blocking solution was applied for 30 minutes, followed by incubation with the primary antibody (anti-vinculin, 1:500 in blocking solution) for 1 hour. Cells were then washed three times and incubated with the secondary antibody and Phalloidine at 1:1000 for 1 hour, followed by three additional washes (5–10 minutes each) with 1× wash buffer. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:5000) for 1–5 minutes at room temperature, and cells were washed three times (5–10 minutes each) with 1× wash buffer. Slides were mounted with Fluoromount, and image analysis was conducted using ImageJ Fiji. Thresholding was applied, and particles were selected based on a size range of ≥30 and circularity values between 0.5 and 1.

#### *Ex vivo* human skin explant model for UA treatment and urban dust/UV-A challenge

Human skin explants were collected, with written informed consent, from abdominal surgery of a 34-year-old Caucasian female donor (phototype II/III; Ref. SKIN0S3M244). Immediately after excision, explants were maintained *ex vivo* on metal grids in standard 12-well plates, in contact with OxiProteomics® proprietary culture medium, at 37 °C in humidified air containing 5% CO2; the medium was renewed every 24 h. Explants were distributed into five experimental groups (*n* = 3 per group). UA was first solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subsequently diluted in ultrapure water to the target concentration. Explants were treated for 24 h topically with 1% (w/v) UA at 30 μL/cm2, using 1% DMSO as vehicle control. After 24 h, explant surfaces were washed, and the culture medium was replaced with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). Explants were then topically exposed to urban dust (PM10-like heavy metals; Ref. ERM-CZ120, certified European Reference Material; 0.375 μg/cm2) for 30 min, followed by UV-A irradiation (LED source, peak emission at 365 nm; 6 J/cm2; 40 min). Control explants received no treatment or exposure except for culture medium renewal. At 3 hours and 24 hours post-challenge, explants were collected, embedded in OCT, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until analysis.

Cryosections (5 μm thick) were prepared from OCT-embedded explants using a cryostat (Leica) and subsequently fixed using an OxiProteomics® fixative solution. For morphological assessment, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H/E). For immunofluorescence, non-specific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). Sections were then incubated with the appropriate primary antibody diluted in 3% BSA/PBS. After PBS washes to remove unbound primary antibody, sections were incubated for 1 hour with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (PINK1, Abcam, ab216144, Anti-Rabbit Alexafluor 647, Invitrogen A21244). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). Excess antibodies and DAPI were removed by successive PBS washes. Brightfield (RGB) and fluorescence images were acquired using an epifluorescence microscope (EVOS M5000 Imaging System; 40× objective), keeping acquisition settings (exposure time, resolution) constant within each image series. Raw 16-bit TIFF files capturing the full fluorescence dynamic range were analyzed with ImageJ [D Schneider et al., 2012]. Biomarker signal was quantified as the integrated fluorescence intensity above a fixed threshold, normalized to the analyzed tissue area.

#### Nuclear protein extraction and in-gel fluorescence quantification of carbonylated histone-enriched proteins

At the time of analysis, samples (*n* = 3 per condition) were processed independently. Proteins were extracted using the OxiProteomics® procedure optimized for skin explants and histone enrichment, based on sequential membrane lysis with buffers of increasing detergent strength, followed by nuclear protein extraction according to the OxiProteomics® protocol Total protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. The histone-enriched, carbonylated protein fraction was labelled with a carbonyl-specific fluorescent probe. Labeled proteins were separated on 4–20% gradient SDS-PAGE gels. After electrophoresis, gels were fixed and carbonylated proteins were detected by fluorescence scanning at 647 nm. Total proteins were subsequently stained in-gel with SyproRubyTM (Life Technologies, USA) and imaged by fluorescence at 595 nm. Gel images were acquired using the iBright system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and densitometric analyses were performed with ImageJ (NIH). For each sample, the carbonylation signal was normalized to the corresponding total protein signal to calculate a Carbonyl Score:

Carbonyl Score (sample X) = Carbonylated protein (fluorescence signal sample X) Total protein (fluorescence signal sample X)

#### Histogram illustration and statistical analysis

Biomarker quantification was normalized to the untreated control group (set to 100%). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for *n* = 3 explants per group. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad (La Jolla, CA, USA). Group comparisons were conducted by one-way ANOVA.

#### Human Repeat Insult Patch Test (HRIPT) for skin irritation and sensitization

The objective of this skin safety study was to establish the safety of UA topical application by testing its irritation and skin sensitization potential in a shared panel of 112 healthy volunteers (aged 19 -65 years, both genders, multiple ethnicities) employing repeated occlusive patch applications based on the modified Draize method. The test articles were applied under occlusive conditions using Finn chambers or equivalent occlusive patches. A total of nine inductions patches were worn for 47 hours or 71 hours, with applications occurring on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays over a three-week period. A makeup day was allowed to ensure subjects had completed all 9 induction patches. Subjects had a rest period of 14 days. Challenge patches were applied for 48 hours and readings were made 1 hour and 48 hours post removal. Three subjects withdrew for personal reasons and 109 subjects completed the study.

#### Human efficacy clinical trials

##### Randomization and instructions of use

The first efficacy skin aging study followed the split-face study design, i.e., each participant was randomly assigned to the test formulations to apply on either the left or the right side of the face and arm *,* where skin biopsies were collected (see). Both the *aging efficacy study* and *the photo-damage trial* were randomized, double-blinded and placebo-controlled.

##### Products tested and instructions of use

The tested formulations for both the *aging efficacy study* and the *photodamage trial* were: a moisturizing skin base cream containing standardized excipients (vehicle) and for the (active) arms containing either 0.5 or 1% UA (Mitopure TM) added in the base cream. A study site representative assigned test formulations so that both the subjects and the investigators were blinded to the test article identification. The information containing the assignment of formulations to labels was kept at the study site by a person not involved in the investigations and analysis of data during the study. The statistical analyses plan was finalized prior to unblinding. Thereafter, the envelope containing the code-to-treatment assignments was opened to unblind the study for analysis. A study technician demonstrated the correct amount of the test formulation to be applied during the first application to the study participants. The participants were instructed to use the test formulation every day. The test materials were applied twice daily by the subjects, in the morning and evening, for 8-weeks according to the application training in the *aging efficacy study*.

##### Skin barrier function

Skin barrier function was quantified as transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and was measured via a TEWAMETER® TM 300 (Courage & Khazaka, Cologne, Germany). TEWL is a non-invasive method to measure the barrier function of the skin and is regarded as a sensitive parameter to quantify skin barrier damage. One measurement per test area and assessment time was recorded. Briefly, water evaporation from the skin was measured by placing cylindric open chamber with two hygrosensors at a defined distance from the skin. The probe was held in place for each measurement for 30 seconds. The values of the last 10 seconds (= 10 values) were averaged as the actual measurement value.

##### Skin roughness and wrinkle assessment

Skin roughness and wrinkle assessment was performed either via standardized, computer-controlled facial photography with a 24 Megapixel Nikon camera (Colorface®, Newtone Technologies, Lyon, France) in the *aging efficacy study*. The anti-wrinkle efficacy of each treatment code was assessed in the periorbital regions *(* A) by investigating the three-dimensional structure of the wrinkles. These assessments were conducted at 2 and 8 weeks. Using phase-shifted and gray-coded measurements of human skin the three-dimensional surface structure of the investigated skin site was captured. The measuring principle is based on digital fringe projection. The fringes that are projected under a defined triangulation angle onto the surface of the measured target with a sinus-like intensity of brightness are detected with a CCD camera. The three-dimensional skin surface profile is calculated from the position of the fringes in combination with the gray values of each pixel. From the captured three-dimensional structure, roughness parameters are calculated. Parameters: Rz and Ra are chosen, representing mainly the rough structure (Rz) or the finer skin structure (Ra). A decrease in the roughness parameters Rz and Ra corresponds to a decrease in the degree of skin roughness. One measurement per test area and assessment time was performed.

Skin hydration assessment was measured via the CORNEOMETER CM 825 (Courage & Khazaka, Cologne, Germany) in the *aging efficacy study*. The measurement of stratum corneum hydration was performed by the electrical capacitance method with the corneometer. The measuring principle is based on changes in the capacitance of the measuring head, functioning as a capacitor. Between the conductors consisting of gold, an electrical field is built. By these means, the dielectricity of the upper skin layer is measured. Because the dielectricity varies as a function of the skin's water content, the stratum corneum hydration can be measured. Five measurements per test area and assessment time were performed and the average recording used for statistical analysis.

##### Skin biopsy excision (from *aging efficacy study*)

The room and biopsy area (dorsal forearms) were disinfected prior to the procedure with the disinfectant spray (e.g., Octenisept®). A local anesthetic (e.g., Skandicain® 1%) was administered to the biopsy site subcutaneously using a sterile syringe, fitted with a suitable sterile needle, to the biopsy area prior to excision of the biopsy. Punch biopsies (3-mm) were sampled by a physician using a punch biopsy instrument. The biopsy specimen contained the epidermis and the dermis. Each biopsy sample was then appropriately labelled (with the study and subject number). Following the biopsy excision, the wound was closed with SteriStrips® and covered with a protective dressing. The subjects were supplied with waterproof dressings for covering the wounds after skin biopsies were taken. The wounds had to be kept dry until the wounds healed. Instructions on changing the dressing and spare dressings were provided to the subjects. In order to reduce the risk of hyperpigmentation the participants undergoing skin biopsy procedure were instructed to avoid sunlight at the test areas after conducting the study and/or to use a sun protection cream. Biopsies were collected at baseline and end of the study ().

##### MED dose calculation, UVB erythema induction and assessments

In the *photodamage trial*, a solar simulator multiport 300 W (SOLAR Light Co, Philadelphia, USA) lamp equipped with appropriate filtration was used to irradiate the skin test areas with the requested irradiation intensity on spots with a diameter of 1 cm. A single irradiation per test area was performed. Test areas were outlined with a skin marker on the backs of the subjects. Thereafter, baseline measurements were performed on all test areas. Minimal erythema dose (MED), i.e. dose to be used to induce erythema, was determined by irradiation on the back with a diameter of 0.9 cm each increasing from spot to spot by an increment of 25%. An untreated irradiated control area served as a negative control. The dose used to induce erythema was 1.6MED. After irradiation with this dose, the test formulations were applied using a patch (i.e. occlude) to the irradiated skin areas, 23 ± 2 hours after irradiation, subjects returned to the study site and formulation topical patches were removed (C). Residues of the test formulations were carefully removed with a dry soft paper tissue. The MED dose and a change in skin redness (erythema score) from baseline was recorded via a CHROMAMETER CR 400 (Minolta, Device D-Langenhagen, Germany). The Chromameter defines the measured color in the L∗a∗b∗ color coordinate system. The L∗-value defines brightness. The color is defined by the parameters a∗ (red-green axis; negative a∗-value green, positive a∗-value red) and b∗ (blue-yellow axis, negative b∗-value blue, positive b∗-value yellow). The a∗ value correlates well with visual assessments of skin redness (erythema). a∗ is a measure for erythema. An increase in the a∗-value corresponds to an increase in the degree of skin redness. A trained grader also assessed the skin erythema for each group of irradiation dose of the different test areas, according to the following scale: -2=marked increase in redness compared to negative control (untreated); -1 =slight increase in redness compared to negative control (untreated); 0=no difference in redness compared to negative control (untreated) (no effect); 1=slight decrease in redness compared to negative control (untreated); 2=marked decrease in redness compared to negative control (untreated); 3=complete suppression of redness.

##### Adverse reactions recording

All ICH GCP guidelines of recording of adverse reactions (ARs) were followed. For all the clinical studies all ARs were assessed by the study physician for a causal relationship between the test material and the adverse event (AE) was established. All adverse reactions (excluding those parameters being scored as part of the protocol) were documented in the study records. ARs were recorded from study enrolment of the subject in a study to until 5 days following last administration of test formulation.

##### Bulk RNA-seq in skin biopsies from human aging efficacy clinical trial

Skin tissue samples were disrupted in a TissueLyser and RNA extraction performed using an automated QiaSymphony extraction robot and the QIAsymphony RNA Kit. Strand-specific cDNA library preparation was performed by purification of poly-A containing mRNA molecules, mRNA fragmentation, random primed cDNA synthesis (strand-specific), adapter ligation and adapter specific PCR amplification. RNA-seq run was performed using the NovaSeq6000 using S4 flowcells with 2x150bp. Quality Control (QC) and preprocessing of raw reads was performed by an in-house QC pipeline. The pipeline utilizes fastP version 0.23.2 [Chen, 2018], for quality control, quality trimming, and adapter clipping procedures. Reads with a minimum PHRED score of 15, a minimum length of 15 bp, and a minimum unqualified percent limit of 40 (40%) were retained for downstream analysis. QC reports were summarized by MultiQC version 1.12 [Ewels et al., 2016]. Quality-processed read pairs were mapped against the Ensembl human reference genome GRCh38.99, using a transcriptome annotation GTF file (version GRCh38.105). In particular, an in-house RNA-sequencing alignment pipeline that utilizes the STAR algorithm version 2.6.1c [Dobin et al., 2013] was applied. Read counting was performed on the gene-level, using htseq-count [Putri, 2022]. To inspect the alignment results, MultiQC was used accordingly. Read counts were normalized to account for inherent compositional and technical biases using “median of ratios” [Anders and Huber, 2010] method from DESeq2 R package [Love, 2014].Differential expression analysis was applied to isolate the effect of treatment by comparing samples between two visits (T2, end of treatment vs T1, baseline) for both interventions (Placebo, UA 1%) while accounting for paired measurements and effect of Placebo condition. This was achieved by using the design formula “∼ Individual_ID + Treatment + visit + Treatment: visit” and isolating the effect due to the coefficient “TreatmentPlacebo.visitT2”.

Before DEA, a pre-filtering step was applied to remove low abundance mRNA measurements, by excluding genes with less than 10 total counts across samples. Additionally, an independent filtering procedure of DESeq2 was enabled, to filter out genes with very low counts that are unlikely to show significant alterations in gene expression. Gene symbols (HGNC nomenclature), descriptions, and biotypes were matched to Ensembl GTF ids by using the R package biomaRt. v. 2.46.3 [Durinck et al., 2009]. *p* -values were corrected for multiple-testing using the Benjamini and Hochberg (BH) method [Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995].

##### Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed by using using fgsea R package (https://doi.org/10.18129/B9.bioc.fgsea) via the R package ClusterProfiler. All processed and filtered genes (genes with at least 10 total counts across samples having an applicable p-adjusted value) were ranked by the comparison’s signed Wald t-test statistic, and the enrichment of a given gene set among “activated”- or- “suppressed” genes was statistically tested. Gene sets from ontologies within Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes (BPs) were tested. The minimum and maximum gene set sizes were set to 10 and 500, respectively. The resulting *p* -values were adjusted using the fgsea *ad hoc* procedure with 10,000 permutations. Enriched terms with a maximum adjusted *p* -value of 0.05 were considered statistically significant. GSEA significant results were visualized as dot plots using the enrichplot R package. Expression changes of core enrichment genes of significant GO BP terms for the “Mitopure normalized over vehicle” comparison was further visualized. For the particular analysis, only subjects present in both visits (T1 and T2) and both treatments (Mitopure and vehicle) were considered. Initially, for each treatment, each subject’s T2 expression values were corrected by their corresponding T1 expression. Then, Mitopure expression changes were further corrected by the vehicle expression changes, between the respective subjects. Ratios are referred as “Fold changes over vehicle”. Finally, log2 Fold changes over vehicle were visualized as heatmaps, by utilizing pheatmap R package [Kolde, 2018]. Genes were hierarchically clustered with a euclidean distance metric using complete linkage.

##### Skin aging dataset analysis

A text file depicting gene expression changes with age in skin was retrieved by the supplemental material of a previously published Glass D. et al., 2013 study: https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art%3A10.1186%2Fgb-2013-14-7-r75/MediaObjects/13059_2012_3307_MOESM1_ESM.TXT. The particular dataset was reanalyzed by GSEA as described above, adjusting “Beta” value which was considered a ranking factor instead of log2 Fold Change. Significant suppressed GO BP terms were intersected with the corresponding significant activated GSEA results of “Mitopure normalized over vehicle” comparison, resulting to one common BP term, “Collagen Fibril Organization”. Common core enrichment genes between the two instances of the particular term were further processed, and visualized as follows: (a) For the RNA-seq dataset, bar plots of “Fold changes over vehicle” (see above for definition) were plotted, while (b) the aging dataset was visualized as bar plots of Beta values.

### Quantification and statistical analysis

Unless otherwise indicated, statistical analyses were performed GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Data are reported as mean ± SEM. Statistically significant differences between 2 independent groups were determined with Student’s unpaired t-test. Unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction were used to determine significance for pairs (*p* < 0.05). Statistically significant differences between three independent groups were determined by one-way ANOVA test followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. For group analysis involving two variables, a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was performed. *p* values less than 0.05 were considered significant. Statistical analysis of RNA-seq experiments is described in the corresponding sections Clinical study statistical analyses. The mean values of raw data and calculated values were presented in bar charts with 95% confidence limits for each treatment and assessment time. A significance level of 0.05 (alpha) was chosen for statistical analysis. Pairwise comparison of treatments was done by paired t-test on differences to baseline for each assessment time and treatment group. For visual and imaging data pairwise comparison of treatments was done by Wilcoxon signed rank test on raw data. The computation of the statistical data was carried out with commercially available statistics software (SAS for Windows).
