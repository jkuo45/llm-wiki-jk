## SUMMARY 

Despite widespread recommendations for higher protein intake during aging, increasing evidence suggests that dietary protein restriction (PR) promotes metabolic health, healthspan, and lifespan across diverse organisms. Mechanistic studies further demonstrate that restriction of specific amino acids, particularly methionine, isoleucine, and valine, recapitulates many of the benefits of PR, highlighting the specific amino acid composition of the diet as a key determinant of aging. Here, we define the hallmarks of PR through the lens of aging, examining how PR and restriction of specific essential amino acids impact metabolic health, nutrient sensing, senescence, mitochondrial function, and the epigenome. We also discuss the known and unknown roles of non-essential amino acids in healthy aging. Overall, this review provides a comprehensive overview of current knowledge regarding the benefits of PR and amino acid restriction for healthy aging and highlights the therapeutic potential of interventions based on these diets to promote healthspan and longevity. 

## INTRODUCTION 

Since McCay’s 1935 report that calorie restriction (CR) extends rat lifespan, CR has proven to be a robust and reproducible intervention, with benefits for lifespan and healthspan in organisms ranging from yeast to non-human primates, as well as significant benefits for human health in short-term studies.[1] However, a CR diet is generally thought to be too difficult for many people,[2] which raises the question: is there a way to achieve the healthspan and lifespan effects of CR without reducing overall energy intake? 

In rodents, the ratio of dietary macronutrients profoundly impacts lifespan, with low-protein, high-carbohydrate diets extending lifespan and improving metabolic health.[3][,][4] A low-protein diet, also referred to as protein restriction (PR), is a robust geroprotective regimen that lowers total dietary protein intake while still meeting nutritional needs. Like CR, PR improves healthspan and increases the lifespan of yeast, flies, and rodents.[3][,][5–9] 

Despite these findings, human dietary recommendations generally suggest increasing protein intake. While the official Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein is 0.8 g/kg of bodyweight regardless of sex or age,[10] intakes of 1.0–1.2 g protein/kg of bodyweight are routinely recommended for individuals over the age of 65 to prevent sarcopenia and frailty,[11–15] and the most recent Dietary Guidelines for America suggest 1.2–1.6 g protein/kg of body weight.[16] These recommendations are supported by studies finding that short-term high protein diets pro- 

mote weight loss, largely by promoting satiety and reducing food intake[17–20] in highly compliant subjects.[21] Sarcopenia, agerelated muscle loss, is of great concern in the elderly due to its contribution to frailty, and increasing dietary protein, often paired with resistance exercise, is advised by medical doctors as a way to improve muscle mass, combat sarcopenia, and reduce frailty in older individuals.[12][,][22–24] 

However, accumulating evidence challenges the idea that higher protein intake is beneficial. Human association studies have found that high-protein diets are associated with an increased risk of diabetes, cancer, and mortality,[25][,][26] as well as an increased risk of death due to cardiovascular events.[27][,][28] An analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data found that higher protein consumption correlates with increased mortality and age-associated disease incidence, including diabetes.[25][,][26][,][29] A 2023 study in the UK reported that increased dietary protein intake was positively associated with sarcopenia in older twins, challenging the conventional idea that dietary protein prevents sarcopenia.[30–33] 

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) support the metabolic benefits of PR in humans. ## HALLMARKS OF DIETARY PR 

Conducting studies in model organisms allows precise control of nutrition, with respect to both quantity and composition. Recent 

work from our lab and others has shown that PR extends the lifespan of rodents.[3][,][5][,][41–43] Table 1 summarizes the broadly positive impact of PR on the lifespan of laboratory animals. Using laboratory models as a basis for PR response, we will discuss the hallmarks of PR, shown in Figure 1, through an aging-focused lens. 

## Hallmark 1: Metabolic health 

Aging in laboratory animals and humans is characterized by decreased energy expenditure,[52–54] increased body weight, and fat accumulation.[55] In young rodents, dietary PR improves metabolic function by reducing adiposity, increasing energy expenditure, and improving glucose homeostasis.[5][,][56–60] This is not due to decreased calorie intake; PR actually increases food intake yet decreases fat mass and weight due to increased energy expenditure.[57][,][61][,][62] Although these effects are most pronounced in males, females also show improved metabolic health with PR.[63][,][64] PR remains effective when initiated late in life (18– 20 months of age), increasing energy expenditure and improving metabolic health in aged mice.[65][,][66] The metabolic improvements induced by PR likely contribute to the longevity benefits of PR, making improved metabolic health our first hallmark.

## Hallmark 2: Nutrient-sensing pathways 

A leading hypothesis for the geroprotective effects of PR is altered nutrient pathway signaling. Amino acid sensing is physiologically important because scarcity promotes catabolic pathways and induces autophagy. Molecular analysis of aging rate indicators shows that PR shifts the molecular signature of the liver toward a more youthful state.[66] General control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2) and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) are two kinases we will discuss, which phosphorylate a subset of aging rate indicators. The ability for organisms to muster a coordinated response to PR requires activation of GCN2 and inhibition of mTORC1. 

_Pathway 1: GCN2_ 

GCN2 is an evolutionarily conserved stress-responsive serine/ threonine kinase that plays a key role in maintaining cellular homeostasis.[96] GCN2 is activated by amino acid restriction through uncharged tRNAs, which bind to GCN2 and directly activate it,[97][,][98] as well as by ribosomes stalling and colliding on RNA.[99] Activated GCN2 phosphorylates the alpha subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2α), suppressing global protein synthesis during nutrient stress[100][,][101] while selectively promoting the translation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), a key activator of stress response and autophagy genes.[102] 

_Drosophila_ lacking _Gcn2_ die when amino acids are restricted,[103] while deletion of _Gcn2_ in yeast blocks the ability of dietary restriction to extend lifespan,[104] demonstrating the importance of _Gcn2_ across species. Conversely, overexpression of _Gcn2_ in yeast and _Drosophila_ extends lifespan.[105][,][106] During PR, GCN2 is initially required for the induction of FGF21,[68][,][107] but when PR is extended, FGF21 is induced even in _Gcn2_ -deficient mice. The response to PR is coordinated by GCN2 activation of FGF21 and by inhibition of mTORC1, thereby activating autophagy pathways.[108] Similarly, GCN2 activates hepatic gluconeogenesis in response to decreased amino acid availability, suggesting its pivotal role in organism metabolism.[109] 

_Pathway 2: mTOR_ 

The master nutrient sensor mTOR forms two complexes: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and complex 2 (mTORC2), with distinctive functions and substrates and which impact aging in different ways. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of mTORC1 extends lifespan in many species; in mice, inhibition of mTORC2 worsens insulin sensitivity, whereas in _Drosophila_ , _C. elegans_ , and mice, it decreases lifespan.[110–115] Normal aging leads to constitutive mTORC1 activation in many tissues, leading to cellular dysfunction,[116–118] and mouse knockout models have revealed that without inhibition of mTORC1, the benefits of PR are lost.[119] PR inhibits mTORC1 in a variety of tissues, including liver, heart, muscle, adipose tissue, and brain.[3][,][66][,][120][,][121] 

mTORC1 integrates nutrient availability and hormonal cues to regulate cellular processes. The canonical regulation of mTORC1 by amino acids has been reviewed at length.[122][,][123] Most recently, it has been discovered that internal and exogenous sources of amino acids differentially regulate mTORC1[124] and that leucine signals to mTORC1 in specific neurons by binding to and activating a specific voltage-gated calcium channel.[125] PR likely inhibits mTORC1 through multiple complementary mechanisms, including reduced availability of mTORC1activating amino acids and alterations in hormones. 

## Review 

PR may promote autophagy while inhibiting protein synthesis. Autophagy preserves cellular homeostasis by removing ‘‘cellular trash,’’ including protein aggregates and dysfunctional organelles.[126][,][127] Upon mTORC1 inhibition, ULK1 initiates autophagosome formation; mice lacking the ability to activate hepatic autophagy have blunted responses to PR.[126] Many age-associated diseases exhibit impaired autophagy, including neurodegeneration,[128] whereas fasting and geroprotective interventions tend to activate autophagy.[129] Inhibition of mTORC1 suppresses translation by reducing phosphorylation of S6K1 and ribosome biogenesis. Genetic ablation of _S6K1_ in mice results in a significant extension of lifespan,[130] suggesting that PR might promote lifespan by decreasing S6K1 activity. Decreased mTORC1 activity also inactivates translation by phosphorylating eIF4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1), blocking eIF4E from binding to RNA and initiating translation. Together, these coordinated effects on autophagy and protein synthesis may drive many of the geroprotective effects of dietary PR. 

## Hallmark 3: Senescence 

Cellular senescence is an irreversible cell-cycle arrest triggered by different cellular stressors.[131][,][132] Senescent cells develop a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) characterized by the activation of pro-inflammatory pathways that promote tissue dysfunction, which can accelerate aging,[133] while clearance of senescent cells can ameliorate age-related disease.[134] 

High-protein diets increase senescence in the liver, while PR reduces hepatic senescence.[66][,][135] Similarly, high-protein diets increase inflammation and senescence in WAT,[136] while PR reduces both senescence and inflammation in the adipose tissue through an FGF21-dependent mechanism.[88] High-protein diets also promote senescent cell accumulation in the kidney, and PR reduces this burden.[137] 

FGF21 also suppresses senescence in cultured cells, reducing drug-induced senescence and the expression of proinflammatory SASP markers.[138][,][139] In diabetic mice, FGF21 administration reduced senescent cells in vascular smooth muscle cells, which experience exacerbated senescence due to chronic hyperglycemia.[140] The detrimental nature of senescent cells in the metabolic tissues could contribute to worsening obesity upon aging, and the ability of dietary PR to decrease adipose and liver senescence may represent an important mechanism by which PR regulates healthspan. 

## Hallmark 4: Mitochondrial dysfunction 

Possessing their own genome, metabolome, proteome, and transcriptome, mitochondria generate ATP while also serving as central regulators of metabolism. Mitochondria become less efficient with age, with reduced mitochondria recycling, the accumulation of DNA damage, and impaired electron transport chain activity reducing ATP production. Mitochondria are the primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS), extremely reactive chemical molecules that damage proteins, lipids, and DNA. Increased ROS with aging can drive senescence in a variety of cell types,[134] and mitochondrial dysfunction has been linked to many age-associated diseases, including insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D).[141]

## Review 

While CR preserves mitochondrial function by reducing ROS, improving mitochondrial biogenesis, and reducing DNA mutations, few studies have examined the impact of PR on mitochondria.[142][,][143] PR reduces hepatic ROS and increases mitochondrial activity, suggesting that PR improves mitochondrial function.[3][,][144][,][145] Conversely, feeding mice a high-protein diet significantly reduces electron transport chain activity in the skeletal muscle and impairs exercise endurance, suggesting that increased dietary protein may be harmful to skeletal muscle mitochondrial homeostasis, perhaps due to mTORC1-mediated suppression of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK).[146][,][147] Similarly, increased dietary protein also decreases hepatic ATP synthesis in chickens.[148] 

The association of PR with improved mitochondrial function and high-protein diets with dysfunction is not uniformly observed. Dietary protein supplements have been reported to increase mitochondrial respiration and reduce oxidative stress in the brain,[149] while a different clinical trial reported no improvements in mitochondrial energy production after 10 days of PR.[150] In a mouse study, we observed no effect of dietary protein on mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle.[151] These varying effects of PR on mitochondrial function may be due to experimental factors that vary between experiments. These studies highlight not only the potential benefits of PR for mitochondrial function but also the remaining knowledge gap in clarifying how dietary protein regulates mitochondrial function. 

## Hallmark 5: Epigenetic modification 

Epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation and posttranslational modifications (PTMs) of histone tails, allow organisms to adjust gene expression without altering the underlying DNA. Aging is accompanied by and perhaps driven in part by the erosion of the epigenetic landscape, leading to improper gene regulation and promoting the hallmarks of aging, including cell senescence.[152] Histone methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation all change with age, and a subset of histone PTMs can be used as molecular markers of cellular aging.[152][,][153] 

The severe food restriction experienced during the Dutch winter of 1944/1945 resulted in transgenerational effects of nutrition on health mediated by epigenetic mechanisms,[154][,][155] and CR has been shown to blunt age-associated changes in DNA methylation.[156] In contrast, little is known about how PR impacts the epigenome. PR in pregnant rodents leads to loci-specific changes in histone methylation and thus gene expression,[157][,][158] while in pigs, changing dietary protein levels led to altered expression of DNA methyltransferases in the liver and skeletal muscle in the mothers,[159] as well as loci-specific changes in histone methylation in the livers of the offspring.[160] Some studies report that the offspring from PR mothers exhibit reduced body weight, reduced hepatic lipid accumulation, and improved glucose tolerance early in life,[161] while others report long-term metabolic dysfunction in the offspring.[162–164] As discussed below, accumulating evidence suggests that dietary levels of specific amino acids regulate histone PTMs.[165–167] Collectively, these data suggest that PR can impact gene expression in both organisms and their offspring by regulating the epigenome and may promote healthspan by preserving or restoring a youthful epigenetic landscape.

## Hallmark 6: Healthy aging 

PR not only extends lifespan but also extends healthspan across diverse species.[3][,][5][,][41][,][49][,][168–173] In addition to the many positive effects on the hallmarks we have defined above, a key factor in healthspan is frailty, a state of vulnerability in which the risk of dependency, falls, hospitalization, and mortality increases. 

Older adults are now advised to consume more protein to prevent frailty and combat sarcopenia, a recommendation supported by several studies. For example, adults consuming 1.2 g/kg of bodyweight/day had 40% less skeletal muscle loss over 3 years compared to those consuming 0.8 g/kg.[14] Higher protein intake is associated with a reduced risk of frailty in observational studies,[174][,][175] and an analysis of UK Biobank data found that consuming more protein reduced the risk of frailty in individuals over the age of 50.[176] However, a recent study of British twins showed that eating more protein is associated with increased risk of sarcopenia,[30] while consumption of a low-protein Mediterranean diet is associated with increased muscle mass and strength.[177] Protein source may partially explain these conflicting data, as plant but not animal protein has been shown to be associated with a reduced risk of frailty.[178] 

Our own studies have found that although a high-protein diet promotes muscle growth, especially when combined with resistance exercise,[151] PR blunts age-associated increases in frailty.[173] Further, PR initiated late in life reduces lean mass but may reduce or blunt further declines in frailty.[64][,][66] Exercise can largely offset the lean mass loss associated with low-protein diets in humans, suggesting that individuals may be able to obtain the benefits of PR while preserving muscle.[179] 

Although the effects of PR on human lifespan are unknown, it has been hypothesized that the long lifespan of the Japanese population of Okinawa is due to their diet, which contains about 9% protein and is strikingly similar to that of PR laboratory studies.[180] The Okinawan diet also derives 80% of its calories from plant sources, and individuals generally consume fewer calories overall, making it difficult to assign all of the benefits to the lower protein intake.[181] Nevertheless, these observations support the possibility that PR may promote healthy aging in humans. 

Another important component of aging is resilience, the ability to ‘‘bounce back’’ from stress. Although lab animals are maintained in a low-stress, controlled environment, the ability to withstand and recover from a variety of stresses, including extremes of temperature, fasting, and infections, is critical to healthy aging in humans. Cellular resilience, the ability of cells to respond to stressors such as hypoxia, pathogens, or heat, also declines with age.[182] The loss of resilience contributes to chronic disease, multimorbidity, and death and is influenced by diet, lifestyle, and genetic factors. PR in _Drosophila_ increases resilience during aging, improving responses to starvation, bacterial infection, and heat stress.[169] The impact of PR on resilience in mammals remains to be determined.

## Methionine 

Methionine restriction extends the lifespan of many species.[183][,][184][,][186][,][191–193] When initiated early or late in life, methionine restriction reduces body weight; increases lean mass; improves mitochondrial function, lipid metabolism, and frailty; and extends the lifespan of rodents.[184][,][194–198] The metabolic response to methionine restriction is partially dependent on FGF21, and mice lacking _Fgf21_ exhibit blunted responses of energy expenditure, beiging, and insulin sensitivity upon methionine restriction.[72][,][199] Methionine restriction also inhibits mTORC1, which may contribute to the lifespan-extending effects.[196] 

Methionine is the precursor of S-adenoysl-L-methionine, the universal methyl donor.[200] Dietary methionine deprivation may impact physiology through changes in histone methylation due to decreased availability of methyl donors,[201] suggesting that chromatin alterations are a direct result of carbon sensing and availability. While this could be thought to be detrimental to cellular health, cells are able to preserve heterochromatic stability through upregulation of H3K9 mono-methylation in the liver, suggesting that organisms may undergo epigenetic adaptation in response to altered amino acid availability.[166][,][167] 

There are promising studies investigating methionine restriction or sulfur amino acid restriction in humans. Eight weeks of sulfur amino acid restriction increased FGF21, reduced leptin, and reduced body weight.[202][,][203] Likewise, individuals adhering to a strict vegan diet, which has been shown to reduce blood levels of methionine,[204][,][205] experience weight loss, reduced circulating lipid levels, and improved glucose homeostasis.[206–208] 

## BCAAs 

High circulating levels of BCAAs are associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and mortality in humans.[209–211] In rodents, highBCAA diets promote obesity and insulin resistance, increase inflammation, and decrease lifespan.[209][,][212–214] Conversely, dietary restriction of the BCAAs promotes metabolic health in both lean and obese rodents, and in lean mice, it reduces frailty and extends lifespan, recapitulating many of the benefits of PR.[5][,][34][,][56][,][59][,][73][,][215–217] BCAA restriction improves Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology and cognition in rodent models of AD, and restriction of each individual BCAA impacts cognition, Aβ plaque deposition, and tau pathology differently. While these studies generally conclude that decreased dietary BCAAs are beneficial for healthy aging, positive effects of BCAAs on mitochondrial function, hepatic steatosis, physical performance, and lifespan have been reported.[218–221] 

BCAA restriction also recapitulates several of the hallmarks of PR. BCAA restriction reduces mTORC1 signaling, induces _Fgf21_ expression, and reduces hepatic cellular senescence.[5][,][56][,][222] BCAA restriction in individuals with metabolic syndrome resulted in improved mitochondrial respiratory efficiency, promoting overall health.[223] In _Drosophila,_ epigenetic changes induced by BCAA restriction mediate the benefits of this diet for lifespan.[165] 

To date, there have been two human studies investigating the impact of dietary BCAA restriction. The first reduced BCAA intake by 75% for 7 days in lean individuals and found that circulating BCAAs were reduced by 50% and insulin sensitivity was improved.[224] The second RCT reduced dietary BCAAs by ∼60% for 4 weeks, resulting in improved glucose and insulin homeostasis, increased circulating FGF21, and reduced mTORC1 signaling in the WAT.[223] Conversely, increased BCAA intake was correlated with reduced sarcopenia risk in a cohort of Chinese adults aged 55+,[225] while a study found that BCAA supplementation paired with light resistance exercise improved muscle strength in patients in long-term care.[226] The incomplete consensus on BCAA restriction/supplementation, perhaps due to alternative factors such as physical activity, highlights the

Leucine is a potent mTORC1 activator that stimulates skeletal muscle anabolism[227] and has been rigorously studied as a dietary intervention to promote healthy aging. In humans, leucine supplementation increases muscle protein synthesis[227][,][228] but does not improve muscle function.[229][,][230] Leucine supplementation decreases body fat in aging rats,[231] consistent with our finding that leucine restriction in mice increases body weight, adiposity, and fat mass.[34] However, some groups have reported the opposite, finding that leucine restriction is effective at reducing fat mass and improving glucose homeostasis.[196][,][232] It is possible that differences in the specific degree of leucine restriction, or the ratio of other amino acids to leucine, explain these divergent results. 

Similarly, divergent results about the effect of leucine on cardiovascular health have been reported. Some studies suggest that leucine negatively impacts cardiovascular health, showing that leucine supplementation increases atherosclerosis plaque progression in mice in an mTORC1-dependent manner[233][,][234] and that the leucine metabolite isovaleric acid accelerates the development of atrial fibrillation.[235] However, other groups report that leucine supplementation improves vascular function in young and aged mice.[236–238] 

When it comes to longevity, while leucine supplementation extends the chronological lifespan of yeast,[239] leucine supplementation does not extend mouse lifespan.[240] Collectively, these results suggest that changes in leucine are unlikely to be responsible for the benefits of a PR diet. 

_BCAA: Isoleucine_ 

Isoleucine has recently emerged as a key regulator of metabolism and aging. Dietary isoleucine levels are correlated with body mass index in humans,[73] and increased circulating isoleucine levels are associated with increased mortality risk.[210] In Western diet-fed mice, increasing dietary isoleucine promotes body weight and fat accretion, potentially because of lower energy expenditure, as well as glucose intolerance and insulin resistance.[213] 

In contrast, isoleucine restriction consistently improves metabolic health across multiple dietary contexts and age groups in mice.[66][,][73][,][173][,][213] Isoleucine restriction extends the lifespan of both _Drosophila_ and mice, reduces frailty and increases healthspan in mice, and rejuvenates aged mice at the molecular level, modifying signaling through nutrient-sensing pathways.[66][,][165][,][172][,][173][,][190] Additionally, isoleucine restriction induces substantial changes in the epigenome, including PTMs on histone tails.[166][,][241] Isoleucine restriction also improves AD outcomes in male mice.[242] Together, these studies show that isoleucine is an exceptionally powerful modifier of the hallmarks of PR, and reduced isoleucine almost certainly contributes to the benefits of a PR diet. 

## _BCAA: Valine_ 

Valine metabolism involves the conversion to succinyl-CoA, which can enter the TCA cycle and produce ATP. Restricting valine alone reduces body weight, improves metabolic health, 

reduces hepatic senescence, and extends the lifespan of male mice.[185] In female mice, valine restriction is protective of AD progression.[242] Dysregulated valine catabolism, driven by 3-hydroxy-isobutyrate in diabetic rats, has been shown to contribute to their insulin resistance.[243] Carp fish fed a diet reduced in valine show reduced weight gain,[244][,][245] confirming dietary valine’s impact on body composition across species. 

Human plasma association analysis found that reduced circulating valine is associated with aging and moderate cognitive impairment,[246] suggesting that valine supplementation might be beneficial. However, valine supplementation worsens glucose tolerance and induces gut inflammation and hepatic lipid deposition, thereby associating high valine levels with negative health markers.[247–249] Reduced levels of valine may contribute to the benefits of PR, but the role of valine in healthy aging may vary between species. 

## Other EAAs 

While isoleucine, valine, and methionine restriction are the main EAAs driving the benefits of PR, other EAAs may also impact aging. A 1978 report showed that tryptophan restriction extends the lifespan of rodents[250] ; however, the failure of this finding to be reproduced in the 2000s brings this conclusion into question. Histidine restriction blunts weight gain in Western diet-fed mice without extending lifespan.[251] Another group reported that dietary threonine restriction improves the metabolic health of mice through direct FGF21 induction.[183] Similarly, phenylalanine administration induces insulin resistance and contributes to T2D progression in mouse models, suggesting its negative contribution to healthy aging.[252] Lastly, it was shown that a lysine-restricted diet ameliorates obesity in mice.[253] These data taken together highlight ongoing research into how these canonically understudied EAAs contribute to the hallmarks of PR. 

The data on EAAs are abundant in comparison to NEAAs, yet new evidence is emerging for other amino acids that may play a role in the response to PR. While BCAAs and EAAs have long been thought to be responsible for the benefits of PR, investigations into the role of NEAAs have muddied the water. 

## NEAAs 

Traditional classification of amino acids into essential and nonessential relies on an organism’s ability to synthesize an amino acid from glucose or other amino acids. While EAAs are required from the diet and restriction of EAAs is sufficient to promote healthy aging and mimic the effects of PR,[49][,][183] there is currently little evidence that restriction of any single NEAA broadly recapitulates PR. Indeed, there is comparatively little research on NEAAs, most likely because restricting NEAAs has been thought to be futile, as they can be synthesized as needed to meet demand. However, emerging evidence suggests that dietary NEAAs can impact specific aspects of aging. With regard to metabolism, alanine supplementation blunts high-protein-induced weight gain in mice.[272] Alanine administration robustly activates AMPK in mouse liver, thereby reducing blood glucose levels[273] ; however, administration to diabetic rats had no effect on body weight or blood glucose levels.[274] In yeast, deletion of the alanine transaminase (ALT) gene leads to an accumulation of alanine and decreased chronological lifespan.[254] In humans, observational studies have found that low ALT levels are associated with an increased risk of frailty and mortality in middle age.[275–277] Alanine declines with age in circulation and some tissues across diverse species, including humans, mice, and wasps.[118][,][278][,][279] While it is clear that alanine impacts metabolism, the question of whether altering dietary levels impacts aging remains unknown. 

_Arginine_ 

Arginine is a conditionally essential amino acid in mammals, as early embryos and the young are unable to synthesize the amount of arginine that is required for growth.[280][,][281] Arginine plays a key role in multiple biochemical processes, including detoxification through the urea cycle, generation of nitric oxide for cardiovascular function, and immune regulation,[282] and is metabolized into compounds such as nitric oxide, urea, citrulline, creatine, agmatine, and polyamines, including spermidine.[283][,][284] Arginine regulates mTORC1 activity,[285] and arginine depletion _in vivo_ and _in vitro_ reduces mTORC1 activity,[286] similar to the effects of PR. A recent report found that plasma arginine levels increase with age,[257] suggesting that less arginine might be beneficial. 

_Drosophila_ fed extra arginine have increased body weight, decreased lifespan, and increased levels of oxidative stress.[255] Mice lacking nitric oxide synthase, which converts arginine to citrulline and releases nitric oxide, have increased arginine levels and increased body weight, diminished metabolic health, and increased lipids in circulation and the liver.[287][,][288] Supplementation of arginine to aged rodents was detrimental to kidney function.[289][,][290] Long-term arginine supplementation of human endothelial cells results in increased senescence, partially driven by increased mTORC1 activity.[291][,][292] While these data argue that arginine supplementation is detrimental to health, there are also studies reporting that arginine supplementation is beneficial. For example, obese Zucker diabetic fatty rats supplemented with arginine in drinking water exhibited reduced weight gain, improved glucose tolerance, and smaller white fat pads.[293–295] One clinical study found that dietary arginine supplementation during obesity reduces blood pressure, circulating glucose, and serum fatty acids; improves wound healing; and may improve cardiovascular health,[296] while a clinical trial in middle-aged obese patients with T2D found that supplementation of arginine in combination with CR and exercise exhibited significant fat loss that was not observed in the placebo group.[297] Potentially, these varied results may be explained by arginine supplementation having different impacts in lean and obese subjects. 

Increasing arginine catabolism through drug treatment is often used as a therapy for patients with cancer,[298] as some cancer cells lack the enzymes to directly synthesize arginine them- 

selves.[299] Arginine depletion is generally successful at inducing apoptosis in cancer cells, leaving healthy somatic cells unscathed.[300] Arginine deprivation of human cells in cell culture media is sufficient to reduce senescence,[257] making it a potential target for aging. The impact of dietary arginine restriction on aging and aging-associated diseases in laboratory models and humans is still unknown. 

## _Asparagine/aspartate_ 

Cellular asparagine is metabolized into aspartate; current literature on dietary aspartate is minimal, while dietary asparagine studies have some exciting results for lifespan. These two amino acids serve as building blocks for glucose and protein synthesis and directly activate two important nutrient-sensing pathways: AMPK and mTORC1.[301][,][302] 

Clinical studies have revealed that upon aging, circulating asparagine levels are reduced.[303][,][304] In humans, a higher asparagine/aspartate ratio is associated with increased risk of diabetes, suggesting that dysfunction of asparagine/aspartate homeostasis can worsen metabolic health.[305] Analysis of community-dwelling older frail individuals revealed elevated plasma asparagine, suggesting that increased asparagine levels may be a marker of frailty.[306] 

Removing asparagine from yeast media significantly extends chronological lifespan, most likely by inhibiting TOR.[258] Druginduced depletion of asparagine has been used in the clinic for cancer treatment for many years. In this context, asparaginase is administered intravenously or intramuscularly, resulting in the apoptosis of cancer cells that lack the enzymes to produce the amino acid themselves.[307] While the exact mechanism of asparaginase-induced apoptosis is unknown, it is believed to be coordinated through PR hallmarks: GCN2, mTORC1, and FGF21.[107][,][308] 

Using _Drosophila_ to investigate dietary asparagine supplementation, a group recently found that supplementation extends lifespan through improved intestinal stem cell maintenance.[260] However, studies in _C. elegans_ suggest that there may be a limit at which supplementation becomes detrimental: at 1 and 5 mM, supplementation extended lifespan, but 10 mM supplementation significantly reduced lifespan.[259] Additional studies of dietary manipulation of asparagine in mammals are needed to clarify if supplementation or restriction is beneficial for healthy aging. _Cysteine_ 

Cysteine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that can be directly metabolized from methionine. In fact, most dietary methionine restriction studies also require depletion of cysteine, or the benefits of methionine restriction on metabolic health are lost.[309][,][310] 

There are few studies investigating cysteine restriction or supplementation on lifespan. Cysteine accumulation may promote aging and tissue dysfunction through mitochondrial decline.[311] One report found that dietary cysteine deficiency improves metabolic health through increased energy expenditure by activating thermogenesis and beiging in rodents.[261] Conversely, cysteine supplementation at <5 mM concentrations to _C. elegans_ significantly extends median lifespan by 8%– 16%[259] ; however, this observation has not been reported in other organisms. As cysteine can affect mitochondrial function and metabolic health, it is important that future studies determine whether cysteine depletion affects other hallmarks of PR.

## _Glutamic acid/glutamate_ 

Glutamate is the most abundant neurotransmitter in the brain and has a critical role in learning and memory.[315] In normal aging, glutamate levels increase in the brain, which can drive the development of neurodegenerative disorders.[316][,][317] The current literature has conflicting results on the impact of external glutamic acid/glutamate, as supplementation[104] and restriction[258] both extend yeast chronological lifespan. _C. elegans_ supplemented with 1 or 5 mM had significant lifespan extension; however, at 10 mM, the lifespan was significantly shortened, suggesting a dose-dependent effect of glutamate.[259] Interestingly, the exceptionally long-lived naked mole rat species has higher circulating glutamate levels compared to the normal aging C57BL/6J mice.[318] 

## _Glutamine_ 

Glutamine has an integral role in energy metabolism, entering the TCA cycle through α-ketoglutarate.[319] Long-term glutamine deprivation in _Drosophila_ increases senescence and decreases lifespan, while supplementation of glutamine ameliorated the senescent phenotype observed with glutamine deprivation.[262] A different group found that senescent cells require extracellular glutamine to exert the SASP and that glutaminase inhibition induces senescent cell death.[320] 

There are a few studies investigating glutamine supplementation in humans. One trial reported that glutamine supplementation in six females reduced body weight without impacting other metabolic health parameters,[321] while another group reported that glutamine supplementation reduced waist circumference but not weight in overweight adults.[322] Overall, the literature is not conclusive on whether supplementing or restricting glutamine is beneficial for aging. _Glycine_ 

Glycine plays an important role in metabolism, serving as a substrate for glutathione, a key antioxidant scavenger.[323] Glutathione levels are reduced in individuals with obesity, which could exacerbate the pro-inflammatory phenotype of the adipose tissue,[324] suggesting that increased glutathione could be a therapeutic strategy for age-associated obesity. 

The ITP reported that glycine supplementation beginning at 9 months of age extended the lifespan of male and female mice by 6.2% and 3.7%, respectively.[263] Glycine supplementation also extends the lifespan of _C. elegans_ ,[259][,][264] although, conversely, overexpression of the glycine catabolic enzyme _Gnmt_ increases median lifespan in _Drosophila_ .[325] Supplementation of the diet with collagen, which is rich in glycine, improves healthspan and increases the lifespan of _C. elegans_ ,[326][,][327] _Drosophila_ ,[328] and rodents.[329][,][330] Dietary supplementation with amino acids in the ratio found in collagen—3:1:1 glycine:proline: hydroxyproline—promotes longevity in mice and reduces biological age in humans.[331] 

## Review 

Supplementation of glycine is associated with increased production of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), potentially because the cells that release GLP-1 are glycine-gated channels.[332] This phenomenon may explain the improvements in metabolic health seen with glycine supplementation. Higher glycine levels in human plasma are associated with improved metabolic health through improved insulin sensitivity,[333][,][334] and glycine supplementation improves metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD) in laboratory models.[335] However, glycine supplementation does not impact body weight in individuals, suggesting the improved metabolic health is observed more at the pathological level and not at the physiological level.[336] Interestingly, the mechanism responsible for the lifespan extension of glycine supplementation and the impact on human healthspan and lifespan are currently unknown. _Proline_ 

Proline serves as the major nitrogen donor for synthesizing polyamines.[265] Impairment in the proline catabolic pathway leads to shortened lifespan through impaired mitochondrial function, suggesting that proline may be beneficial for healthy aging.[337][,][338] Proline levels in plasma, muscle, and skin are reduced in aged organisms,[339][,][340] and supplementation extends the lifespan of yeast and _C. elegans_ .[259][,][265] Proline levels are reduced in senescent cells, and supplementation decreases senescence of mesenchymal stem cells in culture.[266][,][341] It is hypothesized that proline may promote lifespan through its ability to scavenge ROS.[342] 

## _Serine_ 

Serine contributes to diverse metabolic processes, with serine intermediates playing roles in glycolysis, methionine cycling, and lipid synthesis.[343] Rodent models of obesity and insulin resistance have reduced tissue levels of serine, and supplementation with serine alleviates some of the co-morbidities that occur with diabetes.[344][,][345] Normal aging in humans leads to lower circulating serine levels that contribute to age-related disorders.[346][,][347] Mice fed a serine free diet or pharmacological depletion had increased hepatic lipid accumulation,[348] increased inflammation and oxidative damage,[349] suggesting that serine supplementation rather than depletion may be beneficial for healthy aging. 

Studies in yeast[350] and _C_ . _elegans_[259][,][267] reveal that dietary supplementation of serine leads to lifespan extension. Serine supplementation in rodents improves metabolic health, improving glycemic control and reducing hepatic lipid accumulation[351] while decreasing body weight and food intake in aging rodents.[352] These data taken together suggest that decreased serine is unlikely to drive the benefits of PR, as supplementation is beneficial for metabolic health and lifespan. _Tyrosine_

## DISCUSSION 

As the world’s population grows ever older, it is becoming increasingly imperative to identify interventions that can promote healthy aging and reduce the burden of age-related disease. Over the past two decades, research has identified a critical role for dietary protein in the regulation of lifespan, with PR extending lifespan in _Drosophila_ and rodents. The subsequent discovery that restricting specific amino acids can recapitulate the benefits of PR has transformed our understanding of how diet regulates aging. Research has highlighted that there is a shared conserved response to aging-associated phenotypes between CR, PR, and amino acid restriction. Understanding the strings that connect diet, aging, and maintenance of organismal function could be imperative for advancing therapeutic interventions in aging. 

In this review, we detail, for the first time, the hallmarks of PR: improved metabolic health, induction of nutrient-sensing pathways, decreased senescence, improved mitochondrial function, altered epigenome, and the promotion of healthy aging. Taken together, these hallmarks describe the robust impact of PR on aging-related phenotypes.[360] While these hallmarks are seen in most organisms on dietary PR, some variations exist based on biological sex or animal strain.[64] The hallmarks discussed in this review are highly interconnected, providing a firm foundation for future exploration on the causal nature of these hallmarks to understand which hallmarks are most important in orchestrating the response to PR. Undoubtedly, as research in the field continues, there will be additions to our proposed hallmark list that may shed further light on the mechanisms of PR and identify new therapeutic targets.

Many elderly individuals are protein deficient due to factors such as decreased appetite, financial constraints, and social isolation,[361] and protein or amino acid restriction could move these individuals into a protein- or amino acid-insufficient state. Finally, those who are exercising may have higher protein needs or at least be able to consume higher levels of protein or specific amino acids without metabolic risk. 

While challenges remain in determining the optimal levels of dietary protein and specific amino acids for individual health, the potential benefits of doing so are great enough that they demand that scientists take the necessary next steps to translate these interventions into therapies for humans. The next critical steps place an enormous amount of responsibility on clinical researchers. We must work together to design suitable diets for individuals; identify methods and metrics to assess the effects of diet on health, frailty, and aging; and understand how personal factors impact dietary needs. 

Many questions remain about the role specific amino acids play in the lifespan-extending benefits of PR. Manipulating dietary NEAA levels could significantly impact the aging process of certain tissues and could promote metabolic health, brain function, and cardiovascular disease. One limitation of this conclusion is that most of the NEAA studies were done in lower-order organisms such as yeast or _C. elegans_ ,[259] thus highlighting the need for more in-depth research in mammals to fully understand how the restriction of each dietary amino acid contributes to the PR phenotype and overall organismal aging. By targeting specific NEAAs, researchers could identify metabolic pathways to therapeutically target to improve health outcomes with aging.
