## **Summary**

Partial reprogramming is therapeutically useful only if cells keep its benefits while recovering their lineage identity and function after the reprogramming factors are withdrawn. Whether the relationship between reprogramming depth and recovery differs between lineages is unknown. Using reversible, traceable Sendai-OSKM, we prospectively isolated intermediate reprogramming states from primary human fibroblasts, cytotoxic T cells, and aortic endothelial cells and followed their recovery. Outcomes diverged: fibroblasts kept a reduced DNA methylation age but retained stromal remodeling and incomplete proliferative recovery; T cells recovered only from shallow reprogramming, with enhanced effector function; endothelial cells rapidly recovered identity, kept a reduced methylation age, and markedly improved vascular function. None of this tracked iPSC derivation efficiency. Multiome and knockdown analyses identified TOX as a shared progression dependency, whereas its candidate downstream programs were predominantly lineage-specific. Partial reprogramming involves a lineagespecific tradeoff between rejuvenation and recovery that must be optimized one lineage at a time.

## **Introduction**

Age-associated molecular and epigenetic alterations progressively compromise tissue function<sup>1-6</sup> . However, some age-associated alterations are reversible in the lab, which has motivated efforts to repair aging tissues by resetting cellular states without replacing the cells themselves. Complete reprogramming with OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and MYC (OSKM) can erase age-associated molecular features<sup>7-9</sup> . However, it entails the acquisition of pluripotency and the loss of somatic identity, thereby raising substantial safety concerns for in vivo applications, particularly the risk of teratoma formation<sup>10</sup> . Several alternatives to complete reprogramming have therefore been investigated. Transient reprogramming has improved age-associated molecular and functional phenotypes in vivo<sup>11,12</sup> and in cultured human cells<sup>13,14</sup> , while reversible reprogramming has promoted regenerative plasticity<sup>15</sup> . Several modes of transient reprogramming have been investigated, including brief exposure to reprogramming factors of dermal fibroblasts and endothelial cells derived from iliac arteries and veins,

One study mapped these dynamics at single-cell resolution in murine adipogenic and mesenchymal stromal cells, and found that partial reprogramming restored youthful gene expression while suppressing somatic identity only transiently, and that the two effects were not tightly coupled<sup>16</sup> . Another found that a single fixed OSK exposure attenuated endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and cellular senescence in human aortic endothelial cells and lowered blood pressure in hypertensive mice<sup>17</sup> . Nevertheless, no study has systematically compared multiple primary human lineages to determine how reprogramming depth affects the cells’ ability to retain the benefits of reprogramming and to recover their lineage identity and function after withdrawal of the reprogramming factors. This information is necessary to define, for each cell type, a partial reprogramming window that yields the desired benefits without compromising post-withdrawal recovery.

In addition, the endogenous processes linking OSKM-induced progression to intermediate reprogramming states and post-withdrawal outcomes remain poorly defined. The ability of OSKM to induce pluripotency across diverse somatic cell types suggests that OSKM engages common core mechanisms. Yet reprogramming efficiency and trajectories vary markedly with cell of origin, and cross-lineage studies have identified both shared late-stage features and cell-type-specific sequences of identity loss and pluripotency acquisition<sup>18,19</sup> . These observations raise the question of whether partial reprogramming proceeds through equivalent intermediate states across lineages or instead combines shared regulatory dependencies with lineage-specific molecular programs.

Here, using a traceable, reversible Sendai-OSKM system, we prospectively isolated graded intermediate-state populations from primary human fibroblasts, cytotoxic T cells, and aortic endothelial cells and followed their recovery after OSKM withdrawal. Our findings argue against defining partial reprogramming by the duration of factor exposure or inferring the cells’ ability to recover from the efficiency with which they can be fully reprogrammed into iPSCs. Interventions intended to modify ageassociated phenotypes should therefore be judged not by molecular-age changes alone, but by whether cells recover their lineage identity and specialized function after factor withdrawal. Our findings also identify the HMG-box factor TOX as a shared reprogramming dependency that coexists with lineagespecific intermediate states and recovery responses. TOX provides a common mechanistic entry point from which we can ultimately dissect the origins of lineage-divergent mechanisms and outcomes of partial reprogramming.

## **Reporter clearance and recovery kinetics vary by cell type and reprogramming depth**

Next we aimed to analyze the kinetics and extent of recovery after OSKM withdrawal. Immediately after sorting, cultures were shifted from 35°C to 38°C to inhibit replication of the temperature-sensitive SeV genomes and initiate the loss of vector-derived OSKM expression. We defined reporter clearance as the point at which GFP and OFP fluorescence returned to the level of non-transduced cells, measured below (Figures 2A–2C). One day after the shift, while both reporters were still readily detectable, HDF Gr1 cells retained a spindle-like morphology similar to Ctrl cells, whereas HDF Gr2 and Gr3 cells were more rounded. HAEC Gr2 cells were also more rounded than Gr1 cells, although the difference was less pronounced. CTL morphology varied little among groups, but fewer Gr3 cells remained (Figure S2A). We conclude that morphological recovery had barely begun after one day at 38°C.

To monitor the loss of SeV-derived reporter expression after the temperature shift, we quantified GFP and OFP median fluorescence intensity (MFI) over time. At the time of sorting, GFP MFI, reflecting KOS expression, was higher in Gr2 and Gr3 populations than in Gr1 across all cell types (Figure 2A). After the temperature shift, HDF Gr1 cells showed the fastest decline in MFI and reached the baseline level of non-SeV-transduced cells by day 14 (7 days after the temperature shift), followed by Gr2 and Gr3. CTLs showed the most rapid reduction in MFI overall. CTL Gr1 declined fastest, and by day 9 (4 days after the temperature shift), both Gr1 and Gr2 reached MFI levels comparable to non-SeVtransduced cells. CTL Gr3 cells proliferated extremely poorly and disappeared before the MFI reached baseline. The more rapid fluorescence loss in CTLs than in HDFs or HAECs was likely due in part to stronger signal dilution through cell proliferation. In HAECs, Gr1 and Gr2 showed similar rates of MFI decline and reached the baseline slightly earlier than in HDFs, around day 12 (5 days after the temperature shift). In contrast to KOS-GFP MFI, OFP MFI, reflecting cMYC expression, tended to be higher in Gr1 than in Gr2 or Gr3, but the post-temperature-shift decline pattern was broadly similar to that of GFP (Figure 2B).

We also followed the fraction of double-positive (GFP+OFP+) cells (Figure 2C) and found that they disappeared fastest in Gr1, followed by Gr2 and Gr3, in both HDFs and CTLs. CTL Gr3 cells were lost before later measurements because of poor proliferation. HAEC Gr1 and Gr2 lost reporter signal with similar kinetics to each other; HAEC Gr3 was too rare to culture and was not analyzed. Thus, GFP and OFP signals declined gradually after temperature shift in every analyzable condition, albeit with celltype- and state-dependent kinetics, consistent with progressive loss of vector-derived OSKM expression. We next related reporter-clearance kinetics to recovery of lineage-marker states and proliferative capacity. In HDFs, cells sorted as Gr1 remained almost entirely in a Gr1 state thereafter. By contrast, cells sorted as Gr2 or Gr3 gradually changed group profile: by day 22, nearly all Gr2 cells had returned to Gr1, and 10%–20% of Gr3 cells were classified as Gr2 because of relatively weak CD13 expression,

## **Post-clearance outcomes reveal lineage-specific relationships between reprogramming benefits and functional recovery**

Having established reporter-clearance and recovery kinetics, we next analyzed cells after GFP and OFP signals became undetectable. For each cell type, we asked whether the cells retained the reduction in DNA methylation age induced by reprogramming and whether they recovered their initial lineage identity and specialized function at the defined endpoint (Figure 3).

In HDFs, reduced DNA methylation age persisted despite incomplete functional recovery. By day 25, HDFs recovered a morphology broadly similar to Ctrl, and their global transcriptomes converged toward a shared somatic state (Figures 3A and S3B). Gr2 and Gr3 nevertheless retained significantly lower DNA methylation age estimates than Ctrl, whereas Gr1 was variable across donors and no longer differed significantly from Ctrl (Figure 3B). Homeostatic fibroblast identity was broadly reacquired, but Gr2 and Gr3 retained elevated ECM/matrix-remodeling and contractile myofibroblast-like programs (Figure 3D).

Functional recovery depended on reprogramming depth: scratch closure varied among Gr2 donors and did not differ significantly from Ctrl, whereas it was significantly reduced in Gr3, consistent with Gr3 cells’ persistent growth impairment (Figures 3C and S3A). Among the HDF states tested, Gr2 therefore provided the most balanced outcome: sustained DNA methylation age reduction without the severe growth and wound-closure deficits of Gr3. Nevertheless, proliferative recovery remained incomplete and stromal remodeling persisted, indicating a mixed outcome rather than complete functional normalization.

Importantly, reprogramming states that were comparable between cell types (e.g., Gr2 or Gr1) did not produce equivalent relationships between epigenetic rejuvenation and functional recovery.

## **The reprogramming trajectories of HDFs, CTLs and HAECs share a TOX-associated regulatory node**

We first generated state-resolved views of each cell type-specific network in relation to the corresponding ATAC UMAP. Somatic-identity- and pluripotency-associated TF networks were distributed according to reprogramming progression, with pluripotency-related networks becoming increasingly prominent in progressed states (Figure 4G). These patterns were consistent with the flow-cytometric and bulk RNAseq evidence of progressive reprogramming, but the complexity of the individual cell type-specific networks did not by itself nominate a common regulator.

## **_TOX_ is transiently induced across the three cell types within a candidate OSK-responsive cisregulatory landscape**

We first examined _TOX_ expression during reprogramming. Low basal _TOX_ expression was detectable in both Day 0 and Ctrl populations of HDFs and HAECs, and increased markedly in later reprogramming states, particularly from Gr2 onward. In CTLs, _TOX_ induction was most prominent in Gr3, although _TOX_ -high cells (cl6 and cl12) were also substantially represented in Gr2 (Figure S4A). Consistent with these expression patterns, accessibility of _TOX_ -linked peaks and _TOX_ RNA expression were highest in HDF Gr2/Gr3, CTL Gr3, and HAEC Gr2 (Figures 5A and 5B).

To determine whether _TOX_ induction also occurs during full reprogramming to iPSCs, we reanalyzed three independent human HDF-to-iPSC single-cell RNA-seq datasets generated using SeV-OSKM, lentiviral OSKM, or mRNA OSKMLN (OSKM plus LIN28 and NANOG)<sup>33-35</sup> . _TOX_ was transiently induced during early-to-mid reprogramming in all three datasets and subsequently declined toward the established iPSC state (Figure S5A). Thus, transient _TOX_ induction is reproducible across distinct reprogramming platforms and is concentrated in the early-to-mid phase of reprogramming.

To investigate the cis-regulatory basis of _TOX_ induction during partial reprogramming, we integrated peak-to-gene linkage at the _TOX_ locus with nomination of candidate _TOX_ -linked cis-regulatory elements (CREs), cell type- and state-resolved scPrinter/seq2PRINT<sup>36</sup> footprint and sequence-attribution analysis, and motif annotation at attribution peaks (Figure 5C). Genome browser tracks identified seven labeled intronic and distal candidate CREs that gained accessibility with reprogramming progression and showed peak-to-gene links to _TOX_ across HDFs, CTLs, and HAECs (Figure 5D).

Analysis of all seven elements—three representative CREs in the main figure and the remaining four in the supplement—revealed cell type- and state-dependent scPrinter/seq2PRINT sequence-attribution profiles, supporting coordinated opening of multiple intronic and distal _TOX_ -linked CREs rather than regulation through a single proximal element. JASPAR 2026 annotation<sup>37</sup> revealed numerous POUfamily, SOX-family, POU:SOX composite, and KLF-family motif matches at attribution peaks. These findings are consistent with potential inputs from the POU/SOX/KLF network, including plausible contributions from the exogenous OSK factors (Figure 5E and Figure S5B).

## **_TOX_ depletion impairs reprogramming progression across the three cell types**

To test whether TOX contributes functionally to reprogramming rather than merely marking advanced states, we suppressed _TOX_ during reprogramming using siRNAs in HDFs and HAECs and shRNAs in CTLs. HDFs and HAECs were treated separately with each of three _TOX_ -targeting siRNAs (siTOX #1– #3), with transfection repeated every 3 days. CTLs were transduced separately with each of three puromycin-selectable lentiviral vectors expressing distinct _TOX_ -targeting shRNAs (shTOX #1–#3). Bulk RNA-seq and parallel flow cytometric analyses of the marker-defined Gr states were performed on day 7 for HDFs and HAECs and day 5 for CTLs, and iPSC colony formation was assessed on day 14. A non-targeting siRNA (siCtrl) for HDFs and HAECs and a scrambled-sequence shRNA (shCtrl) for CTLs served as matched controls (Figure 6A). _TOX_ knockdown efficiency was assessed on day 7 in HDFs and HAECs after siRNA transfections on days 1 and 4, and on day 5 after SeV-OSKM transduction in CTLs that had undergone lentiviral shRNA transduction and selection before reprogramming (Figure 6B). Knockdown efficiency differed among reagents. The same siRNA sequences were used in HDFs and HAECs, with siTOX #1 and #2 producing particularly efficient knockdown. In CTLs, shRNA-mediated knockdown was weaker overall, with shTOX #2 producing the greatest reduction.

_TOX_ knockdown sharply reduced iPSC derivation efficiency in all three cell types. In HDFs and HAECs, siTOX #1 and #2 nearly abolished colony formation; in CTLs, shTOX #2 produced the largest decrease (Figure 6C). qPCR on day 2 showed that _TOX_ knockdown did not decrease total SeV RNA (Figure 6D), SeV-KOS RNA (Figure S6A), or SeV-cMYC RNA (Figure S6B). Lower early abundance of the exogenous reprogramming-factor transcripts is therefore unlikely to account for the reduced colony formation.

To determine whether reduced iPSC derivation could reflect a toxic effect of TOX reduction, we reduced _TOX_ expression in HDFs, HAECs, and CTLs, in the absence of SeV-OSKM. In HDFs and HAECs, _TOX_ expression was measured 2 days after siRNA transfection and cell yields assessed on day 3. _TOX_ expression was reduced by siTOX #1 and #2 in HDFs and by all three siRNAs in HAECs (Figure S6C). For CTLs, cells transduced with shTOX or shCtrl lentiviral vectors were selected with puromycin for 7 days, stimulated with CD3/CD28, and analyzed 3 days later. _TOX_ expression remained very low in CTLs under this pre-reprogramming stimulation condition, resulting in variable relative estimates; however, the same shRNAs suppressed OSKM-induced _TOX_ expression (Figure 6B). Cell yield was not significantly reduced in HDFs or CTLs. In HAECs, it was unaffected by siTOX #1 or #3 but was reduced by siTOX #2 (Figure S6D). Because siTOX #1 and #3 reduced _TOX_ expression in HAECs without reducing cell yield, this effect of siRNA#2 is unlikely to reflect a requirement for TOX in HAEC survival; it may instead arise from a sequence-specific off-target toxicity of siRNA#2, although a deeper _TOX_ depletion beyond the cells’ viability threshold cannot be excluded. Together, these findings indicated that the negative impact of TOX loss of function on iPSC derivation could not simply be explained by a reduction in cell yield. ## **_TOX_ knockdown shifts shared proliferative and EMT/TGF-** β **programs but produces cell typespecific gene-level responses**

In cells still expressing SeV-OSKM, bulk RNA-seq after acute _TOX_ knockdown revealed the largest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HDFs, followed by HAECs and CTLs. Conversely, the Hallmark epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) gene set was positively enriched in all three cell types, and TGF- β signaling was also positively enriched, most prominently in HDFs (Figure 6G). ## **_TOX_ overexpression is not sufficient to enhance iPSC formation**

Because _TOX_ depletion impaired reprogramming, we next asked whether increasing _TOX_ expression was sufficient to enhance it. We generated lentiviral Tet-On _TOX_ HDFs and induced _TOX_ during time windows corresponding to its endogenous transient expression (Figure 6H). Doxycycline treatment markedly increased _TOX_ expression relative to Ctrl Dox (+), although cells transduced with the _TOX_ overexpression vector showed a slight increase in _TOX_ expression even without doxycycline (Figure 6I). _TOX_ overexpression did not substantially improve iPSC colony formation under any induction schedule; colony output was numerically lower in several conditions, except under the no-doxycycline and day 10–14 doxycycline conditions (Figure 6J).

_TOX_ induction produced few large changes at the individual-gene level beyond TOX itself (Figure S6G), but shifted the pathways affected by knockdown in the opposite direction: E2F target, G2M checkpoint, and MYC target programs increased, whereas EMT and TGF- β signaling decreased (Figure 6K). Thus, the overexpression construct was biologically active, yet it did not increase iPSC colony formation. Together with the loss-of-function results, these findings identify TOX as a contextdependent, permissive component of the OSKM network rather than a factor sufficient to drive iPSC formation. The shared loss-of-function phenotype, coupled with distinct gene-level responses, prompted us to examine the cell type-specific regulatory contexts in which TOX operates.

## **Discussion**

The central challenge in translating partial reprogramming is not simply to reverse molecular marks of aging, but to define how far to reprogram the cells so that they durably recover their source-lineage identity and function after OSKM withdrawal. Our findings suggest that the answer to this question depends on lineage or cell type, as human fibroblasts, cytotoxic T cells, and aortic endothelial cells displayed different abilities to recover from partial reprogramming in our in vitro assays. Importantly, we did not find a strict relationship between depth of reprogramming and functional recovery that would apply across cell types. This finding suggests that for therapeutic applications, partial reprogramming protocols should not be defined by exposure duration alone and must be paired with post-withdrawal assessment of recovery. Our study adds to and complements previous investigations of partial reprogramming and postwithdrawal recovery. Previous studies established that transient reprogramming can modify ageassociated features while preserving somatic identity<sup>11,13</sup> , and interrupted reprogramming allowed human fibroblasts<sup>14</sup> and multiple murine cell types<sup>16</sup> to reacquire their identity after factor withdrawal. Cross-lineage analyses of full reprogramming further identified shared late-stage features<sup>18</sup> and cell-oforigin-dependent routes to pluripotency<sup>19</sup> . These studies, however, did not systematically compare cells at different intermediate states of reprogramming and across multiple primary human lineages for their ability to recover both source-lineage identity and specialized function after OSKM withdrawal. In vivo studies in mice have established that cyclic or transient OSK(M) expression can improve tissue and organismal phenotypes<sup>11,12</sup> , including endothelium-directed OSK delivery that attenuates endothelial-tomesenchymal transition and lowers blood pressure in hypertensive mice<sup>17</sup> . However, such readouts are integrated at the level of the tissue or animal and cannot resolve how far an individual lineage has progressed or what it retains from its reprogramming once the factors are withdrawn. The two approaches are therefore complementary: organism-level studies establish whether a therapeutic benefit accrues, whereas the state-resolved human measurements reported here define the depth at which that benefit can be obtained without losing the source lineage.

Our findings challenge three assumptions implicit in how partial reprogramming is currently designed and interpreted. The first is that a given OSKM exposure leaves different cell types in a comparable state, and therefore allows comparable recovery. Under identical exposure conditions, however, the three lineages reached different states at the same time point (Figure 1D), so fixed-duration comparisons conflate time under OSKM with the state actually reached. By prospectively isolating marker-defined states ranked by progression within each lineage, we could separate intermediate-state biology from reprogramming kinetics. Even states matched by ordinal rank were not equivalent: HDF Gr2 retained a reduced DNA methylation age but recovered proliferation only partially, whereas HAEC Gr2 rapidly regained endothelial identity together with markedly improved vascular function.

A second expectation is that lineages which reprogram efficiently to iPSCs will also recover well from partial reprogramming. CTLs formed iPSCs roughly 150-fold more efficiently than HAECs, yet only the shallowest CTL state recovered, and it did not retain its DNA methylation age reduction. HAECs, the least iPSC-competent lineage, showed the most favorable combination of retained methylation-age reduction, identity recovery, and improved specialized function under the conditions tested here. Competence for full pluripotency therefore does not predict the depth from which a lineage can recover, or the function it displays afterwards.

The third expectation is that partial reprogramming resets molecular age and then lets cells return to a normal state. The fibroblast outcome deserves a closer look, because fibroblasts dominate the human partialreprogramming literature. The constraints behind the CTL and HAEC outcomes are already known ones: CD28 extinction accompanies late T-cell differentiation<sup>59</sup> , whereas endothelial function depends on angiogenic and mechanotransduction programs<sup>49-51,57</sup> . Fibroblasts, T cells and endothelial cells therefore differ not only in how deep they can be taken, but in which functions must return before recovery can be called complete.

From a practical standpoint, the three lineages point in different directions. For HDFs, no state combined a retained methylation-age reduction with full functional recovery, so dermal fibroblasts appear the least favorable of the three for partial-reprogramming-based rejuvenation. This is worth noting, because most human work to date has used this cell type. For CTLs, Gr2 and Gr3 failed to sustain expansion after OSKM suppression whereas Gr1 outgrew matched controls, which suggests that continued stimulation after withdrawal could enrich for recovering cells without prospective sorting. HAECs were the only lineage in which all three of the benefits we measured were retained together, and are the most promising of the three. Because these three lineages span mesenchymal, hematopoietic, and endothelial origins and none behaved like another, per-lineage determination is likely to be a general requirement rather than a property of these particular cells.

These results have direct implications for developing partial-reprogramming strategies. Optimization should not ask only how long OSKM should be expressed, but which state represents an acceptable stopping point for the intended cell type and application: a uniform schedule could take a sensitive lineage past its recoverable range while leaving a useful state unexploited in another. Stopping criteria should therefore be state-based, defined with lineage-appropriate live markers, and paired with postwithdrawal confirmation of factor clearance, stable identity, proliferative competence and specialized function. The deepest attainable state should not be presumed to be the best one. Likewise, factor withdrawal alone cannot establish safety, because clearance of exogenous expression does not by itself demonstrate genomic stability, absence of aberrant states, or lack of tumorigenic potential<sup>10,60</sup> . Our data do not establish clinical safety or superiority over iPSC-based approaches; rather, they define the measurements needed to evaluate whether a partial-reprogramming intervention has achieved a durable and functional somatic endpoint.

However, these studies left unanswered the question of whether different lineages rely on similar or different gene programs to progress through reprogramming. A simple expectation is that since OSKM can reprogram a variety of lineages, reprogramming must depend on at least some shared pathways between lineages.

Indeed we identified TOX as a shared reprogramming dependency. TOX emerged from the compact Pando-inferred regulatory module shared among all three lineages, was transiently induced in advanced intermediate states, and its transient expression was accompanied by the opening of _TOX_ -linked candidate cis-regulatory elements. _TOX_ depletion reduced the establishment or maintenance of reprogrammed populations and sharply impaired iPSC derivation in all three cell types without reducing early SeV-OSKM transcript abundance. Conversely, increasing _TOX_ expression in HDFs altered progression-associated pathways but did not enhance iPSC formation. These asymmetric loss- and gain-of-function phenotypes support a role for TOX as a permissive progression dependency rather than an independently sufficient reprogramming driver.

Shared TOX dependence did not produce a shared downstream program. Integrating Pando-inferred relationships with cell type-matched _TOX_ -knockdown transcriptomes yielded predominantly lineagespecific candidate genes and pathways. These programs fell into established lineage-relevant domains: ECM/stromal and developmental-guidance programs in HDFs<sup>43-46,52-54</sup> , immune-signaling and adhesion/motility programs in CTLs<sup>47,48,55,56</sup> , and vascular-homeostasis and

The corresponding CTL endpoint module was not significantly elevated, limiting inference in that lineage. These relationships remain associative rather than causal. Further experiments will be needed to determine which downstream candidates are direct targets of TOX, and which ones determine the post-withdrawal phenotypes.

Two features of TOX biology help explain why a single shared dependency can act through largely different programs in each lineage. First, TOX is an architectural HMG-box protein predicted to recognize DNA structure with relatively low sequence specificity<sup>31</sup> , so it would be expected to act on whatever regulatory landscape it encounters; consistent with this, TOX perturbation can reshape transcriptional and chromatin-accessibility programs in CD8+ T cells<sup>63</sup> . Second, because TOX is best known as a driver of CD8+ T-cell exhaustion<sup>63-66</sup> , it is worth saying explicitly that TOX induction alone should not be interpreted as evidence of exhaustion. _TOX_ expression in humans is not restricted to exhausted cells and is also found in polyfunctional effector-memory populations<sup>67</sup> . More broadly, our findings argue that partial reprogramming is a family of lineage-conditioned transitions rather than a single reversible intermediate shared across cell types. ## **Limitations of the study**

Our experiments were performed in vitro using primary cells from four middle-aged donors per cell type and therefore do not capture the full diversity of age, tissue, and multicellular contexts encountered in vivo. DNA methylation age estimates are clock-, cell type-, and culture-time-dependent and should be interpreted together with transcriptomic and functional readouts. KD support was defined directionally, by reduced expression after _TOX_ depletion rather than by a statistical threshold, so individual candidates require independent confirmation. Finally, integration of computational inference with acute TOX perturbation supports TOX-associated regulatory relationships but does not establish direct TOX binding to the nominated CREs or causal control of the associated rejuvenation and functional phenotypes. Genome-wide TOX occupancy data remain limited and their interpretation is debated, consistent with the classification of TOX within the sequence-independent HMG-box subfamily<sup>31,70</sup> , so the cisregulatory relationships reported here should be treated as candidate rather than validated targets.

## **Human primary cell culture**

## **Pando GRN inference**

Gene regulatory networks were inferred independently for the CTL, HDF, and HAEC single-nucleus multiome datasets using Pando v1.1.1<sup>30</sup> . The analyses used the quality-controlled nuclei defined above. For each dataset, RNA and chromatin-accessibility counts were extracted from the corresponding RNAintegrated ArchR project using the GeneExpressionMatrix and PeakMatrix and converted into matched Seurat/Signac objects. Peaks detected in at least one cell were retained in a Signac ChromatinAssay, and gene annotations were obtained from EnsDb.Hsapiens.v86 with UCSC-style hg38 coordinates. Pando objects were initialized from the RNA and peak assays using the phastConsElements20Mammals.UCSC.hg38 conserved regions supplied with Pando. Candidate regulatory regions were defined by intersecting conserved elements with accessible peaks and excluding exonic sequence. Motif scanning was performed using the extended motif resource distributed with Pando v1.1.1. This resource contains 1,590 position-frequency matrices, including 1,024 derived from JASPAR 2020<sup>41</sup> and 566 from CIS-BP<sup>89</sup> , together with sequence-similarity-based motif-to-TF assignments. The Pando-provided motifs and motif2tf resources were supplied to find_motifs() to scan candidate regulatory regions against the hg38 genome (BSgenome.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38). RNA counts were log-normalized in Seurat with a scale factor of 10,000, and 2,000 variable genes were selected independently for each dataset using FindVariableFeatures(selection.method = "vst"); no genes were force-added.

Pando infer_grn() was run with the dataset-specific variable genes, peak_to_gene_method = "Signac", and parallel = TRUE. Unspecified arguments used Pando v1.1.1 defaults, including Gaussian generalized linear models, candidate regions overlapping each annotated gene body or its strand-aware 100-kb upstream extension, an absolute TF-target expression-correlation threshold of 0.1, a peak-target accessibility-correlation threshold of 0, unscaled model inputs, and FDR adjustment of coefficient _p_ values. Each modeled coefficient represented an interaction between accessibility of a motif-containing candidate region and expression of the corresponding motif-matched TF in relation to target-gene expression.

During inference, OpenMP and BLAS threads were restricted to one before registering a four-worker PSOCK cluster for Pando, to avoid CPU oversubscription while retaining independent worker processes. After inference, complete coefficient and model-fit tables were retained. Region-level associations with FDR-adjusted _p_ < 0.05 were considered supported and were classified as positive or negative according to the sign of the coefficient estimate. Pando modules were identified using find_modules(p_thresh = 0.05, rsq_thresh = 0.05, nvar_thresh = 2, min_genes_per_module = 1).

## **Pando TF-regulatory region-target gene analysis**

We next extended the cross-cell-type comparison from TF-to-TF relationships to all supported positive TF-to-target relationships, including non-TF target genes. Because multiple regulatory regions could support the same TF-target relationship, region-level records were collapsed to one directed TF-to-target edge for gene-level comparison. A shared gene-level edge required the same regulator TF, the same target gene, and the same edge direction in multiple cell types. Each edge in the union network was classified as detected in one, two, or all three cell types, from which pairwise and three-cell-type intersections were obtained.

We then asked whether shared TF-to-target relationships were supported by the same genomic regulatory regions across cell types. Because overlapping Pando regions could have slightly different coordinates, regions from all three cell types were merged into a common set of non-overlapping consensus intervals using GenomicRanges::reduce(). Each original Pando region was assigned to the consensus interval that it overlapped. A regulatory-region-level association was considered shared only when the same TF, the same target gene, and the same consensus interval were detected in multiple cell types. This criterion was more stringent than gene-level sharing, which required only the same directed TF-to-target relationship.

Results were visualized as TF-to-target graphs and as three-layer TF-consensus-region-target graphs. The two levels of sharing were therefore reported separately: a TF-to-target relationship detected in all three cell types was classified as gene-level shared, whereas it was classified as regulatory-region-level shared only if at least one matching consensus interval also supported that relationship across the same cell types. Consensus intervals were computationally defined and were not interpreted as experimentally validated enhancers.

## **TOX-centered target and CRM analysis**

For _TOX_ -focused analysis of the Pando GRNs, positive _TOX_ associations (FDR-adjusted _p_ < 0.05 and estimate > 0) were further evaluated for cluster-level RNA enrichment with Seurat FindMarkers() using dataset-specific Leiden cluster assignments. Each selected Leiden cluster was compared with the remaining selected clusters, and the tested features were restricted to positive Pando-supported _TOX_ targets. Thus, FindMarkers() was not used to discover _TOX_ targets de novo from the transcriptome, but to determine whether Pando-supported targets showed higher RNA expression in a selected cluster relative to the remaining selected clusters. The default Wilcoxon rank-sum test was run with only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.05, and logfc.threshold = 0.1. The cluster-level output retained the Leiden cluster identity and RNA fold-change information and was joined to the Pando-supported target list. Genes retained by this expression filter in at least one tested cluster were termed cluster-supported _TOX_ candidate targets. A gene could occur in multiple rows when enriched in multiple clusters; these records were collapsed by gene identity before cross-dataset comparison. Sharing classes were then defined by whether each unique gene occurred in one, two, or all three candidate tables and were calculated separately from sharing of the underlying CRMs.

The _TOX_ -centered tripartite network was generated by restricting positive Pando TF-region-target associations to the cluster-supported _TOX_ candidate genes and then mapping the regulatory regions to consensus CRMs. _TOX_ was displayed as the focal TF. Other TFs were included as potential coregulators when a positive Pando TF-region-target association involving the same candidate gene was mapped to a consensus CRM. The three layers represented TFs, computationally defined consensus CRMs, and candidate target genes. Each consensus CRM was generated by merging overlapping Pando regulatory regions across the three datasets and served as a common genomic unit for comparing TFCRM-target connections across cell types. Pando mapped three JASPAR motifs to _TOX_ by sequence similarity (MA0077.1, MA0442.2, and MA0523.1). A consensus CRM was considered _TOX_ -associated when at least one underlying Pando region contained one of these _TOX_ -mapped motifs and the corresponding _TOX_ -region-target coefficient was positive with FDR-adjusted _p_ < 0.05. Target-node classes were determined solely by whether a gene occurred in one, two, or all three of the CTL, HDF, and HAEC candidate tables. For each TF-consensus-CRM-target connection, the edge table recorded the number of cell types supporting the same TF, target gene, and consensus CRM. In the final visualization, all CRM nodes and edges were displayed uniformly in gray; target-node color therefore represented candidate-gene sharing rather than CRM sharing.

To identify KD-supported TOX downstream candidate genes for Figure 7B, the cluster-supported TOX candidate targets defined above were intersected with the corresponding cell type-matched bulk RNAseq genes that decreased after _TOX_ knockdown. KD support was defined by a negative log2 fold change after _TOX_ depletion relative to the matched control, without applying an additional FDR threshold. The resulting gene lists therefore required Pando-supported TOX regulatory association, cluster-level RNA enrichment, and concordant reduction after _TOX_ depletion. Unique genes passing this combined filter were used for the HDF, CTL, and HAEC Venn analysis in Figure 7B and downstream functional summaries. ## **scPrinter/seq2PRINT footprint and sequence-attribution analysis**

Single-cell chromatin accessibility profiles from HDF, CTL, and HAEC were analyzed using hg38based scPrinter/seq2PRINT models<sup>36</sup> . Processed scPrinter objects contained cell metadata, insertion profiles, aggregate accessibility tracks, and peak definitions. Cell-state or stage labels used for stagespecific low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine-tuning were inherited from the preceding ArchR-associated annotation workflow and stored in the scPrinter metadata. HDF stages were defined as d0, d7c, d7g1, d7g2, and d7g3; CTL stages as d0, d5c, d5g1, d5g2, and d5g3; and HAEC stages as d0, d7c, d7g1, and d7g2. Fragment files were matched to annotated cells during scPrinter object construction. Candidate regulatory regions were generated by all-cell scPrinter/MACS2 peak calling and converted into seq2PRINT-compatible BED files.

For each dataset, bulk scPrinter models were first trained to predict aggregate chromatin-accessibility profiles from DNA sequence using a five-fold chromosome-held-out split. Training used reversecomplement augmentation, positional jittering, convolutional and inception blocks, batch normalization, exponential moving average weights, and mixed-precision training. Fold-specific bulk checkpoints were then used to initialize the corresponding stage-specific LoRA fine-tuning models. Bulk training and LoRA fine-tuning were performed on a 48 GB NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU.

Stage-resolved sequence attribution was computed for candidate _TOX_ and _COL25A1_ regulatory loci using the fine-tuned LoRA models. Candidate peaks and visualization windows were selected from peak-to-gene links assigned to the corresponding target genes and then prioritized for focused visualization based on concordant stage-associated attribution dynamics across HDF, CTL, and HAEC. For _TOX_ , the full gene locus together with flanking intergenic regions was inspected, and seven regulatory windows with reproducible stage-associated attribution patterns were retained for visualization. Hypothetical sequence-attribution scores were calculated for each fold and stage, foldlevel profiles were averaged, and dynamic seqlet regions were visualized as sequence-logo tracks. Motif annotations for _TOX_ plots were obtained from the UCSC-hosted JASPAR 2026 track, with labels prioritized for hits overlapping highlighted seqlet regions and redundant labels suppressed. For _COL25A1_ , two representative peak-to-gene-linked peaks with concordant attribution dynamics across HDF, CTL, and HAEC were selected for visualization. LoRA attribution profiles were plotted for these regions, and motif matches were annotated using a custom FIMO scan restricted to the three Pando-assigned TOX-associated motif classes: HMG/SOX9-like, HMG/SOX10-like, and TCF/TCF7L2like. Final highlight windows and motif labels were assigned by integrating exact FIMO hit positions with dynamic scPrinter seqlet patterns.
