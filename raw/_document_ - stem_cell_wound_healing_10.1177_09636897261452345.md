**Stem cell and extracellular vesicle therapies for wound healing: An umbrella review of predominantly diabetic and preclinical evidence**

## **Abstract** 

Chronic and complex skin wounds, including diabetic ulcers and burns, represent a major clinical challenge with substantial morbidity and healthcare costs. Regenerative approaches have evolved from stem cell–based therapies toward stem cell– free strategies, particularly mesenchymal stroma/stem cell–derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs). However, the relative strength, consistency, and translational readiness of these approaches remain unclear. The aim of this study is to synthesize and critically appraise evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses on cell-based and cell-free regenerative therapies for wound healing, with emphasis on clinical relevance, methodological quality, and recent developments. An umbrella review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidance. PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for systematic reviews and meta-analyses published in English within the last 5 years. Eligible reviews evaluated cell-based therapies and/or extracellular vesicle–based interventions for skin wound healing in clinical and/or animal models. Methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR-2, and overlap of primary studies was evaluated using the corrected covered area (CCA). Seven systematic reviews and meta-analyses were included, encompassing both clinical and preclinical evidence. Notably, the majority of included evidence was derived from diabetic wound models, particularly in preclinical animal studies. Cell-based therapies demonstrated clinically relevant benefits in specific settings, such as autologous skin cell suspensions for burns and biologic skin substitutes for diabetic foot ulcers, although certainty of evidence was low to moderate. In contrast, MSC-EVs showed large and consistent effects on wound closure, angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and inflammatory modulation in preclinical models. No randomized human trials of EV-based wound therapies were identified. Current evidence suggests a paradigm shift toward stem cell–free regenerative strategies in wound healing. While cell therapies have limited but established clinical roles, MSC-EVs offer strong preclinical efficacy and translational potential. Standardization, regulatory alignment, and well-designed clinical trials are required to enable safe clinical implementation.

## **Introduction** 

Skin wound healing is a complex, tightly regulated bio­ logical process involving hemostasis, inflammation, pro­ liferation, and tissue remodeling[1] . Recent advances have highlighted the roles of immune–stromal interactions, angio­ genic signaling, and extracellular matrix remodeling in coor­ dinating the wound­healing cascade[2,3] . While acute wounds generally progress through these stages efficiently, chronic 

and complicated wounds—such as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), pressure ulcers, ischemic wounds, and extensive burns—often fail to heal, representing a major global health­ care burden[4] . These wounds are associated with prolonged hospitalization, infection, limb amputation, reduced quality of life, and substantial economic costs, particularly in aging and metabolically compromised populations[5] .

Consequently, regenera­ tive medicine approaches have emerged as promising adjuncts aimed at actively stimulating tissue repair rather than merely supporting passive healing[7] . Over the past two decades, cell­ based therapies, particularly those involving mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) and autologous skin cells, have been extensively investigated for their ability to enhance angiogenesis, modulate inflammation, and promote re­epi­ thelialization[8] . MSCs exert therapeutic effects through immu­ nomodulation, secretion of trophic factors, and promotion of angiogenesis rather than long­term engraftment[9,10] . 

Early clinical and preclinical studies suggested that trans­ planted stem cells could engraft within damaged tissue and differentiate into skin­resident cell types[11] . However, accumu­ lating evidence has challenged this paradigm, demonstrating that long­term cell engraftment is rare, and that most therapeu­ tic benefits arise from paracrine signaling mechanisms rather than direct tissue replacement[12] . This shift toward a paracrine mechanism has led to increasing interest in cell­free therapeu­ tic strategies[13,14] . This realization has fundamentally reshaped the field and catalyzed interest in stem cell–derived secre­ tomes, particularly extracellular vesicles (EVs)[15] . 

EVs—including small EVs (sEVs), commonly referred to as exosomes—are membrane­bound nanoparticles released by cells that transport bioactive cargo such as microRNAs, mRNAs, proteins, and lipids[16] . EVs are now recognized as key mediators of intercellular communication, capable of transferring functional RNA, proteins, and lipids to target cells[17,18] . MSC­ EVs have been shown to recapitulate many of the regenerative effects of parent cells by modulating inflammation, stimulating angiogenesis, enhancing fibro­ blast and keratinocyte activity, and promoting extracellular matrix remodeling[19] . Preclinical studies consistently demon­ strate that MSC­EVs enhance wound healing through angio­ genesis, fibroblast activation, and immunomodulation[20,21] . Importantly, EV­based therapies offer several theoretical advantages over live cell transplantation, including lower immunogenicity, absence of tumorigenic risk, improved 

storage stability, and greater potential for standardization and large­scale manufacturing[22] . Standardization of EV research is guided by the MISEV (Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles) framework[23,24] . 

Recently, research on EV­based wound therapies has expanded rapidly, with numerous preclinical studies and meta­analyses reporting robust improvements in wound clo­ sure, angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and scar quality across diabetic, ischemic, burn, and excisional wound mod­ els[25] . Parallel advances have also occurred in engineering strategies, such as hypoxia­preconditioned EVs, miRNA­ enriched vesicles, and EV­loaded biomaterial scaffolds, further enhancing therapeutic efficacy[26] . Emerging strategies such as cargo loading and genetic modification further enhance EV therapeutic efficacy[27,28] . Despite these promis­ ing developments, clinical translation remains limited, and no EV­based wound therapy has yet achieved routine clinical use. Despite promising preclinical results, translation into clinical applications remains limited due to regulatory and standardization challenges[29,30] . 

At the same time, clinical evidence for cell­based therapies remains heterogeneous[31] . While autologous skin cell suspen­ sions (ASCS) and biologic skin substitutes have demonstrated benefits in selected patient populations—particularly burns and DFUs—the certainty of evidence is often low, and out­ comes vary widely depending on cell source, delivery method, and wound type[32] . Moreover, practical challenges related to cost, logistics, donor variability, and regulatory oversight continue to limit broader implementation[33] . 

Given the rapid evolution of the field and the growing volume of secondary evidence, there is a clear need for a high­level synthesis that critically evaluates and contex­ tualizes both cell­based and stem cell­free regenerative strategies for wound healing. Umbrella reviews, which sys­ tematically assess and integrate findings from multiple sys­ tematic reviews and meta­analyses, are particularly well suited to address this need by identifying consistent signals of efficacy, methodological limitations, evidence overlap, and translational gaps[34] .

## **Methods** 

## _Protocol registration_ 

This umbrella review was designed and conducted in accor­ dance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta­Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement and methodological guidance for umbrella reviews. A formal protocol was prepared _a priori_ to define the objectives, eligi­ bility criteria, search strategy, and methods for study selec­ tion, data extraction, and quality appraisal. 

Although prospective registration of umbrella reviews is not mandatory, registration in the Open Science Framework (OSF; Registration DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/K6HVS; Associated proj­ ect: https://osf.io/aswnr) is recommended to enhance transpar­ ency and reduce risk of selective reporting. The protocol for this review was therefore documented prior to study selection and followed throughout the review process. The full protocol, detailed search strategies, data­extraction forms, AMSTAR­2 assessments, and overlap matrices are provided as Supplemental Materials. 

## _Eligibility criteria_ 

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework and were applied at the level of system­ atic reviews and meta­analyses. 

We included systematic reviews with meta­analysis, including network meta­analyses, that synthesized evidence 

on cell­based or exosome­based therapies for wound healing. Narrative reviews, scoping reviews, editorials, commentar­ ies, primary experimental studies, and clinical guidelines were excluded. 

Eligible reviews included studies conducted in human participants and/or animal models. Both clinical evidence (randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and comparative clini­ cal studies) and preclinical evidence (controlled animal experiments) were considered. Reviews focusing exclusively on _in vitro_ studies without _in vivo_ outcomes were excluded. 

Reviews evaluating cell­based therapies and/or exosome­ based or EV therapies, including sEVs derived from MSCs, were eligible. 

Eligible reviews were required to report at least one wound­healing–related outcome, including but not limited to wound closure or healing rate, time to re­epithelialization, angiogenesis or vascular density, collagen deposition or tis­ sue remodeling, inflammatory or immunomodulatory mark­ ers, and/or safety and adverse events. Reviews focusing on unrelated outcomes were excluded. 

## _Search strategy and information sources_ 

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science using key­ words applied to title, abstract, and keyword fields to maxi­ mize sensitivity (Table 1). This approach was adopted to ensure inclusion of all relevant systematic reviews and meta­ analyses. Searches were limited to English­language publica­ tions. No restrictions were applied regarding year of publication. The final search results were exported to refer­ ence­management software, and duplicate records were removed prior to screening. Study selection was subsequently performed in two stages (title/abstract screening and full­text assessment) in accordance with predefined eligibility criteria. 

## _Study-selection process_

## _Statistical analysis and software_ 

## _Data extraction_ 

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized, pilot­tested extraction form. For each included systematic review or meta­analysis, the following information was extracted: first author and year of publication, type of review, number and design of included primary studies, population or animal model, inter­ vention type, cell or EV source, wound type, main outcomes assessed, and key conclusions. 

In addition, information required for methodological appraisal and overlap analysis was extracted, including details relevant to risk­of­bias assessment, reporting quality, and lists of primary studies included in each meta­analysis. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by consensus. 

## _Methodological quality assessment_ 

The methodological quality of the included systematic reviews and meta­analyses was assessed using the A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR­2) instrument, which is mandatory and recom­ mended for umbrella reviews. AMSTAR­2 evaluates 16 domains related to protocol registration, literature search comprehensiveness, study selection and data­extraction procedures, risk­of­bias assessment, appropriateness of meta­analytic methods, and transparency of reporting. Each review was independently assessed by two reviewers, with judgments categorized as Yes, Partial Yes, or No for each AMSTAR­2 domain. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. 

## _Assessment of evidence strength_ 

To assess the degree of redundancy and potential inflation of evidence across the included meta­analyses, the overlap of primary studies was evaluated using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) method. For this purpose, a citation matrix was constructed in which each row represented a primary study, and each column represented an included meta­analysis. The CCA was calculated based on the total number of primary study occurrences, the number of unique primary studies, and the number of meta­analyses included. The magnitude of overlap was interpreted according to established thresholds 

All data management, synthesis, and visualization were per­ formed using R statistical software (R version ≥4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within the RStudio integrated development environment. Descriptive analyses and data manipulation were conducted using the tidyverse suite of packages. 

Methodological quality assessment data (AMSTAR­2) were processed and visualized using ggplot2, with heat­ map construction facilitated by tidyr, dplyr, and forcats. Overlap analysis of primary studies across meta­analyses, including construction of citation matrices and visualiza­ tion of overlap patterns, was conducted in R. The CCA was calculated based on established formulas using custom R scripts, and overlap patterns were graphically represented using heatmaps. 

## **Results** 

## _Search results and study characteristics_ 

The systematic literature search identified 30 records through database searching, including Scopus (n = 14), Web of Science (n = 8), and PubMed (n = 8) (Figure 1). After the removal of 16 duplicate records, 14 unique records remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, five records were excluded during the screening stage for not meeting the inclusion criteria. 

A total of nine reports were sought for retrieval, of which two reports could not be retrieved. Consequently, seven full­ text reports were assessed for eligibility. No reports were excluded at the eligibility stage due to unrelated outcomes, resulting in seven systematic reviews and meta­analyses being included in the final synthesis and umbrella review. 

The included reviews were published between 2019 and 2025, reflecting the publication range of eligible reviews identified without date restrictions. Five reviews synthesized data exclusively from animal models, one review focused on RCTs, and one review included RCTs and comparative stud­ ies in patients with DFUs. The wound types addressed across the included reviews were diverse and included full­thick­ ness excisional wounds, diabetic cutaneous ulcers, burns, pressure ulcers, donor­site wounds, and infected wounds.

## _Methodological quality of included reviews_ 

The methodological quality of the included systematic reviews and meta­analyses was assessed using the AMSTAR­2 instrument, which evaluates 16 key domains related to protocol rigor, transparency, risk­of­bias han­ dling, and interpretability. The item­level judgments for each review are summarized visually in Figure 2. 

Overall, the included reviews demonstrated generally acceptable methodological quality, with strong performance across several core domains. All reviews clearly formulated their research questions using defined PICO components, applied comprehensive literature search strategies, con­ ducted study selection and data extraction in duplicate, and provided adequate descriptions of included studies. In addi­ tion, all reviews assessed the risk of bias of the primary stud­ ies and disclosed conflicts of interest. 

However, recurrent methodological limitations were identified across multiple reviews. The most frequent weakness concerned the reporting of funding sources of the primary studies, which was absent in several reviews.

## _Evidence from cell-therapy reviews_ 

This umbrella review included four cell­therapy–focused evi­ dence sources: (1) ASCS (RECELL®) for burns and donor­ site wounds (RCTs), (2) biologic skin substitutes for DFUs (clinical trials), (3) MSC therapy in diabetic skin ulcers (ani­ mal network meta­analysis), and (4) stem cells combined with photobiomodulation (preclinical meta­analysis)[35,36,38,39] . 

_Cell sources and delivery strategies._ Across clinical and pre­ clinical reviews, the therapeutic “cell” component varied by setting. ASCS were used in acute partial­thickness burns and split­thickness donor sites; ASCS were prepared using com­ mercial harvesting devices or laboratory methods[35] . Cellular skin substitutes encompassed constructs containing living cells and related biologic products used as adjuncts for DFU care[36] . In animal diabetic wound models, MSCs derived from multiple tissues were compared, including ADSCs, 

BM­MSCs, UC­MSCs, and AMSCs[38] . In photobiomodula­ tion­adjunct studies, included experiments largely used ADSCs[39] . 

Delivery was predominantly local in both clinical and preclinical evidence. In ASCS trials, cells were delivered topically to the wound bed as a suspension (spray/droplet application), reflecting a localized strategy for epithelial resurfacing[35] . In photobiomodulation + stem cell animal studies, stem cells were applied directly to the injury site, most commonly intradermally, and less commonly intramus­ cularly, indicating primarily local delivery[39] . For biologic skin substitutes in DFU, the intervention is inherently local (applied to the ulcer as an adjunct dressing/substitute)[36] . Systemic delivery was not a dominant theme in these four reviews; however, the DFU literature includes various adjunctive approaches, and the biologic products are applied locally in routine practice[36] . 

_Clinical outcomes in wound healing._ In DFUs, biologic skin substitutes improved the proportion of ulcers achieving com­ plete closure. Pooled estimates showed ulcers treated with biologic dressings were more likely to heal by 12 weeks risk ratio (RR 1.67, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.48–1.90) and 6 weeks (RR 2.81, 95% CI 1.65–4.77) than standard­of­care dressings[36] . 

In ASCS trials, the primary outcome was time to re­epithe­ lialization. Meta­analysis indicated time to re­epithelialization was shorter in pediatric burn wounds (SMD −1.75, 95% CI −3.45 to −0.05) and adult donor­site wounds Standardized mean difference (SMD −5.71, 95% CI −10.61 to −0.81) treated with ASCS, whereas time to re­epithelialization in adult burn wounds was not estimable due to inconsistency[35] . Direct clini­ cal angiogenesis/perfusion endpoints were not a main focus of the ASCS review (clinical endpoints were time to re­epithelial­ ization, pain, infection, and scarring measures)[35] . 

In contrast, preclinical reviews explicitly assessed wound­ healing biology. The MSC network meta­analysis in diabetic

_Safety and adverse events._ Safety reporting varied markedly by intervention type. In the ASCS review, adverse events included infection outcomes and “requirement for further surgical management.” Effect estimates were imprecise, and some analyses suggested possible increased odds of surgical wound infection in certain subgroups, while other outcomes favored ASCS but with wide confidence intervals and very low certainty[35] . In the DFU biologic skin substitutes review, the authors explicitly noted that the study did not focus on adverse events and therefore could not draw conclusions on long­term or post­closure adverse outcomes[36] . Overall, across the cell­therapy evidence base in this umbrella review, safety conclusions are limited by incomplete reporting and small trial sizes or lack of explicit safety synthesis[35,36] . 

_Strength and consistency of evidence._ The ASCS evidence was based on five trials (n = 347), and the review concluded that certainty of evidence for key outcomes was very low, driven by high/unclear risk of bias, imprecision, and suspected pub­ lication bias/industry funding[35] . For DFU biologic skin sub­ stitutes, the pooled effect for complete closure by 12 weeks was robust and statistically significant; however, the review highlighted heterogeneity across products and limitations in comparative evidence between substitutes[36] . 

The MSC network meta­analysis in diabetic animal models found no statistically significant differences between MSC sources on short­term wound­healing rates, although probabilistic ranking suggested AMSCs may be most effec­ tive[38] . The photobiomodulation + stem cell meta­analysis detected significant improvements but also reported moder­ ate­to­high heterogeneity and important protocol variability, limiting certainty and direct translation[39] . 

## _Evidence from exosome-based/EV therapies_ 

_Exosome sources and engineering approaches._ Across the included reviews, MSC­EVs—predominantly sEVs—were the most extensively investigated cell­free therapeutic modality for wound repair. EVs were derived primarily from ADSCs, BM­MSCs, and UC­MSCs, with additional sources including AMSCs, gingival­derived MSCs, dermal­derived MSCs, and decidua­derived MSCs. 

Zhu et al.[41] highlighted that while most studies used the term exosomes, the majority of EV preparations did not 

confirm endosomal biogenesis, supporting the recommen­ dation to use the broader sEV terminology in accordance with MISEV2023 guidelines. In addition to conventional sEVs, a smaller subset of studies evaluated apoptotic EVs (ApoEVs), including apoptotic small extracellular vesicles (ApoSEVs) and apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs), which were directly compared with sEVs in subgroup analyses. Engineering and optimization strategies were common and included preconditioning of parent MSCs, genetic modifi­ cation of MSCs to enrich EV cargo, direct EV loading with therapeutic molecules, and incorporation of EVs into bio­ material scaffolds, particularly hydrogels. Despite promis­ ing results, the reviews consistently emphasized substantial heterogeneity in EV isolation methods, dosing units (pro­ tein mass vs particle number), administration frequency, and storage conditions, underscoring the lack of method­ ological standardization. 

_Preclinical and clinical outcomes._ Evidence for diabetic wound healing was robust at the preclinical level. Yue et al.[40] synthe­ sized 21 animal studies focusing exclusively on type II diabetic wound models and demonstrated that MSC­sEVs significantly accelerated wound closure compared with controls (SMD ≈ 3.16, with low­to­moderate heterogeneity). Secondary out­ comes consistently favored MSC­sEVs, including enhanced angiogenesis, improved re­epithelialization, increased collagen deposition, and reduced pro­inflammatory cytokines. 

Similarly, Zhu et al.[41] reported strong pooled effects of MSC­EVs on wound closure, collagen deposition, vascular density, and scar reduction in both diabetic and non­diabetic animal models. Subgroup analyses suggested that ADSC­ derived EVs performed particularly well for wound closure and collagen deposition, whereas BM­MSC­EVs showed relatively stronger effects on revascularization. 

While chronic wound models were less frequently iso­ lated as a distinct category, diabetic ulcers constituted the majority of chronic wound models in all EV­focused reviews. Across these models, EV­based therapies consistently out­ performed placebo or vehicle controls, particularly when administered via local subcutaneous or intradermal injection or when incorporated into bioactive scaffolds. 

Burn and surgically induced wound models were less rep­ resented than excisional diabetic wounds but showed con­ cordant findings. Zhu et al.[41] included burn and full­thickness excisional wound studies demonstrating accelerated wound closure, improved dermal regeneration, and reduced scar width following MSC­EV administration. However, the number of studies in these categories was limited, and effect estimates were associated with higher uncertainty. Notably, no RCTs of EV­based wound therapies were included in any of the reviewed meta­analyses, confirming that current evi­ dence remains entirely preclinical. 

_Mechanisms of action._ Mechanistic analyses across the reviews converged on several interconnected pathways.

MSC­EVs deliver bioactive miRNAs (miR­21, miR­126, miR­146a) that regulate angiogenesis, fibroblast migration, keratinocyte proliferation, and extracellular matrix remodel­ ing. Genetic enrichment of specific miRNAs within EVs often amplified therapeutic effects. EV treatment was consis­ tently associated with suppression of excessive inflamma­ tion, characterized by decreased IL­6 and increased IL­10 expression, facilitating a favorable wound microenviron­ ment. MSC­EVs promoted fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, endothelial cell migration, and neovascularization through activation of pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3’ ­kinase/Protein Kinase B (PI3K/Akt), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming growth factor­beta (TGF­β) signaling cascades. Collectively, these mechanisms support the concept that EVs act primarily via paracrine sig­ naling, recapitulating many of the regenerative effects of parent MSCs without direct cell engraftment[37,40,41] . 

_Quality and certainty of evidence._ Across all EV­based reviews, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate to low, pri­ marily due to substantial methodological hetero geneity; inconsistent reporting of EV isolation, characterization, and dosing; limited adherence to MISEV and International Soci­ ety for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT) recommendations; pre­ dominant reliance on small animal models; and unclear or high risk of bias across multiple SYRCLE domains[37,40,41] . Despite large pooled effect sizes favoring MSC­EV therapies, the reviews uniformly cautioned against premature clinical translation. The lack of standardized EV production and qual­ ity­control protocols, together with the absence of human clinical trials, was consistently identified as a major barrier to regulatory approval and clinical implementation[37,41] . 

## _Comparative synthesis_ 

_Cell therapy vs exosome-based therapy._ Both cell­based and exosome­based strategies demonstrated beneficial effects on wound­healing outcomes, but the strength and consistency of evidence differed markedly between approaches. Cell ther­ apy showed clinically relevant benefits in specific contexts. ASCS were associated with a reduction in time to re­epithe­ lialization in pediatric burns and adult donor­site wounds; however, the certainty of evidence was very low, largely due to small sample sizes, imprecision, and methodological limi­ tations of the included trials[35] . In contrast, biologic skin sub­ stitutes significantly improved complete wound closure in patients with DFUs at both 6 and 12 weeks, with pooled risk ratios consistently favoring treatment over standard care[36] . Nevertheless, substantial heterogeneity across products, variability in study design, and a lack of robust head­to­head comparisons limited firm conclusions regarding the compar­ ative efficacy of individual cellular products[36] . 

In contrast, exosome­based therapies demonstrated large and consistent effect sizes in preclinical models, particularly in 

diabetic wound settings. Meta­analyses of MSC­derived sEVs showed significant improvements in wound closure, angio­ genesis, collagen deposition, re­epithelialization, and scar­ related outcomes across multiple animal models[37,40,41] . Subgroup analyses further suggested that EV source, adminis­ tration route, and EV subtype influenced the magnitude of therapeutic benefit[40,41] . Despite these robust preclinical sig­ nals, no human clinical trials of EV­based wound therapies were included in the reviewed meta­analyses[37,40,41] . Overall, while cell therapy currently provides direct but limited clinical evidence, exosome­based therapy offers stronger biological efficacy signals, albeit confined to preclinical research[35,36,40,41] . 

Safety profiles differed in both depth and certainty of reporting. Cell­therapy reviews generally reported accept­ able safety; however, adverse event reporting was inconsis­ tent. In ASCS trials, infection and the need for additional surgical intervention were variably reported, with some analyses suggesting a possible increase in infection risk in adult burn wounds, although estimates were imprecise and based on very low­certainty evidence[35] . In DFU studies using biologic skin substitutes, safety outcomes were not a primary focus, and long­term adverse effects could not be adequately evaluated[36] . 

Exosome­based therapies exhibited a favorable safety profile in animal studies, with no serious treatment­related adverse events reported across EV­focused reviews[37,40,41] . Importantly, EV therapies avoid risks inherent to live cell transplantation, such as uncontrolled differentiation, tumori­ genicity, or long­term engraftment; nevertheless, the absence of clinical trials and long­term follow­up precludes defini­ tive safety conclusions in humans[37,41] . 

Cell­based approaches are already partially integrated into clinical practice, benefiting from established regulatory pathways. However, their broader adoption is constrained by donor variability, limited cell survival, storage and trans­ port requirements, cost, scalability, and inconsistent clinical efficacy[35,36] . 

By contrast, exosome­based therapies offer several theo­ retical translational advantages, including improved storage stability, lower immunogenicity, and potential for large­scale manufacturing. Moreover, EVs can be engineered, precondi­ tioned, or incorporated into biomaterial scaffolds, enhancing therapeutic flexibility. Nonetheless, the included reviews consistently emphasized substantial heterogeneity in EV iso­ lation, characterization, dosing, and nomenclature, along with incomplete adherence to MISEV and ISCT guidelines, representing major barriers to regulatory approval and clini­ cal translation[37,40,41] . 

Across included meta­analyses, effect sizes for EV­based therapies were consistently large (SMD >2 in several analy­ ses), particularly in diabetic wound models, whereas clinical cell­based therapies demonstrated moderate effect sizes (RR ~1.5–2.8), indicating a divergence between preclinical effi­ cacy and clinical effectiveness.

_Clinical vs preclinical evidence gaps._ A prominent finding of this umbrella review is the mismatch between the maturity of preclinical and clinical evidence. Cell­therapy research has progressed further along the translational pipeline, with RCTs and meta­analyses available for specific indications such as burns, donor­site wounds, and DFUs[35,36] . However, these clinical studies are frequently underpowered, heteroge­ neous, and restricted to narrow wound types, resulting in low or very low certainty of evidence for many clinically relevant outcomes[35,36] . 

In contrast, exosome­based therapies are supported by a large and rapidly expanding preclinical evidence base, demonstrating reproducible improvements across multiple wound­healing endpoints, including wound closure, angio­ genesis, collagen deposition, and re­epithelialization[37,40,41] . Nevertheless, the complete absence of RCTs represents a critical translational gap. Moreover, substantial variability in animal models, outcome timing, intervention protocols, and reporting quality reduces confidence in direct extrapolation of these findings to clinical practice[37,40,41] . 

Collectively, these findings indicate that cell therapy cur­ rently holds stronger clinical plausibility, whereas exosome­ based therapy exhibits superior preclinical consistency and mechanistic clarity. Bridging this gap will require rigorously designed, standardized, and adequately powered clinical tri­ als of EV­based therapies, alongside direct comparative studies evaluating cell­based interventions versus their sec­ retome­derived products[37,41] . 

## _Overlap of primary studies across included meta-analyses_ 

The overall CCA was 4.43%, indicating a slight degree of overlap among the included meta­analyses. The degree of overlap among primary studies included in the systematic reviews and meta­analyses was examined to evaluate redun­ dancy and potential inflation of evidence within this umbrella review. Figure 3 presents a matrix­based overlap heatmap depicting the distribution of individual primary studies across the included meta­analyses, with rows representing primary studies (author, year) and columns representing meta­analyses (author, year). Study type is visually distin­ guished, allowing simultaneous assessment of overlap pat­ terns and evidence origin (clinical vs preclinical). 

Overall, the heatmap demonstrates limited overlap across most meta­analyses, indicating that the majority of reviews synthesized largely distinct sets of primary studies. This pat­ tern was particularly evident between clinical meta­analyses and preclinical meta­analyses focusing on MSC­EVs, where minimal cross­inclusion of studies was observed. 

Within preclinical EV­focused meta­analyses, some degree of overlap was apparent, reflecting the shared reli­ ance on frequently cited animal models and foundational experimental studies in the field. However, this overlap was generally confined to a limited subset of studies and 

did not dominate the evidence base. In contrast, clinical meta­analyses showed negligible overlap with each other, consistent with their focus on different interventions, wound types, and patient populations. 

Importantly, the visual distribution of study types con­ firms a clear segregation between clinical and preclinical evidence, with clinical studies clustering within specific meta­analyses and preclinical studies predominantly con­ tributing to EV­based reviews. This finding supports the interpretation that conclusions drawn in this umbrella review are not driven by excessive duplication of primary data but rather reflect complementary bodies of evidence across distinct therapeutic approaches. 

## **Discussion** 

## _Principal findings_ 

This umbrella review provides a contemporary synthesis of regenerative strategies for skin wound healing, spanning both cell­based therapies and cell­free approaches, particu­ larly MSC­EVs. By aggregating evidence from seven sys­ tematic reviews and meta­analyses, published between 2019 and 2025, and contextualizing these findings with recent advances, several key conclusions emerge. 

First, cell­based therapies—including ASCS, cellular skin substitutes, and MSC transplantation—have demonstrated clinically meaningful benefits in selected indications, such as partial­thickness burns and DFUs. However, the certainty of clinical evidence remains low to moderate, largely due to small trial sizes, heterogeneity in intervention protocols, and inconsistent reporting of safety outcomes[35,36] . 

Second, exosome­based and EV therapies show robust and reproducible efficacy across multiple preclinical models, particularly in diabetic and chronic wounds. Meta­analyses published since 2022 consistently report large effect sizes favoring MSC­EVs for wound closure, angiogenesis, colla­ gen deposition, and inflammatory modulation[37,40,41] . These findings reinforce the concept that the regenerative effects traditionally attributed to transplanted stem cells are largely mediated through paracrine mechanisms rather than direct engraftment. Collectively, the evidence indicates a paradigm shift in regenerative wound healing—from reliance on live cell transplantation toward cell­free, EV­based therapeutics, which may offer improved safety, scalability, and regulatory feasibility. Although direct pooling across meta­analyses was not feasible, comparison of reported effect sizes sug­ gests stronger and more consistent effects in preclinical EV studies compared with clinical cell­therapy studies.

## _Translational challenges_ 

Manufacturing scalability is a critical determinant of transla­ tional success. Cell therapies require stringent cell culture con­ ditions, donor screening, and cold­chain logistics, which limit widespread implementation. In contrast, EVs offer greater potential for large­scale production, cryopreservation, and off­ the­shelf availability. Nevertheless, recent reviews emphasize substantial variability in EV yield depending on MSC source, culture conditions, and isolation techniques[37,41] . 

A major obstacle to EV translation is the lack of stan­ dardization (Figure 6). Across studies published between 2021 and 2026, EV dose has been reported using heteroge­ neous metrics (protein concentration, particle number, or parent cell equivalents), and isolation methods range from ultracentrifugation to commercial precipitation kits. Limited adherence to MISEV2023 recommendations further compli­ cates cross­study comparisons and regulatory evaluation[43] . 

From a regulatory standpoint, EVs occupy an ambiguous position between biologics and advanced therapy medicinal products. Although they circumvent risks such as uncon­ trolled cell proliferation or tumorigenicity, concerns remain regarding batch­to­batch consistency, long­term safety, and biodistribution. Regulatory agencies increasingly require rigorous characterization, potency assays, and standardized 

release criteria, which are not yet uniformly implemented in preclinical wound­healing studies[41] . 

A structured heterogeneity analysis reveals substantial vari­ ability across studies, including differences in MSC sources (ADSCs, BM­MSCs, UC­MSCs), EV isolation methods (ultracentrifugation vs precipitation), dosing metrics (protein vs particle number), animal models, and outcome time points. This heterogeneity limits reproducibility and complicates translation to clinical settings. Standardization of protocols, particularly in EV characterization and dosing, is essential to improve comparability and regulatory acceptance. 

## _Research gaps and future directions_ 

A critical gap identified in this umbrella review is the absence of direct comparisons between cell­based therapies and EV­based products (Figure 7). Future trials should explicitly evaluate whether EVs can replicate or surpass the clinical benefits of parent MSCs in equivalent wound mod­ els and patient populations. Dose–response relationships remain poorly defined for both cells and EVs. Meta­analyses consistently report substantial heterogeneity, and few stud­ ies systematically evaluate optimal dosing, frequency, or timing of administration[40] . Establishing standardized dose metrics is essential for clinical translation. Emerging research from 2021 to 2026 highlights the potential of engi­ neered EVs, including miRNA­enriched vesicles, hypoxia­ preconditioned EVs, and EV­loaded hydrogels. While these approaches enhance efficacy, they also introduce additional regulatory complexity. Future research should balance biological optimization with translational feasibility and reproducibility. A major unmet need is the lack of direct head­to­head comparisons between cell­based therapies and EV­based therapies under equivalent experimental or clini­ cal conditions.

## _Strengths and limitations of this umbrella review_ 

This umbrella review has several strengths, including adher­ ence to PRISMA 2020 guidance, use of AMSTAR­2 for methodological appraisal, and formal assessment of study overlap using the CCA. By integrating both clinical and pre­ clinical evidence, it provides a comprehensive overview of the regenerative wound­healing landscape. However, lim­ itations must be acknowledged. The conclusions are con­ strained by the quality of included reviews, many of which synthesized heterogeneous and predominantly preclinical studies. In addition, the rapid evolution of EV research means that some experimental innovations published after 2025 may not yet be captured in meta­analytic form. A key limitation is that most included evidence relates to diabetic wound models, which may limit generalizability to other wound types. The limited number of eligible reviews (n = 7) highlights the early stage of evidence synthesis in this field. 

## **Conclusions** 

Current evidence supports a transition from stem cell–based to stem cell–free regenerative strategies in wound healing. While cell therapies provide limited but tangible clinical benefits in selected indications, MSC­EVs demonstrate stronger and more consistent biological efficacy in preclinical models. To realize their clinical potential, future efforts must prioritize standardiza­ tion, rigorous comparative trials, and alignment with regulatory requirements. Well­designed translational studies will be essen­ tial to determine whether EV­based therapies can safely and effectively redefine wound­management paradigms.
