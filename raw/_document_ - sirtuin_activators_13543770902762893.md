## **Sirtuin activators** 

## Francisco J Alcaín & José M Villalba[†] 

> † _Universidad de Córdoba, Departamento de Biología Celular, Fisiología e Inmunología, Spain_ 

_Background_ : Sirtuin 1 – 7 (SIRT1 – 7) are deacetylases that are dependent on NAD[+] for their activity. SIRT1 down-regulates p53 activity, increasing lifespan, cell survival, and neuroprotection; it also deacetylates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ and its coactivator 1α, promoting fat mobilization, increasing mitochondrial size and number, and positively regulating insulin secretion. Sirtuins link nutrient availability and energy metabolism. Calorie restriction, which increases lifespan and is beneficial in age-related disorders, activates sirtuin. Major efforts are thus focused to developing sirtuin activators. _Objective_ : After discussing the potential involvement of sirtuins in pathophysiological processes, this review looks at new, synthetic sirtuin activators. _Conclusions_ : To date, resveratrol is the most potent natural compound able to activate SIRT1, mimicking the positive effect of calorie restriction. Resveratrol might help in the treatment or prevention of obesity and in preventing the aging-related decline in heart function and neuronal loss. As resveratrol has low bioavailability and interacts with multiple molecular targets, the development of new molecules with better bioavailability and targeting sirtuin at lower concentrations is a promising field of the medicinal chemistry. New SIRT1 activators that are up to 1000 times more effective than resveratrol have recently been identified. These improve the response to insulin and increase the number and activity of mitochondria in obese mice. Human trials with a formulation of resveratrol with improved bioavailability and with a synthetic SIRT1 activator are in progress. 

**Keywords:** aging, metabolic diseases, resveratrol, sirtuin activators, sirtuins

## **1. Introduction** 

The seven proteins (SIRT1 – 7) that comprise the human sirtuin family are deacetylase enzymes, which depend on nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD[+] ) for their enzymatic activity. SIRT1, the orthologue of yeast Sir2, is the most extensively studied mammalian sirtuin to date. 

**==> picture [55 x 35] intentionally omitted <==**

SIRT1, SRT6, and SIRT7 are – largely – nuclear proteins  [1-3]. However, depending on the cell lines or organisms examined, a partial or temporary cytoplasmic localization of SIRT1 has been observed. For instance, SIRT1 was found in the cytoplasm of embryonic and adult neural precursor cells, but was transiently localized in the nucleus in response to differentiation stimulus  [4]. Some subsets of neurons express SIRT1 predominantly in the cytoplasm. For example, SIRT1 is an exclusively cytoplasmic protein in glucagon-positive α cells, whereas in murine embryonic cardiomyocytes it is expressed exclusively in the nucleus; in the adult heart, SIRT1 is expressed in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus  [5,6]. Nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of _Drosophila_ Sir2 during embryonic development has also been reported  [7]. Although SIRT2 is a predominantly cytoplasmic protein, it can be also located in the nucleus  [8]. SIRT3, SIRT4 and SIRT5 are mitochondrial proteins  [9].

SIRT1, SIRT2, and SIRT3 have been reported to be up-regulated by calorie restriction (CR), external cold temperature, or oxidative stress  [13-16]. SIRT1 expression in mouse liver was not regulated at the messenger RNA level in fasted mice, although SIRT1 protein levels were induced by fasting and returned to nearly control levels upon refeeding  [17]; in addition, glucagon stimulated SIRT1 levels in mouse liver  [18]. However, the general assumption that SIRT1 is up-regulated by CR has been challenged by the recent demonstration that SIRT1 regulation by CR is tissue specific, increasing SIRT1 levels in white adipose tissue, muscle, and pancreas, but decreasing them in liver when it is activated by a high-calorie diet. In the brain, SIRT1 regulation by CR is region specific  [19,20]. 

SIRT1 down-regulates the activity of the nuclear transcription factor p53  [2,21], among other targets, which has been related with an increase in lifespan and cell survival associated to stress resistance. The transcription factor p53 is also strongly implicated in neuronal apoptosis and increasing evidence suggests that activation of p53 contributes to neurodegeneration in Alzheimer’s disease (AD)  [22]. Thus, SIRT1-mediated p53 down-regulation might play a neuroprotective role in such neurodegenerative diseases as AD, Huntington’s diseases (HD), and Wallerian neurodegeneration  [11,23]. 

CR-stimulated SIRT1 activity represses the nuclear receptor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ), promoting fat mobilization in white adipocytes  [24] and controlling the gluconeogenic/glycolytic pathways. Deacetylation of the master regulator of mitochondrial peroxisome proliferator activator receptor γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α) leads to an increase in the mitochondrial size and number in animal and _in-vitro_ models  [17,25]. In liver, SIRT1 induces the gluconeogenic genes, phosphoenolpyruvate kinase, and glucose-6-phosphatase by forming a protein complex with PGC-1α and HNF4α. In addition, SIRT1 modulates the effects of PGC-1α repression of glycolytic genes, glucokinase, and pyruvate kinase  [17]. Furthermore, 6 months of caloric restriction in healthy humans caused an increase in mitochondrial number and in the expression levels of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis in skeletal muscle  [26]. An improvement in mitochondrial function is promising in the treatment of many neurodegenerative diseases because some of their common biochemical processes include mitochondrial dysfunction and an increased generation of reactive oxygen species  [23,27]. Moreover, SIRT1 increases insulin sensitivity  [28] and positively regulates insulin secretion in pancreatic β cells in rodents  [20]. In humans, 6 months of CR decreased fasting insulin levels and body temperature, which are considered to be biomarkers of longevity  [29]. These results link SIRT1 to the control of metabolic processes such as metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance. Furthermore, SIRT1 also regulates myogenic differentiation (MyoD) 

factor, inhibiting muscle gene expression and differentiation when overexpressed in muscle cells  [30]. 

In view of the many functions of sirtuins in cells, manipulation of sirtuin activities has attracted considerable attention because of its potential to address a broad range of diseases, including obesity, diabetes, cancer, inflammation, and cardiovascular, neuronal and aging-related diseases. CR increases lifespan and is beneficial in age-related disorders such as diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease in animal models  [15]. 

All the evidence suggests that the molecular mechanisms of CR are mediated by sirtuins in yeast, worms, flies, and mammals  [31-34]; CR has induced sirtuin activity in all the species studied. As yeast Sir2 and its homologous counterparts in metazoans are associated with increased lifespan, there is an enormous interest in the discovery of sirtuin activators. This interest has been reinforced by the fact that the increase of sirtuin activities in mammals accelerates fat mobilization, glucose consumption, and insulin sensibility, implicating this class of enzymes in obesity and diabetes, which are currently important and extended challenges for the health systems of developed countries. Because of the difficulty of ensuring that people in developed countries adhere to a low-calorie diet, there is growing interest in identifying molecules that mimic the effects of CR. A leading candidate is resveratrol (RV), a polyphenol found in red wine. RV is the first discovered molecule that can mimic CR by stimulating sirtuins  [33,35]. 

This review focuses on the current compounds that activate sirtuin activities, which support the therapeutic use of sirtuin-based therapies for the treatment of human diseases. 

## **2. Resveratrol** 

RV (3,5,4(-trihydroxy- _trans_ -stilbene) is a polyphenol consisting of two phenol rings connected by a 2-carbon methylene bridge ( **Figure 1** ). Resveratrol exists in both isomeric _trans_ and _cis_ forms. Even though both forms occur naturally, most of the recorded health benefits are attributed to the _trans_ -isomer. RV increases cell survival by stimulating SIRT1dependent deacetylation of p53. In yeast, resveratrol mimics calorie restriction, increasing DNA stability and extending lifespan by 70%. Dose–response experiments showed that RV doubled the rate of deacetylation by human recombinant SIRT1 at about 11 μM, whereas at saturated concentration of 200 μM RV activated SIRT1 8-fold  [35]. Of the mammalian sirtuins, only SIRT1 is responsive to RV  [36]. 

One of the most abundant natural sources of RV is the grape _Vitis vinifera_ , which is used to make wines. The grape skin contains the highest concentration of RV  [37] and the RV content of wine is related to the length of time the grape skins are present during the fermentation process. The RV concentration in Greek red wine ranges between 0.325 and 1.569 mg/L (1.4 – 6.8 μM); in white wine it is usually < 0.050 mg/L (0.2 μM)  [38]. Generally, white wines contain 1 – 5% of the RV content present in most red wines. The

Recently, methods to produce RV in recombinant microbial systems have been provided as an alternative to extraction from plant or chemical synthesis. The grape skins are included in the must during red wine vinification; this is not the case for white wine vinification. RV is therefore found in higher quantities in red wine. Both epidemiological and experimental studies have revealed that drinking wine, particularly red wine, in moderation protects cardiovascular health; however, the experimental basis for such an action is not fully understood. Although not supported by bioavailability data (see below), it has been claimed that CR mimetic effects of RV could explain the so-called French paradox, i.e., the fact that wine-drinking French people have a low incidence of coronary heart disease despite a lowexercise, high-fat diet  [44]. Besides its effects on the cardiovascular system, the effects of RV are pleiotropic. It exhibits a remarkable inhibitory potential in various stages of tumor development, and has neuroprotective effects. Furthermore, RV induces a multitude of effects that depend on the cell 

type, cellular condition, and concentration and can have opposing activities (proliferative vs. growth arrest). Targets of RV include cycloxygenases and lipoxygenases, many kinases, adenylyl cyclase, DNA polymerase, ribonucleotide reductase, quinone reductase 2, and sirtuins. RV is chemopreventive in some cancers models, shows a cardioprotective effect, and presents a positive effect on glucose and lipid metabolism and on oxidative phosphorylation. The targets, effects, and benefits of RV have been excellently summarized recently  [45-47]. This review focuses on RV effects proposed to be mediated by sirtuins. 

## **2.1 Sirtuin activation by resveratrol** 

RV activates sirtuins by lowering the Michaelis constant of SIRT1 for both the acetylated substrate and NAD[+] [35]. Recently, it has been hypothesized that RV and its analogues might bind to the N-terminus of SIRT1 to induce a conformational change that lowers the Km for the substrate, promoting a more productive conformation that enhances deacetylation activity. This model is supported by studies showing that the mutations SIRT1-E230K and Sir2-D223K within the N-terminal sequences close to the catalytic cores of SIRT1 and Sir2 prevent activation by RV without affecting basal activity  [48]. Analysis of a series of amino-terminal truncations of the human SIRT1 enzyme identified the region contained within amino acids 183 – 225 as important for activating function, and possibly for defining the putative allosteric site. However, the fact that RV actually behaves as a direct activator of SIRT1 is questioned by the demonstration that activation of human SIRT1 by RV requires the use of peptides conjugated with a non-physiological fluorophore, but no activation is observed when the deacetylation assay is carried out with acetylated peptides lacking the fluorophore  [36,49]. Thus, special caution must be taken when trying to explain RV effects in cells and organisms on the basis of a direct activation of SIRT1. 

## **2.2 Cardioprotective effect** 

The _db/db_ diabetic obese mouse presents a cardiomyocyte contractile dysfunction through, at least in part, a SIRT1dependent pathway. Protein expression of SIRT1 was reduced in _db/db_ mouse hearts  [50]. Conversely, increased expression of SIRT3 protects myocytes from genotoxic and oxidative-stress-mediated cell death  [51]. Furthermore, RV has also been reported to have a protective effect on cigarette-smoke-induced vascular oxidative stress and inflammation, which is a clinically highly relevant model of accelerated vascular aging. The protective effects of RV were abolished by knockdown of SIRT1, whereas the overexpression of SIRT1 mimicked the effects of RV  [52]. 

Heart failure is an end-stage pathological condition of virtually all heart diseases. Activation of sirtuins is a target for designing new therapeutic strategies, and methods and compositions that include RV have been proposed to manage heart failure and to correct endothelial dysfunction

## **2.3 Neuroprotective effect** 

As the population worldwide is aging, age-associated neurodegenerative diseases are becoming a major medical, economic, and social challenge to modern societies. The common biochemical and cellular processes include protein misfolding, oligomerization and aggregation, proteolysis, mitochondrial dysfunction, abnormal energy metabolism, activation of stress, inflammation and pro-apoptotic responses, among others  [23]. CR can protect neurons against degeneration in animal models of AD, Parkinson’s disease (PD), HD, and stroke  [57]. SIRT1 is not only up-regulated in mouse models of AD and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), but also in primary cultured neurons under neurotoxic stress. Furthermore, RV promotes neuronal survival in cell-based models of AD and ALS, making SIRT1 a molecular link between aging and neurodegenerative diseases. Treatment with RV resulted in reduced neuronal loss and improved associative learning in p25 transgenic mice compared with controls  [58]. For this, the use of RV in the composition for prevention or treating of AD and another neurodegenerative diseases has been proposed  [59,60]. Moreover, overexpression of SIRT1 or pharmacological activation of SIRT1 by NAD[+] promotes α-secretase activity and attenuates the generation of αβ peptides in embryonic Tg2576 mouse neurons _in vitro_ [61]. A novel therapeutic strategy directed to increase the supply of NAD[+] by overexpressing a chimeric protein, which includes the NAD[+] biosynthetic enzyme Nmnat1, might be effective for treatment of diseases characterized by axonopathy and neurodegeneration  [62]. SIRT1 is the major effector of the increased NAD[+] supply that effectively prevents axonal self-destruction. Exogenous NAD[+] added 24 h before axotomy also significantly delayed axonal degeneration  [62]. However, it has been also reported that both exogenously applied NAD[+] and nicotinamide, a known SIRT1 inhibitor  [63], could protect axons from degeneration and no significant differences in the extent of Wallerian degeneration or the protective effects of NAD[+] were observed between neurons from wild-type and SIRT1 knockout mice  [64]. Moreover, compared with the NAD[+] concentrations (0.1 – 1 mM) used by Araki _et al_ .  [62], 

it was found that only higher concentrations (1 – 20 mM) of NAD[+] could result in significant protective effects, and that NAD[+] efficiently prevented axon degeneration both when added at the time of axon transaction and when added as a pretreatment 24 h before. Future studies should resolve these experimental discrepancies. 

Axonal damage and neuronal loss are also significant pathologic components in patients with optic neuritis and multiple sclerosis (MS). Two distinct SIRT1 activators, nicotinamide riboside and SRT501, attenuate retinal ganglion cell loss in a dose-dependent manner in animal models. SIRT1 activators could provide an important potential therapy to prevent neurologic disability in optic neuritis with MS  [65]. An invention that includes ophthalmically acceptable formulations of sirtuin modulators has been reported  [66]. 

## **2.4 Metabolic diseases** 

The number of overweight individuals worldwide has reached 2.1 billion  [67]. The ‘metabolic syndrome’ comprises a cluster of risk factors for cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus that are due to abdominal obesity and insulin resistance. Both congenital and environmental factors, such as exercise and eating habits, contribute to the disease. In addition to affecting the lifestyle of an individual, obesity can lead to a number of complications and diseases, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, gall bladder disease, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. The association of moderate wine consumption with lower incidence of the metabolic syndrome and atherosclerotic heart disease has been repeatedly documented in numerous epidemiological studies on diverse ethnic groups, the beneficial effect being associated to the presence of RV in red wine  [68] SIRT1 activated by RV binds to and represses genes controlled by the fat regulator PPAR-γ, including genes mediating fat storage, and triggers lipolysis and loss of fat in differentiated fat cells  [24]. SIRT1 also decreases PGC-1α acetylation, which results in an increase in its activity (inducing gluconeogenic genes and hepatic glucose output  [17] positively regulates insulin secretion in pancreatic β cells  [20] and elevates glucose uptake in muscle cells by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation  [69], providing a link between SIRT1 and the control of metabolic processes. Recently, it has been demonstrated that muscle expression of PGC-1α and PGC-β is reduced both in mouse model, genetic obese ( _Lep_[ob] _/Lep_[ob] ) and in acquired obesity by feeding with a high-fat diet  [70]. 

As CR reverses insulin resistance and decreases visceral fat  [28], activation of SIRT1 by RV has attracted considerable attention because of its potential to attenuate symptoms of the metabolic syndrome. In fact, in mice fed with a diet that induces obesity, the group treated with high-dose RV (200 or 400 mg/kg/day) tended to gain less weight and showed improved insulin sensitivity, increased aerobic capacity in muscle, and improved motor function compared with

Methods and compositions for treating or preventing metabolic disorders, such as obesity and diabetes, by modulating the activity or levels of sirtuin are being actively research. Sirtris Pharmaceuticals has developed the SIRT1 activator SRT501, a formulation of RV with improved bioavailability  [74]. SRT501 was found to be safe and well tolerated in two Phase Ib clinical studies in patients with type 2 diabetes. Patients receiving 2.5 g twice a day had a statistically significant lowering of both fasting blood glucose and glucose levels during the postprandial period  [75]. SRT501 is in a Phase IIa study in type 2 diabetes patients unresponsive to metformin therapy; the results are expected soon  [76]. SRT501 is also in a Phase Ib trial in the orphan indication MELAS syndrome (mitochondrial myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes) and has shown preclinical activity in an HD model  [12]. 

Another pharmaceutical company, Resveratrol Partners Llc., has developed a nutraceutical mixture called Longevinex[®] , which contains plant or synthetic small molecules, such as RV, that are capable of activating genes that function as a feedback system to enhance survival during times of stress, e.g., during drought or famine. Once activated, these longevity genes induce defensive changes at the cellular level, such as slowing metabolism and enhancing cellular respiration, to help the body adapts to an adverse environment  [77]. Finally, LifeGen Technologies (based in Madison, WI), in collaboration with Swiss-based DSM Nutritional Products, has recently released Resvida[™] , a high-purity form of RV for use in many types of dietary supplements and in food and 

beverage formulations to provide a daily dosage of 0.03 mg/kg body weight to about 10 mg/kg body weight of the subject, which might have significant health benefits for the heart and other organs  [78]. 

## **2.5 Bioavailability** 

A major criticism arises when the beneficial effects of RV are associated with sirtuin activation: RV needs a concentration ranging between 10 and 200 μM to activate SIRT1, but the RV concentration found in rat plasma generated peak values not higher than 6.6 μM 5 – 10 min after oral administration of 50 mg/kg, and 50 nM after 2 h oral administration of _trans-_ RV in a solution of β-cyclodextrin  [79,80]. The halflife of RV in plasma, after intravenous administration of 20 mg _trans-_ RV/kg body weight, is very short (e.g., 14.4 min in rabbits) and the highest concentration (2.6 ± 1 μM) of RV in plasma after receiving 20 mg _trans-_ RV/kg orally was reached within the first 5 min  [81]. With time, plasma concentration diminished until a secondary peak appeared. This secondary peak was due to the recirculation of RV after release from bile  [79]. Moreover, in rats fed for 8 weeks with a diet containing 50 mg RV/kg body weight, neither RV nor RV conjugates could be detected in plasma after 24 h animal fasting  [82]. After plasma, the highest concentrations detected in rat tissues were found in liver and kidney and reached less than 1% of administered dose. Only trace amounts (≤ 0.1%) were detected in the brain, lungs, heart, and spleen  [83]. SRT501, the reformulated version of RV developed by Sirtris, has improved its bioavailability by 11%  [84]. 

RV is both a pharmacologically safe and well tolerated drug even at doses up to 5 g/day for seven consecutive days  [76]. In healthy human volunteers, plasma RV concentrations increased from 0.71 ± 0.02 – 1.72 ± 0.1 μM after 15 days of controlled 300 mL/day red wine intake. Fresh grape skin contains 50 – 100 mg/g RV, whereas the concentration of RV in wine ranges from 0.1 – 14 mg/L for red wine  [85]. In humans, oral RV shows high absorption (70%) but very low bioavailability. The peak plasma levels of RV and metabolites were of 491 ± 90 ng/ml (about 2 μM) after an oral dose of 25 mg, with a plasma half-life of 9.2 ± 0.6 h. However, only trace amounts of unchanged resveratrol (< 5 ng/ml) could be detected in plasma  [86]. Another important factor for the low oral bioavailability of RV is its extensive biotransformation during the transepithelial transport of unconjugated RV to glucuronide and sulfate derivates. These conjugates seem to reach much higher plasma levels than the parent agent  [87]. Conversion of RV into hydrophilic conjugates might facilitate its entry into the bloodstream and its diffusion throughout the body. Furthermore, the clearance of RV after administration was markedly higher than that of RV glucuronide, resulting in a systemic exposure of approximately 46-fold lower than that of RV glucuronide  [88]. ## **3. Other polyphenols structurally related to resveratrol** 

RV is not the only plant polyphenol reported to activate sirtuin, but the same effect is found for other compounds that share structural similarity. These include the stilbene piceatannol ( **Figure 1** ), the chalcones butein and isoliquiritigenin ( **Figure 2** ), and the flavones fisetin and quercetin ( **Figure 3** ). These compounds activated human SIRT1 deacetylase activity 4 – 8 fold (under conditions where a 13- fold activation with RV was observed) and extended lifespan in _Saccharomyces cerevisiae_ [35]. These compounds also activated sirtuin deacetylase activity in _Drosophila melanogaster_ S2 cells, and a 23% extension of the lifespan was observed when the _Drosophila_ flies were fed on an abundant diet containing 100 μM fisetin (a 29% of extension was observed with RV)  [33]. 

## **4. Activator compounds unrelated to resveratrol** 

Although RV is a pharmacologically safe drug (see above), it is also commonly referred to as a ‘dirty’ molecule in the pharmaceutical industry because it has the capacity to interact with multiple molecular targets and it is likely that its action results from various complementary effects on different biochemical pathways. Moreover, RV has poor bioavailability and is quickly metabolized, producing metabolites that might be pharmacologically active. Also, the known biological functions of sirtuins are growing, and modulation of sirtuin activity has therapeutic value for a variety of diseases. For that, the development of new molecules that target sirtuin at low concentration, having more specificity, potent effect and better bioavailability is a promising field of the medicinal chemistry.

The mammalian sirtuin family comprises seven enzymes with the potential to protect against different age-related diseases, such as several neurodegenerative diseases, inflammation, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes type 2, and metabolic syndrome. The best studied member of the sirtuin family is SIRT1. 

A common result of age-related diseases plus diabetes plus metabolic syndrome is the malfunction of mitochondria and increased generation of reactive oxygen species. Deacetylation of the master regulator of mitochondrial PGC-1α by SIRT1 leads to an increase in mitochondrial size and number, thus providing new and promising therapies for these diseases. SIRT1 activity also represses PPAR-γ, promoting fat mobilization, increases insulin sensitivity, and positively regulates insulin secretion in pancreatic β cells, linking SIRT1 to the control of metabolic processes such as metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance. To date, RV is the best-studied natural compound that is able to activate SIRT1, mimicking the positive effect of dietary CR. Thus, RV might help to prevent the development of obesity or might be suited to treating obesity. RV was as effective as CR in opposing the majority of age-related transcriptional alterations in the aging heart, helping to prevent the decline in heart function, which is a natural result of aging. Furthermore, treatment with RV resulted in reduced neuronal loss and improved associative learning in a mouse model. As a result, the use of RV for the prevention or treatment of these diseases has been proposed. As RV has low bioavailability and interacts with multiple molecular targets, the development of new molecules that target sirtuin at low concentrations is a promising field of the medicinal chemistry because of their potential to address a broad range of diseases.

Also, endogenous levels of nicotinamide might limit SIRT1 activity, suggesting that the concentration of nicotinamide or change in the NAD[+] /NADH in response to physiological changes or altered under various pathological conditions could affect SIRT1 function. One of the potential side-effects associated with SIRT1 activation is the overconsumption and depletion of NAD[+] , which could provoke cell death. Finally, little is known about the intracellular signaling that regulates the expression of the nuclear protein that directly enhances SIRT1-mediated deacetylation. Identifying the input of all these signals will extend our knowledge of the context of adaptive cellular responses to the nutrient availability that affects the NAD[+] /NADH homeostatic mechanisms often disturbed in human disease and providing additional routes for the study and modulation of SIRT1. 

Activators, like sirtuin inhibitors, have been developed recently and studies into their bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, blood clearance, and toxicity remain to be performed. The efficacy of sirtuin activators in neurodegenerative diseases also requires these preliminary studies in animal models before testing in human volunteers. 

RV is a safe and well-tolerated natural compound in a long-term diet supplement. It can activate SIRT1 in a sustainable way, at much lower doses than originally suspected. However, the fact that activation of human SIRT1 by RV _in vitro_ requires the use of acetylated peptides conjugated to a nonphysiological fluorophore necessitates re-evaluation of the putative role of RV as a direct activator of SIRT1 to explain its beneficial effects _in vivo_ . 

The question of whether RV itself can accumulate to bioactive levels in target organs, or whether this effect is produced by its metabolites, remains to be addressed. Studies are warranted to determine if these metabolites could activate SIRT1, and, if so, to what extent. In the same way, the chemical stability and solubility of RV analogues could be more important than potency in long-term treatments. 

Other apparent liabilities are associated with resveratrol, e.g., the bioavailability and solubility, sensitivity to light, oxidation, and extensive metabolization by enterocytes. In particular, bioavailability data do not support a significant effect of dietary resveratrol on the direct activation of sirtuin _in vivo_ . For this, the discovery of synthetic SIRT1 agonists is an important step for the development of more potent, selective, bioavailable, and brain-permeable SIRT1 activators. 

Obesity has reached epidemic proportions globally and is a major contributor to the global burden of chronic disease and disability. The increased consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods with high levels of sugar and saturated fats, combined with a reduction in physical activity, have led to the current obesity rates. Modifying the dietary habits of populations will be extremely difficult. Thus, the development of a dietary supplement that mimics the transcriptional effects of CR could attenuate some of the pathological consequences of a high-calorie diet, thus improving the overall healthy state of the population. 

A major concern in the development of novel therapies is their safety for human subjects. There are conflicting data _in vitro_ as to whether SIRT1 will be found to act as an oncogene or as a tumor suppressor, and thus whether altering its function despite any health benefits might result in an increased cancer risk as a promoter of cancer cell survival. Furthermore, SIRT1 regulation by caloric restriction is tissue specific, increasing SIRT1 levels in white adipose tissue, muscle, and pancreas but decreasing levels in the liver. This question is important, because the benefits of pharmacological treatment designed to mimic CR systemically could be more complex than currently imagined. Thus, a full understanding of the tissue-specific roles and regulation of SIRT1 in whole organisms is required and will be an essential tool to establishing that the feasibility of developing sirtuin-based therapy for humans will be targeted to specific organs. Any systemic SIRT1 activator would increase metabolism in muscle and adipocytes, thus reducing fat accumulation, but it might have the side effect of inducing the synthesis of fat and cholesterol in the liver or – even worse – increasing the cancer risk in genetically disposed individuals.
